Capable language models can outgrow the benefits of collaboration

Yubin Kim · Ken Gu · Chanwoo Park · Chunjong Park · Samuel Schmidgall · A. Ali Heydari · Yao Yan · Zhihan Zhang · Yuchen Zhuang · Yun Liu · Mark Malhotra · Paul Pu Liang · Hae Won Park · Yuzhe Yang · Xuhai Xu · Yilun Du · Shwetak Patel · Tim Althoff · Daniel McDuff · Xin Liu Published 2026-07-24 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01268-y Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Adding agents is not a reliable way to make an LLM system better. Holding prompts, tools and compute constant and varying only coordination structure and model capability across 260 configurations (6 agentic benchmarks × 5 architectures × 3 LLM families), the single most robust predictor of whether multi-agent coordination helps or hurts is the single-agent baseline itself. Above an empirical capability-saturation threshold of roughly 45% single-agent accuracy, extra agents are unlikely to help — the rule matched the sign of the multi-agent effect in 94% of 16 SWE-bench Verified / Terminal-Bench validation configurations. The effect range is enormous (+80.8% on Finance Agent to −70.0% on PlanCraft) with an overall mean of 0.0%; the authors frame the threshold as a practical within-domain selection rule, explicitly not a universal scaling law.

★

Why It Matters

  • It kills a slogan with a controlled experiment. "More agents is all you need" and "collaborative scaling laws" are tested under matched per-system compute ceilings and identical tool access, so the only thing varying is coordination topology and model capability — the confound that plagued earlier multi-agent claims.
  • It gives a usable decision rule, not just a critique. If your single-agent baseline is already above ~0.45, spend the budget on a better single agent; below it, and if the task decomposes into parallel information streams, coordinate. That is directly actionable for anyone building agentic pipelines.
  • Decomposability, not difficulty, is the real moderator. Finance Agent (D = 0.407) gains +80.8% while PlanCraft (D = 0.419) loses −70.0% at nearly identical domain-complexity scores — the discriminator is sequential interdependence, not how hard the task is.
  • It quantifies the coordination tax. Reasoning turns follow a super-linear power law T = 2.72 (n+0.5)^1.724 (R² = 0.974); hybrid systems burn 6.2× the turns of a single agent (44.3 vs 7.2), and trace-level error amplification climbs from 1.0 (single) to 17.2 (independent multi-agent).
  • Honest about its own limits. Leave-one-dataset-out R² = −2.09 and cluster-robust inference demote several headline effects to "descriptive directional patterns" — a rare and welcome discipline in agent benchmarking.

Core Methods

  • Controlled factorial design: hold task prompts, tool sets and per-system compute ceilings constant; vary only coordination topology (SAS / independent / centralized / decentralized / hybrid) and model capability. Iteration counts serve as safety ceilings, not the fairness control — the fairness control is the matched reasoning-token budget.
  • Agentic-task screening: benchmarks are admitted only if they require (1) sustained multi-step environment interaction, (2) iterative information gathering under partial observability and (3) adaptive strategy refinement — an extension of the Agentic Benchmark Checklist, explicitly excluding static single-shot benchmarks like HumanEval.
  • Trace-derived coordination metrics: from sanitized execution traces, compute efficiency Ec (success normalized by relative reasoning-turn count), overhead O%, trace-level error amplification Ae, interagent message density c, and work redundancy R (mean cosine similarity of agent output embeddings).
  • Mechanistically-specified regression: a 20-parameter standardized linear model with a quadratic capability term, log transforms on right-skewed variables (O%, T, na, Ae), and nine pre-registered interactions — interactions without mechanistic justification (e.g. R × c) are deliberately excluded to avoid overfitting.
  • Two capability metrics: an externally standardized intelligence index I (reported for comparability) and a task-grounded agentic capability index (ACI = mean single-agent performance across all six benchmarks; r = 0.45 with I). ACI fits better (R²CV = 0.413 vs 0.373) and is recommended as primary.
  • Threshold derivation: the single-/multi-agent decision boundary is read off the fitted coefficients as P*SA = −β₄/β₁₇ ≈ 0.040/0.236 = 0.170 in standardized units, ≈ 0.45 raw accuracy after denormalization — then validated out-of-sample by sign-match rate (94% of 16 configurations, binomial P < 0.001).
  • Robustness discipline: five-fold CV with configuration-level holdout; cluster-robust standard errors over G = 6 benchmark clusters to correct pseudoreplication (which demotes the efficiency×tools and intelligence effects to descriptive patterns); VIF diagnostics (I centred, VIF 200 → 1.1); bootstrap CIs per cell; leave-one-dataset-out CV (R² = −2.09) explicitly bounding external validity.
  • Failure taxonomy: errors classified per the Multi-Agent System Failure Taxonomy (MAST) into specification, interagent misalignment and verification failures, then quantified by frequency and propagation depth across architectures.
  • Scaling sweep: agent-count experiment (na ∈ {1,2,3,5,7,9}) on BrowseComp-Plus for Gemini-2.0 Flash and Gemini-2.5 Pro, fitting turn growth to a power law and locating model-dependent coordination optima.

① Add a spatial-reasoning benchmark to the six — does the 45% rule survive geography?

Extending this paperThe paper's own leave-one-dataset-out result (R² = −2.09) is a confession: the fitted model does not transfer to unseen domains, only across configurations within the six benchmarks it saw. The sharpest extension is therefore an out-of-domain stress test on a task family with structurally different interdependence — spatial/geospatial reasoning, where sub-answers are constrained by topology, adjacency and routing rather than by document retrieval or file edits. Concretely: instrument a GeoAI agentic benchmark (network routing, catchment delineation, spatial-join chains, choropleth interpretation) with the paper's exact protocol — matched per-system tokens, identical tools, the same five topologies — measure single-agent baseline PSA, and ask whether the ~0.45 saturation threshold and the domain-complexity score D reproduce. Spatial tasks are an unusually good adversary: they combine PlanCraft-like sequential dependency (you cannot compute accessibility before you have a network) with Finance-Agent-like parallelizability (independent regions), so they should split the paper's own decomposability axis.

Added value to my researchThis gives me a principled way to decide the architecture for a GeoAI analysis agent before spending budget on it. My spatial-accessibility pipelines (2SFCA/R2SFCA catchment construction, MGWR model specification, multi-source fusion of POI + mobile-phone + remote-sensing layers) are exactly the "does this decompose?" question the paper formalizes. If a single strong model already clears 45% on my spatial-reasoning task suite, the paper says: stop building the agent swarm, buy a better base model and invest in tools instead — a concrete, defensible resourcing decision for my lab.

② Replace the scalar domain-complexity score D with a measured dependency graph

Extending this paperThe paper's most interesting failure is that D cannot separate Finance Agent (D = 0.407, +80.8%) from PlanCraft (D = 0.419, −70.0%). The authors correctly diagnose the cause — sequential interdependence, not complexity — but then keep using a scalar. The extension writes itself: recover, from the sanitized execution traces the authors released, an explicit subtask dependency DAG per instance, and replace D with graph-theoretic quantities that actually encode parallelism — critical-path length over total work (the classic parallel-speedup ratio), DAG width, and the fraction of edges that are hard-ordered. Then refit Equation (1) with those in place of D and log(1+na), and test whether the optimal team size is simply the DAG width. That would convert the paper's qualitative "decomposability matters" into a computable pre-flight statistic derivable before any agent is run.

Added value to my researchDependency-graph reasoning is native to my methods stack — network community detection, physics-informed graph learning, job-housing and urban-agglomeration networks all reduce to "which parts of this system can be solved in parallel and which are hard-ordered". Porting the critical-path/width formalism from agent workflows to spatial analysis workflows gives me a graph-based scheduler for reproducible geospatial pipelines, and doubles as an explainability device: the DAG shows exactly which analytical step created the bottleneck.

③ Turn error amplification into a spatial error-propagation model for agentic health analytics

Extending this paperThe paper measures trace-level error amplification climbing 1.0 → 4.4 (centralized) → 5.1 (hybrid) → 7.8 (decentralized) → 17.2 (independent), but treats it as a scalar per architecture and finds it non-significant once other metrics are controlled — precisely because it is only informative when scaled by baseline difficulty. Extend it by modelling amplification as a propagation process on the communication topology: fit a branching/cascade model (an epidemic-style formulation, with each message a potential transmission event) where the reproduction number is estimated per architecture and per benchmark. That predicts not just how much error grows but where in the topology it is absorbed, and it explains the paper's own puzzle — why "independent" (no communication at all) has the worst amplification: there is no absorbing node.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct methodological loan from my own toolkit — SIR-type propagation and Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis — applied to LLM systems rather than to disease spread. More usefully, it runs in reverse: it tells me how to safely stage an agentic pipeline for health-geography analysis, where an early error in geocoding or catchment definition cascades into every downstream accessibility estimate. An amplification-aware topology (mandatory verification nodes at high-fan-out points) is the design principle I would want before letting agents touch primary-care or mental-health access work.

④ From a fixed topology to a capability-aware adaptive controller

Extending this paperEvery one of the 260 cells commits to a topology up front and holds it for the whole episode. But the paper's own evidence says the right topology depends on quantities that change during execution: realized turn overhead, message density, redundancy, and how far the current trajectory is from success. The natural extension is a run-time controller that re-derives the single/multi decision at each checkpoint using the paper's own coordination metrics as state — start single-agent, fork only when the trajectory stalls below the estimated saturation threshold, and collapse back when redundancy rises. Because the paper releases per-instance correctness and sanitized traces, this can be trained offline on their data before any new inference spend. The evaluation target is sharp: beat the paper's own 87% held-out architecture-selection accuracy with a policy that is allowed to switch mid-episode.

Added value to my researchAn adaptive-allocation controller is structurally the same object as the efficiency-versus-equity optimizer in my EV charging-station work and the variable-catchment logic in R2SFCA: in all three, a fixed global parameter (team size, station budget, catchment radius) is replaced by a state-conditioned policy. Reusing the formulation across agent orchestration and spatial resource allocation is a genuine methodological transfer, and it makes my accessibility models "adaptive" in the same defensible sense.

⑤ Stress the threshold where it actually gets deployed: equity-sensitive, high-stakes tasks

Extending this paperThe paper's outcome variable is a single scalar — task success. That is the right choice for a clean experiment and the wrong one for deployment, because two architectures with identical mean accuracy can distribute their failures very differently across sub-populations. The extension: re-run the 260-cell design with a disaggregated outcome — success stratified by instance subgroup — and ask whether the ~0.45 saturation threshold shifts when the objective is worst-group rather than mean performance. The paper's own mechanism predicts it should: redundancy is described as protective when there is "room for improvement" (PSA < 0.45), and hard subgroups are by construction the low-baseline regime. If so, the practical rule becomes conditional — a system that has outgrown collaboration on average may still need it on its hardest slice. Meanwhile the paper's cost side is under-reported (tracked on only 180 of 260 configurations), so pairing the disaggregated outcome with full cost accounting yields a proper cost-per-worst-group-success frontier.

Added value to my researchThis is my central research instinct — the mean hides the inequity — imported into agent evaluation. It maps cleanly onto telehealth and digital-divide work, where an assistant that performs well on average can fail systematically for rural, low-broadband or low-digital-literacy populations. Combining a subgroup-stratified evaluation with explainable-ML attribution over instance features is exactly the FAIR/open-science evaluation protocol I would want to publish alongside any GeoAI agent, and it makes the equity claim measurable rather than rhetorical.
