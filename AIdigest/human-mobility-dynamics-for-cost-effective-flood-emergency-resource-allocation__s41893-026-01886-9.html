Human mobility dynamics for cost-effective flood emergency resource allocation

Junqing Tang · Wei Lyu · Pengjun Zhao · Fengjue Huang · Jiaying Li · Chao Fan · Qiuchen Lu · Jing Wang · Lingzhi Ye · Zengjun Guo Published 2026-07-14 DOI 10.1038/s41893-026-01886-9 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Emergency shelters are misallocated because planning ignores that people move during floods. Using an agent-based model of flood-induced human dynamics across China's 21 large cities, the authors find that 43% of these cities face a substantial demand–supply mismatch (>2,000 km² per city) under a 100-year flood, leaving 16.76 million residents exposed to preventable risk. Under SSP5-8.5, the mismatch persists to 2050 even as China's population declines, while flooded area is projected to expand fivefold. Retrofitting just 30% of high-deficit zones into dual-use shelters delivers benefit-to-cost ratios above 28 — US$0.20–3.05 billion saved per city for a per-capita investment of only US$1.39.

★

Why It Matters

  • It changes what "demand" means in shelter planning. Conventional shelter siting matches capacity to residential population at night. This paper shows the relevant demand is a moving, time-varying field — people are commuting, sheltering-in-place, or fleeing — so a plan that looks adequate on a census map fails in the hours that count.
  • It quantifies an invisible deficit at national scale. "43% of cities, >2,000 km² mismatch, 16.76 million people" converts a diffuse governance worry into an auditable number that a ministry can budget against.
  • Declining population does not solve the problem. The SSP5-8.5 projection separates the two drivers: demographic shrinkage reduces demand slowly, but climate-driven flood extent grows fivefold — so the gap persists to 2050. That is a genuinely counter-intuitive policy finding.
  • It gives a cheap, buildable answer. Retrofitting existing dual-use public service facilities (schools, gyms, community centres) instead of building new shelters yields a BCR > 28 at US$1.39 per capita — an intervention small enough to survive an actual municipal budget cycle.
  • The benefit accounting is broad, not just lives. Mortality reduction, societal co-benefits, carbon savings and economic continuity are all monetized, connecting disaster preparedness to the climate-mitigation and sustainability ledgers rather than treating it as a pure cost centre.

Core Methods

  • Agent-based modelling of flood-induced human dynamics (the core engine). Agents instantiated from 1 km gridded population choose among stay / shelter-in-place / evacuate / relocate as inundation evolves, producing a time-varying demand field rather than a static residential count (Fig. 1).
  • Behavioural rules grounded in social science. The decision layer draws on the theory of planned behaviour, social influence and conformity, and herd-behaviour models — evacuation is treated as a socially contagious decision, not an individually optimal one.
  • Flood hazard simulation. A 100-year flood scenario is generated from precipitation, NASADEM terrain, land cover and building footprints, giving the inundation surface that drives agent perception and blocks routes.
  • Two-event empirical validation. Model output is benchmarked against observed mobile-phone mobility for the Zhengzhou 2021 flood and the Shenzhen 2018 Typhoon Mangkhut flood (Fig. 2) — one inland pluvial disaster and one coastal typhoon event.
  • Demand–supply mismatch mapping. Simulated dynamic demand is differenced against the shelter + dual-use facility supply inventory to delineate mismatch areas (km²) and the population exposed within them, per city (Fig. 3).
  • Knee-point detection for thresholds. The Kneedle algorithm is used to locate the inflection in the deficit/benefit curves, which is how "high-deficit zones" and the 30% retrofit share become data-derived rather than arbitrary.
  • Future-scenario projection. The whole pipeline is re-run for 2050 under SSP5-8.5 with projected population, precipitation and land cover, separating the demographic and climatic contributions to the residual mismatch (Fig. 4).
  • Multi-component cost–benefit analysis. Retrofitting scenarios are priced against four benefit streams — avoided mortality, societal co-benefits, carbon savings, economic continuity — using insurance, relief-fund, carbon-price and transport-emission references to produce per-city BCRs and savings (Fig. 5).
  • Reproducibility. Python implementation released as PKU-FIM; Supplementary Information documents 9 algorithms and 3 method appendices.

① Learn the ABM's behavioural rules from the mobility data instead of hand-specifying them

Extending this paperThe paper's decision layer is theory-specified: planned-behaviour, conformity and herd-behaviour rules with parameters tuned until simulated flows match Zhengzhou and Shenzhen. That is defensible for two events but it burns the billion-record datasets as a validation target rather than a training signal, and every new city needs re-tuning. A direct extension: replace the rule block with a learned flow generator conditioned on inundation state — train an OD-generation network on the observed hourly 1 km trips with flood depth, blocked-link status and time-since-onset as covariates, then let the ABM sample decisions from it. The ABM keeps its causal, scenario-manipulable skeleton; the behavioural kernel becomes data-driven and city-transferable. A second payoff: the learned kernel gives calibrated uncertainty on demand, so the 2,000 km² mismatch and the 16.76 M exposure figure could carry confidence intervals rather than point estimates.

Added value to my researchThis is precisely the hybrid I want for accessibility work: a learned distance-decay / behavioural kernel embedded inside a mechanistic ABM. If evacuation choice can be learned from phone traces and then re-simulated under counterfactual hazards, the same architecture learns care-seeking choice from GPS/mobile traces and re-simulates it under counterfactual clinic closures — turning my 2SFCA supply–demand ratios from a static geometric index into a behaviourally-generated, scenario-testable one.

② Turn "mismatch area (km²)" into a competition-aware dynamic R2SFCA of shelter access

Extending this paperThe paper's headline metric is a binary areal deficit: how many km² have demand exceeding nearby supply. That is legible to policymakers but it discards three things the ABM already knows — capacity competition (many grid cells racing for the same gym), travel-time decay under a flooded network, and the hour at which the deficit bites. Extending it: recompute the same 21-city analysis as a time-sliced, capacity-constrained R2SFCA, where each shelter's supply is depleted by arriving agents and each cell's accessibility decays along the flood-perturbed travel time rather than Euclidean proximity. The mismatch then stops being an area and becomes a per-capita access ratio with a temporal profile — revealing whether a city's deficit is a quantity problem (never enough beds) or a timing/routing problem (enough beds, unreachable in the first six hours). The retrofit-30% rule would likely re-rank substantially under this metric, which is itself a publishable result.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct, high-value transplant of my R2SFCA / service-area delineation machinery into disaster logistics, and it stress-tests the method in the regime it has never faced: a network whose impedance is changing during the measurement. Whatever I learn about time-sliced catchment stability here feeds straight back into telehealth and primary-care accessibility, where supply is also depletable and demand also arrives in surges.

③ Test whether the framework survives outside China — a transferability audit

Extending this paperEverything here is China-specific by construction: CBRA rooftops, Amap POIs, the 2023 Ministry of Emergency Management shelter grading standard, PICC insurance pricing, and a shelter culture where dual-use public facilities are a live policy instrument (the paper cites a 20.4-billion-yuan policy-bank programme). The claim "43% of cities are mismatched" is therefore not portable, and neither is BCR > 28. The honest extension is a transferability audit: re-run the pipeline on a set of cities with open substitutes (OSM POIs, GHSL built-up, global DEM, WorldPop) and explicitly decompose how much of the mismatch is driven by universal geography — where floods go, where people are — versus national institutional design — how many dual-use facilities the state has already built. Pairing this with evidence that urban growth has been fastest inside flood zones globally would show whether China is unusually exposed or merely unusually well-measured.

Added value to my researchThis is the generalization question I keep hitting in cross-national accessibility work: how much of a spatial result is geometry and how much is policy regime. Building an explicit geography-vs-institution decomposition — and the open-data-only fallback stack that makes it runnable anywhere — is a reusable methodological asset for my urban-agglomeration and regional-collaboration line, not just for floods.

④ Open the benefit–cost black box: who is in the 16.76 million, and who does the 30% retrofit actually protect?

Extending this paperThe paper reports aggregate exposure (16.76 M residents) and aggregate efficiency (BCR > 28), but a retrofit rule optimized on total benefit will preferentially upgrade dense, wealthy, facility-rich districts — the ones where a single gym serves the most people. Nothing in the current framing detects that. Extending it: attach a socioeconomic and demographic layer to every mismatch cell (income, age, disability, tenure, migrant status) and re-solve the retrofit allocation under an efficiency–equity Pareto frontier, then use explainable ML (SHAP over a gradient-boosted surrogate of the BCR surface, plus MGWR for spatially varying effects) to name which built-environment and social features actually drive the benefit. The publishable claim would be the equity price of efficiency: how much aggregate BCR must be surrendered to move the protected population toward those least able to self-evacuate.

Added value to my researchThis unites two of my active lines in one artefact — explainable ML for spatial prediction and efficiency-versus-equity facility optimization (the same trade-off I formalize for EV charging stations). The surrogate-plus-SHAP-plus-MGWR stack is method-portable: swap shelters for mental-health clinics and the identical machinery answers "which neighbourhoods does a capacity expansion actually reach, and who gets skipped?"

⑤ Extend from the evacuation hour to the recovery month, and from shelters to coupled lifelines

Extending this paperThe model's horizon is the acute phase — flood arrives, people move, shelters fill — and its resource universe is one facility class. But the health and economic damage of Zhengzhou-scale events accrues over weeks, through interdependent lifelines: hospitals and dialysis centres that must stay reachable, power and EV-charging infrastructure that is itself flood-exposed, transit that determines whether people can return to work. Extending the paper: (a) run the ABM past evacuation into a return-and-recovery phase, scoring how long mobility takes to normalize per neighbourhood — a recovery-speed inequality metric; and (b) co-optimize the retrofit budget across a portfolio of facility types (shelters + clinics + charging + peer-to-peer resource-sharing nodes) instead of shelters alone, since the marginal life-year bought by a reachable clinic on day 5 may exceed that of an extra shelter bed on hour 3. That converts a shelter-siting paper into a multi-lifeline adaptation-portfolio paper.

Added value to my researchRecovery-speed-by-neighbourhood is a spatiotemporal signal tailor-made for my KDE and network-community toolkit, and the portfolio formulation is the multi-infrastructure generalization of my EV efficiency–equity optimization — with the added twist that the charging network is simultaneously a resource and a flood-vulnerable asset. It also gives me a concrete public-health hook: post-disaster access to primary and mental-health care is exactly the "coupling of space–human systems for health policy" question I work on, here with a hazard forcing term attached.
