Demand-side factors as dominant drivers of resource efficiency in the concrete aggregates cycle

Zhi Cao · Jia Liu · Xiaoke Su · Jingyang Song · Xiaoyang Zhong · Takuma Watari · Paul Behrens · Tong Zhang Published 2026-06-22 DOI 10.1038/s41893-026-01860-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across 184 economies from 1990 to 2018, the resource efficiency of the concrete–aggregates cycle is governed by demand-side, not production-side, drivers. Technological fixes inside the plant — clinker substitution, leaner concrete formulations — deliver only marginal reductions, whereas how much stock a society builds, how long it keeps it, and how intensively it uses it dominate the trend. Developing economies show large inflow–outflow imbalances; industrialized economies show stock saturation with declining turnover. The effective levers are therefore longer building lifespans, lower material stock intensity through design and urban planning, reuse, and structural intensification — and efficiency should be benchmarked against human development (HDI), not GDP.

★

Why It Matters

  • Reframes the biggest material flow on Earth. Concrete and its aggregates are the largest anthropogenic mass flow by tonnage. Showing that its efficiency is demand-determined shifts the mitigation burden from cement chemists to planners, designers and building-stock managers.
  • Puts a number on the limits of supply-side technology. Clinker substitution and formulation efficiency are the industry's headline decarbonization levers; the paper quantifies them as marginal against demand growth — a direct challenge to roadmap optimism.
  • Swaps GDP for welfare in the denominator. Defining resource-use intensity as resource use per unit of HDI reframes "decoupling" as a question of how much material a society needs to deliver a decent life — a beyond-GDP metric that travels straight into SDG 8/12 reporting.
  • Separates two different worlds of policy. Developing economies (inflow-dominated, building up stock) and industrialized economies (saturated, slow turnover) require opposite interventions; a single global "circularity" target would misfire in both.
  • Names the durable levers. Lifespan extension, stock-intensity reduction via design and urban planning, reuse and structural intensification are all spatial and behavioural — exactly the levers urban analytics can measure and optimize.

Core Methods

  • Integrated flows–stocks–services framework. The paper's contribution is a single system definition (Fig. 1) that closes the anthropogenic concrete–aggregates cycle: cement production and trade → concrete formulation → aggregates demand → in-use stock → demolition outflow, for 184 economies over 1931–2018.
  • Dynamic material flow analysis (MFA) with a stock–flow model. Historical inflows are propagated through lifetime distributions to reconstruct in-use stock and outflow, which is what makes the inflow–outflow imbalance (developing) versus saturation and low turnover (industrialized) contrast measurable rather than rhetorical.
  • Resource-use intensity (RUI) = resource use per unit HDI. A deliberate departure from the material-footprint-per-GDP convention; the paper's cited "beyond-GDP" literature (Bithas & Kalimeris; Kalimeris et al.) motivates using an alternative welfare indicator as the denominator.
  • Index decomposition analysis into six drivers. The attribution (Figs. 3–4) follows the LMDI family cited in the references (Ang et al. 1998; Guan et al. 2018; Python-LMDI), splitting RUI change into production-side factors (e.g. clinker substitution, concrete formulation efficiency) and demand-side factors (e.g. material stock intensity, stock turnover/lifespan, service provision). Exact factor definitions are in the paywalled Methods.
  • Cross-country typology. Economies are contrasted along their development trajectory so that the same decomposition yields opposite policy prescriptions for stock-building versus stock-saturated societies.
  • Lever assessment. The identified levers — lifespan extension, stock-intensity reduction via design and urban planning, reuse, and structural intensification — are evaluated as demand-side interventions within the same accounting frame, making them directly comparable to clinker substitution.

① Downscale the 184-economy cycle into a spatially explicit stock–flow field

Extending this paperThe paper's entire result rests on national aggregates: one cement inflow, one stock, one RUI per economy. But every lever it recommends — design, urban planning, structural intensification — acts at the scale of a block, not a country. The direct extension is to downscale the same cycle to a gridded stock field by fusing the national mass balance with remote-sensing-derived built-up volume and building-mass maps, imposing the national total as a hard mass-conservation constraint so the downscaled field remains consistent with the paper's own accounting. That converts Fig. 2's single global throughput number into a map of where the aggregates are locked up and where demolition outflow will surface over the next two decades.

Added value to my researchThis is a textbook multi-source data fusion + physics-informed spatial learning problem in my line: a downscaling network whose loss carries a conservation penalty (grid cells must sum to the national stock) and whose inputs are exactly my working data — 3D built-up volume, building footprints, nighttime lights, POIs. The resulting "urban material stock raster" is reusable infrastructure for my urban-analytics work, and the conservation-constrained downscaler is a method transferable to any national-to-grid disaggregation problem (population, energy, emissions).

② Replace the exogenous building lifetime with a learned, explainable demolition-hazard model

Extending this paperThe paper's most consequential lever is lifespan extension, yet in a dynamic MFA the lifetime is an assumed distribution (typically Weibull/normal with literature-borrowed parameters) applied uniformly within an economy. The entire outflow curve — and therefore "stock turnover" as a decomposition driver — inherits that assumption. Extending the paper: estimate demolition hazard empirically as a survival model over observed building-level cohorts (construction year, use type, height, tenure, plot value, neighbourhood renewal pressure), and re-run the decomposition with data-driven, spatially varying lifetimes. If demolition risk is concentrated in a small, predictable subset of the stock, "lifespan extension" stops being a diffuse slogan and becomes a targetable intervention list.

Added value to my researchDirectly my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction agenda: a gradient-boosted or deep survival model with SHAP/ALE attribution tells us which spatial and socioeconomic covariates drive premature demolition, and MGWR-style analysis reveals whether those effects are non-stationary across a country. It also transfers cleanly to my health-geography work, where "facility survival/closure hazard" is the same statistical object — predicting which clinics or pharmacies will close is formally identical to predicting which buildings will be torn down.

③ Swap HDI for a service-access denominator: resource use per unit of realized accessibility

Extending this paperThe paper's boldest move is putting HDI in the denominator instead of GDP — but HDI is a national scalar built from life expectancy, schooling and income, and it is blind to whether the concrete actually delivered services to people. Two economies with identical HDI can have radically different spatial arrangements of the same tonnage: one where housing, schools and clinics sit within reach, one where they do not. The extension is to build a service-delivery denominator — tonnes of aggregates per unit of population-weighted accessibility to essential services — so that resource efficiency rewards not just building, but building in the right place. This turns "reducing material stock intensity through design and urban planning", which the paper names but cannot measure, into a quantity inside the metric itself.

Added value to my researchThis is my 2SFCA/R2SFCA machinery repurposed as a sustainability denominator, and it is a genuinely novel bridge: industrial ecology has stocks without accessibility, health geography has accessibility without material accounting. A "material cost of one unit of primary-care access" or "tonnes of concrete per unit of mental-health service reach" is a metric I am uniquely placed to compute, and it links my accessibility work to the resource-efficiency and SDG literature that currently ignores it.

④ Turn "structural intensification" into a spatial optimization with an equity constraint

Extending this paperThe paper ends on four levers — lifespan extension, stock-intensity reduction, reuse, structural intensification — but presents them as a menu, not as an allocation problem. Yet intensification is inherently where-specific: densifying a transit-rich, under-utilized district avoids far more aggregate demand per additional dwelling than densifying at the fringe, and the co-benefits (shorter trips, lower transport demand) compound. Extending the paper: formulate intensification as a constrained spatial optimization — minimize future aggregate inflow subject to housing-service targets, existing stock utilization, and an equity floor that prevents the burden of densification from landing entirely on low-income neighbourhoods — and report the Pareto frontier between saved tonnage and distributional fairness.

Added value to my researchStructurally identical to my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization, with tonnes of avoided aggregates replacing charging utilization as the efficiency objective. The same solver, the same Gini/Theil-constrained formulation, a new and much larger-stakes application — and it gives my facility-siting work a materials-and-climate outcome variable that policy audiences already care about.

⑤ Make the six-driver decomposition spatially non-stationary — and forward-looking

Extending this paperThe paper's LMDI-style decomposition is exact, additive and backward-looking: it attributes 1991–2018 change into six factors per economy, with one coefficient set per country and no mechanism for prediction. Two extensions follow naturally. First, allow the driver contributions to vary within economies — a geographically weighted decomposition in which stock intensity and turnover carry different weights in a coastal megacity than in a shrinking interior region, exposing the within-country heterogeneity that national averages conceal. Second, embed the decomposition inside a learned forward model that projects RUI under scenario inputs (lifespan +10 years, stock intensity −20%, reuse rate ×2), so the paper's diagnostic becomes a policy simulator rather than a post-mortem.

Added value to my researchSquarely my MGWR + explainable ensemble toolkit: replacing globally constant decomposition coefficients with locally varying ones is the same non-stationarity argument I make in health-accessibility modelling, and pairing it with a differentiable surrogate that preserves the additive identity as a soft constraint is a clean physics-informed learning contribution — an interpretable model whose attributions still sum exactly to the observed change.
