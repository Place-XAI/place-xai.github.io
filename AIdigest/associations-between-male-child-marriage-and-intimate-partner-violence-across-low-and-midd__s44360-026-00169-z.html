Associations between male child marriage and intimate partner violence across low- and middle-income countries

Shaoru Chen · Ning Ma · Yuhao Kong · Zekun Chen · Shang Lou · Shuyi Jin · Peter Karoli · George Boamah Appiah · John Lapah Niyi · Million Phiri · Melkamu Aderajew Zemene · Hajirani M. Msuya · Akanni Ibukun Akinyemi · Rockli Kim · Feng Cheng · Yi Song · Xiaoying Zheng · S. V. Subramanian · Pascal Geldsetzer · Zhihui Li Published 2026-07-13 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00169-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across 41 low- and middle-income countries, men who married as boys (before age 18) show substantially higher risks of perpetrating nearly all types of intimate partner violence (IPV) and of experiencing physical IPV in the past 12 months. Counter-intuitively, the association is stronger where gender inequality is lower or income is higher, and attenuated in the most disadvantaged settings. At the macro level, country gender inequality (GII) correlates positively with IPV among men without a child-marriage history, but not among those who married as boys — implying that early marriage substitutes for, rather than adds to, the contextual risk gradient. Male child marriage is linked to an estimated 17%–25% of IPV cases among men aged 20–24.

★

Why It Matters

  • Puts boys into a debate that has been almost entirely about girls. Child marriage research and policy is overwhelmingly framed around girls' outcomes; this is a systematic, multi-country quantification of what happens when boys marry early — and of their role as both perpetrators and victims of IPV.
  • Converts a risk factor into a policy-sized quantity. The population attributable fraction of 17%–25% among men aged 20–24 says how much IPV would plausibly disappear if early marriage were delayed — a number ministries and donors can budget against.
  • Overturns a comfortable assumption about context. The expectation is that early marriage harms most in the most patriarchal, poorest settings. The paper finds the opposite gradient: the effect is largest in lower-gender-inequality and higher-income contexts, where early-married men are a stigmatized minority rather than the norm.
  • Breaks the macro–micro link. The GII–IPV correlation holds only among men who did not marry as children. That is direct evidence of effect-modification by individual life-course exposure, and a caution against reading country-level gender indices as uniform individual risk.
  • Two-sided victimization. By measuring men's own experience of physical IPV, it documents a rarely quantified outcome in LMICs and reframes prevention as a household-system problem rather than a one-directional one.

Core Methods

  • Pooled multi-country cross-sectional design. Harmonized DHS men's records from 41 LMICs are pooled, with country-specific IPV type definitions aligned to the standard domestic-violence module (physical, sexual, emotional; perpetration and victimization, past 12 months).
  • Regression on a binary early-marriage exposure. Adjusted models relate married-before-18 status to each IPV type, producing the "nearly all types" result of Fig. 1 and the pooled estimates behind Fig. 2.
  • Pre-specified subgroup / effect-modification analysis. Associations are re-estimated across strata — country gender inequality, country income level, and further subgroups reported in Extended Data Table 5 — which is where the counter-intuitive gradient (stronger effect in less unequal, richer settings) emerges.
  • Macro-level ecological correlation. Country-aggregated IPV prevalence is regressed/correlated against GII separately for child-married and non-child-married men (Fig. 3), testing whether the contextual gradient survives within the exposed group. It does not.
  • Population attributable fraction (PAF). Combining adjusted relative effects with exposure prevalence yields the 17%–25% attributable share among men aged 20–24, with variants for perpetration, victimization, and child marriage in either partner (Extended Data Tables 6–7).
  • Three robustness strategies. (i) age at first marriage as a multi-categorical exposure instead of a binary cut at 18; (ii) coarsened exact matching to balance covariates between early- and later-married men; (iii) adding spousal age difference as a covariate to separate early marriage from age-gap unions (Extended Data Tables 8–10).
  • Design limits to keep in view. Cross-sectional and self-reported; IPV reporting by men is subject to social-desirability bias whose magnitude itself varies with context — which is one plausible generator of the observed context gradient. Associational, not causal, despite the matching sensitivity analysis.

① Give the analysis a geography: from 41 country dots to a subnational IPV-risk surface

Extending this paperThe paper's spatial resolution is the country — 41 of them, each collapsed to one GII value and one income label in Figs. 2–3. But DHS ships georeferenced cluster coordinates (displaced 0–2 km urban / 0–5 km rural) for most of these surveys, and one of the paper's own references documents intra-urban variation in IPV in Kenya. A direct extension: re-run the child-marriage → IPV models with DHS cluster as the spatial unit, attach built-environment and deprivation covariates from satellite-derived layers (slum extent, built-up density, night-time lights, market access), and produce a continuous subnational risk surface rather than 41 point estimates. This would test whether the "attenuated in disadvantaged settings" result is a genuine contextual effect or an artifact of averaging heterogeneous provinces into one national GII.

It also extends my health-geography line beyond facility access into violence as a spatially structured health exposure.

② Turn the paradoxical context gradient into a spatially varying coefficient model

Extending this paperThe paper's most interesting result is an interaction: the child-marriage effect is larger where gender inequality is lower and income higher, and the GII–IPV slope vanishes among the early-married. It is currently expressed as discrete subgroup strata (Fig. 2) plus two separate regression lines (Fig. 3) — a coarse, pre-binned view of what is really a continuously varying coefficient. Extending the paper: replace the strata with a multilevel / geographically weighted (MGWR-style) specification in which the child-marriage coefficient is itself a smooth function of contextual covariates (GII, GNI, legal-gender score from Women, Business and the Law, early-marriage prevalence). That yields a coefficient map — "where does early marriage matter most" — and lets one test the paper's implicit "minority-status / normative-deviance" mechanism: the effect should peak where early marriage is rare, i.e. the coefficient should scale inversely with local exposure prevalence.

It also gives me a clean case where non-stationarity is theoretically predicted (deviance amplification) rather than discovered post hoc, which is exactly the kind of confirmatory test my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction papers need.

③ Harden the PAF: from adjusted association to transportable causal effect

Extending this paperThe headline policy number — 17%–25% of IPV among men aged 20–24 — is a PAF built on a cross-sectional, self-reported, adjusted association. The authors already reach for coarsened exact matching and a spousal-age-difference covariate (ED Tables 9–10), which signals awareness of confounding by family poverty, education and marriage-market structure. The natural extension is a full causal-ML upgrade: doubly-robust / TMLE estimation of the PAF with cross-fitted nuisance models; causal forests to learn the heterogeneity surface instead of pre-specifying subgroups; negative-control outcomes to bound reporting bias; and — most importantly — an explicit transportability analysis, since a PAF estimated on pooled 41-country data is not the PAF for any single ministry's population. Stable-learning ideas are directly relevant here because the covariate distribution shifts massively across the 41 countries.

The transportability machinery (density-ratio reweighting, stable feature selection) transfers straight back to my explainable ML for spatial prediction line, where "does this SHAP attribution survive out of region?" is the open question.

④ Close the loop to services: where does a 17–25% attributable burden meet zero support capacity?

Extending this paperThe paper ends at a prescription — "structural efforts to delay marriage alongside targeted support for individuals navigating early unions" — without asking whether that targeted support is reachable. Extending it: overlay the estimated attributable IPV burden onto the supply of GBV response capacity (health facilities offering post-violence care, one-stop centres, police gender desks, legal-aid points, hotline/telehealth coverage), and compute an accessibility-adjusted unmet need — a two-sided index in which demand is the model-predicted attributable burden and supply is service capacity within a realistic travel time. That converts three figures of epidemiology into a siting problem: where do you place the next 200 support points to cut the most attributable IPV per unit cost, under efficiency-vs-equity trade-offs?

⑤ Simulate the counterfactual the data cannot: agent-based marriage timing and norm diffusion

Extending this paperA PAF answers "how much is attributable" but not "what happens if we intervene" — it assumes exposure removal with everything else held fixed, which is exactly what fails for a norm-governed, marriage-market-coupled behaviour. Delaying boys' marriage changes the partner pool, spousal age gaps, and the local prevalence of early marriage, which the paper's own Fig. 2 says modulates the effect itself — a feedback the PAF cannot represent. Extension: build an agent-based marriage-market model calibrated to the paper's country-specific prevalences and effect sizes, with couples matched on age/education, an IPV hazard conditioned on early-marriage status, and a norm-diffusion layer on a social network. Then run the two policy arms the paper names — structural delay (age-of-marriage enforcement, schooling incentives) vs targeted support (couples counselling, media/norm campaigns) — and test whether the intervention that looks best under the PAF stays best once the norm feedback and minority-status amplification are switched on.

Added value to my researchSquarely my agent-based modeling + network community detection methods, applied to a norm-diffusion process instead of a mobility or epidemic one; the SIR-style machinery I use for contagion maps onto norm spread almost directly. Adding a spatial layer (village/cluster network with distance-decayed ties) makes it a spatially explicit policy simulator — a reusable engine for the "healthy cities / social-policy counterfactual" strand of my urban-health work.
