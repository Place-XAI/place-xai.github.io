Short- and long-term shifts in food purchasing behaviours and nutrition disparities in the USA during and after the COVID-19 pandemic

Guanlan Hu · Wei Lu · David Soberman · Mary R. L'Abbé Published 2026-08-14 DOI 10.1038/s41562-026-02547-9 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Using 9,367,550 weekly shopping baskets from over 30,000 US households (2017–2022), linked to county-specific COVID-19 policies and to AI-enhanced nutrition profiling, the paper shows that the pandemic's dietary shock reversed direction over time: during lockdowns households shifted spending towards less processed foods, but by the post-pandemic period spending on ultraprocessed foods rose above pre-pandemic levels. The divergence is visible mainly in the processing-based NOVA measure and much less in nutrient-based profiles (HEI, FSANZ), showing the two families of metrics are complementary rather than interchangeable. Socioeconomically, the spending gap widened in 2020 and then converged, while a new nutrition gap emerged as higher-income households increased spending on less nutritious foods; prepared/frozen foods and carbonated beverages show persistent effects.

★

Why It Matters

  • Separates transient shock from permanent behavioural drift. Most pandemic-diet studies stop in 2020–2021 and therefore record only the "cook-at-home" improvement. By running the panel through 2022, this paper shows that the improvement was temporary and that the durable legacy is a higher ultraprocessed-food share — a qualitatively different policy conclusion.
  • Measured behaviour, not recalled behaviour. Scanner-based baskets at UPC granularity remove the recall and social-desirability bias that dominate dietary-survey literature, and they resolve weekly dynamics that surveys cannot.
  • Shows that the choice of diet metric changes the verdict. NOVA flags a large post-pandemic deterioration while HEI/FSANZ barely move. That is a methodological warning for any monitoring system, dashboard, or policy target built on a single nutrient-based index.
  • Reframes the equity story. The familiar "poorer households eat worse" narrative is complicated: the spending gap converged post-pandemic while a new nutrition gap opened from the top, driven by higher-income households buying more convenience/ultraprocessed products. Equity policy targeted only at low-income groups would miss this.
  • Directly actionable for food-system resilience. Category-level persistence (prepared and frozen foods, carbonated beverages) tells regulators exactly where reformulation, labelling, or fiscal instruments would have the most durable leverage.

Core Methods

  • AI-enhanced nutrition profiling. A fine-tuned language-model / NLP-ML pipeline over product names, ingredient lists and nutrient panels assigns each purchased UPC a NOVA processing level and predicts nutrient-based scores, trained and validated against FLIP-Canada labels. This is what makes population-scale processing classification feasible — manual NOVA coding of millions of UPCs is not.
  • Three-metric diet-quality measurement. Each basket is scored under NOVA (processing), HEI (dietary-pattern adherence) and FSANZ NPSC (nutrient profiling), so the paper can test whether conclusions are metric-dependent — and it finds they are.
  • Quasi-experimental design on staggered policy timing. County stay-at-home and reopening orders arrived at different dates, giving a staggered-adoption panel design; the reference list points to heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences (de Chaisemartin & d'Haultfœuille) and counterfactual estimators for time-series cross-sectional data (Liu, Wang & Xu) rather than naive two-way fixed effects.
  • Dynamic event-study estimation. Fig. 2 reports week-by-week treatment paths around lockdown and reopening, which is how the short-run improvement and its later reversal are separated instead of averaged away.
  • Phase segmentation. Outcomes are estimated separately for pre-pandemic, pandemic/lockdown, reopening and post-pandemic windows — the design element that produces the paper's central short-vs-long-term contrast.
  • SES stratification and gap decomposition. Households are grouped by socioeconomic status (federal poverty guidelines / income are cited as the reference basis), and spending gaps are tracked separately from nutrition gaps, which is what surfaces the emergent high-income nutrition penalty.
  • Category-level heterogeneity. Effects are re-estimated by food category (Fig. 4), isolating prepared and frozen foods and carbonated beverages as the persistent movers.
  • Real-terms normalization. All spending is CPI-deflated so the 2021–2022 food-price surge does not contaminate estimated behavioural effects.

① Give the paper a geography: from household SES to a spatial food-environment exposure model

Extending this paperThe design is aspatial in its outcome layer: counties enter only as carriers of policy timing and case counts, and disparities are cut by SES rather than by place. Yet the same NielsenIQ households sit inside wildly different retail landscapes. A direct extension: geocode household ZIP/county to a food-retail supply surface (store counts, store mix, network travel time to the nearest full-line grocer) and re-estimate the phase effects with supply accessibility as a moderator. The testable claim is that the post-pandemic ultraprocessed rebound is largest where physical access to fresh-food outlets is weakest — which would convert an observed behavioural drift into a targetable spatial intervention, and would explain part of the residual heterogeneity the current SES-only stratification must be absorbing.

Added value to my researchThis is a natural new application domain for my 2SFCA/R2SFCA machinery: swap clinics for grocery stores, patients for households, and the supply-demand catchment formalism transfers intact — except that here, uniquely, I would have a measured consumption outcome to validate the accessibility index against. Almost every accessibility paper I write validates against proxies; this dataset design offers ground-truth behavioural validation of a 2SFCA score, which is exactly the missing link in the accessibility-metric literature.

② Replace the SES cut with a spatial-regime cut: where, not just who

Extending this paperThe paper's most surprising result — the spending gap converging while a new nutrition gap opens from the high-income side — is reported as a single national contrast. But US metropolitan areas are organized into socioeconomically sorted rings and pockets, so a national SES coefficient is almost certainly an average over opposing local regimes: high-income suburban households with car access and delivery services versus high-income urban-core households with dense fresh-retail proximity may be moving in different directions. Extending the paper: estimate the phase-by-SES effects as spatially varying coefficients (GWR/MGWR over county or CBSA units), and report where the emergent high-income nutrition penalty is concentrated rather than averaging it into one number.

Added value to my researchThis is a textbook case for MGWR with process-specific bandwidths: the policy-exposure effect probably operates at a fine (county) scale while the income effect operates at a coarse (regional) scale, which is exactly the multiscale situation MGWR was designed for and rarely gets to demonstrate on a nine-million-observation behavioural panel. It also gives me a route to combine MGWR with staggered-DiD identification — a methodological hybrid (spatially varying treatment effects) that I have wanted a suitable dataset for.

Cross-ref (corpus): US cities are defined by rings and pockets with limited socioeconomic mixing — 10.1038/s44284-025-00350-7

③ Close the loop from basket to body: link purchasing trajectories to health outcomes

Extending this paperThe paper stops at the checkout. It establishes that ultraprocessed spending rose durably above baseline, and cites the literature linking ultraprocessed intake to obesity and cardiometabolic disease — but never estimates the health consequence of the shift it documents. The extension is a two-stage design: use the paper's county-phase-SES effect estimates as an exposure variable, and regress subsequent county-level BMI, diabetes incidence or cardiometabolic indicators on that exposure, controlling for built-environment confounders. This would turn a consumption paper into a health-impact paper and quantify the deferred disease burden of a behavioural change that has already happened — the single most policy-relevant number the current design leaves on the table.

Added value to my researchThis lands squarely in my health geography line and gives it an upstream behavioural mechanism it usually lacks: my accessibility work explains where care is available, but not what makes people need it. A purchasing-to-morbidity pathway lets me build a spatial chain — retail environment → purchasing behaviour → cardiometabolic burden → demand on primary care capacity — where my 2SFCA supply-side models supply the last link. That is a full space–human coupling model of the kind my research program is aimed at, not another isolated accessibility index.

Cross-ref (corpus): The urban built environment and adult BMI, obesity, and diabetes in Latin American cities — 10.1038/s41467-022-35648-w

④ Audit and de-bias the AI nutrition classifier as a measurement instrument

Extending this paperThe entire NOVA-versus-HEI/FSANZ divergence — the paper's headline methodological claim — rests on a fine-tuned language model trained on FLIP-Canada and applied to US branded products. Canadian and US formulations, labelling conventions and category vocabularies differ, so any systematic classification error is a plausible alternative explanation for why NOVA moves while nutrient-based metrics do not: NOVA is inferred from text (ingredient lists), while HEI/FSANZ are computed from numeric nutrient panels, meaning the two metric families do not share an error structure. Extending the paper: run an explainability and transfer audit — per-category confusion analysis on a US-labelled holdout, token-attribution to see which ingredient terms drive the ultraprocessed label, and a sensitivity analysis showing how much classifier error would be required to erase the post-pandemic effect. If the effect survives, the claim hardens considerably.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML agenda applied to a domain where the model is not the product but the measuring instrument — a framing I want to develop further, because the same logic governs my use of fine-tuned geospatial foundation models: when a model generates the variable that a downstream causal estimate consumes, its errors become confounders, and attribution methods become validity checks rather than presentation aids. A reusable "instrument audit" protocol (holdout transfer + attribution + error-tolerance sensitivity) is a portable methodological contribution across my prediction work.

Cross-ref (corpus): Stop explaining black box machine learning models for high stakes decisions and use interpretable models instead — 10.1038/s42256-019-0048-x

⑤ Fuse the scanner panel with mobility and transaction streams to recover the missing half of the diet

Extending this paperA scanner panel sees food-at-home only. Restaurant meals, delivery apps and workplace eating are invisible, which matters enormously here because the pandemic's defining behavioural change was the collapse and partial recovery of food-away-from-home — and because remote work permanently redistributed where people eat lunch. The paper's post-pandemic "ultraprocessed rebound" could therefore be partly a substitution artefact from returning-to-office households. Extension: fuse the basket panel with mobility traces (visits to food-service POIs) and card-transaction sequences to reconstruct total dietary exposure, and test whether the rebound is attenuated once away-from-home consumption is restored to the ledger. This is the most serious threat to the paper's long-run claim and also the most tractable to address with existing corpus methods.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly my multi-source data fusion line — mobile-phone traces, POI visits, transaction records and survey panels reconciled into one behavioural exposure surface — and it supplies the missing behavioural layer for my healthy-city and 15-minute-neighbourhood work, where I currently measure opportunity (what is reachable) but never realized consumption (what is actually bought and eaten). A fused exposure measure also feeds directly into spatiotemporal KDE and agent-based simulation of dietary behaviour under retail or zoning interventions, giving those simulations empirically calibrated agents instead of assumed preferences.
