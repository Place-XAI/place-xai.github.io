Burden of dengue in children in eight endemic countries in Asia and Latin America: a secondary analysis of the DEN-301 trial

Randee Kastner · Eduardo López-Medina · Chukiat Sirivichayakul · Shibadas Biswal · Ian Escudero · Nicolas Folschweiller · Nicholas Roubinis · Vianney Tricou · Suely Tuboi · John Weil Published 2026-07-08 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00163-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

The placebo arm of the phase 3 TAK-003 trial (DEN-301) turns a vaccine trial into a rare standardized, prospective natural-history study of dengue: across 6,687 children aged 4–16 in 8 endemic countries and 30,620.6 person-years of active surveillance (Sept 2016–Dec 2021), 9,698 febrile illnesses occurred (31.7 per 100 p-y) of which only 5.8% (n = 560) were virologically confirmed dengue. Burden was strongly heterogeneous in space, age and time: incidence in Asia Pacific (2.7 per 100 p-y) was more than double Latin America (1.2), DENV-1 (41.1%) and DENV-2 (34.5%) dominated, 25.4% (142/560) of cases were hospitalized — but hospitalization ranged from 4.4% in Panama to 68.0% in Sri Lanka — and cases collapsed after April 2020 during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic. Severe outcomes were rare (19 participants with DHF or severe dengue). The authors conclude that control must be adaptive and place-specific, combining vaccination, vector control and surveillance.

★

Why It Matters

  • It fixes the measurement problem that plagues dengue epidemiology. Routine national surveillance systems differ in case definitions, testing intensity and care-seeking, so cross-country incidence is essentially incomparable. Here, one protocol, one case definition, one RT-PCR pipeline was applied at 26 sites in 8 countries — 98% (9,511/9,698) of febrile episodes were tested, 94.3% within 5 days of fever onset. The resulting country contrasts are real signal, not surveillance artefact.
  • It quantifies how narrow the "dengue = fever" assumption is. Only 5.8% of active-surveillance febrile illness in these highly endemic settings was dengue, ranging from 2.4% (Dominican Republic) to 15.2% (Sri Lanka). Syndromic-surveillance and fever-proxy models that infer dengue burden from febrile counts are calibrating against a very low and spatially variable positive fraction.
  • Hospitalization is revealed as a health-system variable, not a biological one. With no protocol-mandated admission criteria, the 4.4%-to-68.0% spread in hospitalized VCD across countries measures local clinical practice and care capacity as much as disease severity — a direct warning against using hospitalization records as a severity proxy in cross-national burden models.
  • It captures a natural experiment in mobility. The synchronous decline in VCD at all sites from April 2020 links reduced human movement to reduced transmission — mechanistically plausible given that Aedes aegypti flies only ~106 m on average, so it is people, not mosquitoes, that move the virus between neighbourhoods.
  • It sets the counterfactual baseline for vaccine-era policy. Serotype mix (DENV-1 41.1%, DENV-2 34.5%, DENV-3 20.4%, DENV-4 4.1%), 72.6% baseline seropositivity, and the rarity of DHF/severe dengue define exactly the epidemiological conditions under which TAK-003 and future vaccines must show benefit.

Why Nature Health (Editorial Fit)

  • Reuses an expensive trial asset for a public-good question. Nature Health favours work that converts existing clinical infrastructure into population-health evidence; a placebo arm re-read as a natural-history cohort is exactly that move.
  • Multi-country, standardized, and directly policy-translatable. Eight endemic countries on two continents with harmonized case ascertainment gives the comparability that WHO-facing burden estimates and vaccine introduction decisions require.
  • Heterogeneity is the finding, not the noise. The paper's contribution is that geography, age and time each modulate burden — which argues for adaptive, place-specific control strategies rather than a single global recommendation.
  • Long-horizon, prospective, and pandemic-spanning. Fifty-seven months of continuous active surveillance across the COVID-19 disruption is an unusual and non-reproducible observational window.
  • Transparent about its own limits. The authors state plainly that a trial cohort without proportionate country sampling may not represent the general population, and that under-4s were excluded — the kind of honest scoping the journal expects of burden claims.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Primary source
Placebo arm of DEN-301 (ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02747927), a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial of the tetravalent dengue vaccine TAK-003 (Takeda). Secondary post hoc analysis of the long-term (4.5-year) efficacy/safety dataset.
Sample size
Trial enrolled 20,099 participants (2:1 vaccine:placebo). 6,698 randomized to placebo; 6,687 received ≥1 placebo dose and form the safety set analysed here; 91% (6,080/6,687) were still in the trial at the end of the reporting period.
Exposure time
30,620.6 person-years of active surveillance over ~57 months after the first dose.
Spatial coverage
26 medical and research centres in 8 countries — Asia Pacific: Philippines, Sri Lanka, Thailand; Latin America: Brazil, Colombia, Dominican Republic, Nicaragua, Panama. Enrolment split: Latin America 55.2% (3,694), Asia Pacific 44.8% (2,993). Site coordinates are not published in the article; the finest published geographic unit is the country.
Time window
Enrolment began 2016; febrile-illness surveillance September 2016 – December 2021, reported in 3-month bins in Fig. 2.
Population
Healthy children and adolescents aged 4–16 years at randomization; median age 9.6 years (SD 3.34); analysed in age strata 4–5, 6–11, 12–16 years. Children under 4 were not included.
Case ascertainment
9,698 febrile illnesses (≥38 °C on any two of three consecutive days), IR 31.7 per 100 p-y; 9,511 (98%) RT-PCR tested, 9,138 (94.3%) within 5 days of fever onset; 560 VCD cases (13 were second episodes); 142 hospitalized VCD; 15 DHF (WHO 1997 definition) + 5 severe dengue (adjudication committee) = 19 distinct participants.
Serology
Baseline serostatus from pre-vaccination blood samples; seropositive = reciprocal neutralizing titre ≥10 against ≥1 DENV serotype. 72.6% (4,854/6,686) seropositive at baseline (Asia Pacific 74%, Latin America 72%). Immunogenicity subset of 4,000 participants (1,333 placebo); within it, 367 baseline-seronegative placebo participants contributed 1,444.08 p-y and 88 seroconversions → infection incidence 6.09 (95% CI 4.89–7.51) per 100 p-y.
Serotyping
Serotype-specific RT-PCR on all VCD cases. Overall DENV-1 41.1%, DENV-2 34.5%, DENV-3 20.4%, DENV-4 4.1% (23/560).
Software
SAS version 9.3 or above. The authors state that no custom code or algorithms were generated for this study — there is no public analysis repository.
Availability
Individual participant data, redacted protocol and statistical analysis plan available on request within 3 months, de-identified, via Takeda's data-sharing process and Vivli. Not open-access data; requests need a methodologically sound proposal.
Not in the paper
Honest gaps for downstream modellers: no geocoded participant or site locations, no linked climate/land-cover/mosquito-density covariates, no viral sequence data (serotype only, not genotype), no household or school locations, and no site-level (as opposed to country-level) case tables in the main text.

Core Methods

  • Design: secondary post hoc analysis of the placebo arm of a double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled phase 3 trial (2:1 randomization, two doses 3 months apart). The placebo arm is repurposed as a prospective natural-history cohort.
  • Active surveillance, not passive reporting: participants were contacted at least weekly and reminded to present at the study site on any febrile illness (≥38 °C on any two of three consecutive days) — capturing both hospitalized and ambulatory cases, which is the key departure from routine national surveillance.
  • Virological confirmation: VCD defined as febrile or clinically suspected dengue illness with a positive serotype-specific RT-PCR; testing coverage and time-to-test are explicitly reported (98% tested, 94.3% within 5 days).
  • Serostatus stratification: pre-vaccination neutralizing-antibody titres (≥10 threshold) define baseline seropositive/seronegative strata; within the immunogenicity subset, paired serial blood draws yield a seroconversion-based infection incidence that includes subclinical infections missed by fever-triggered testing.
  • Severity classification with two standards in parallel: DHF by the WHO 1997 case definition and severe dengue by an independent dengue case severity adjudication committee — with the overlap explicitly reconciled (one case met both, so 15 + 5 = 19 participants, not 20).
  • Incidence estimation: rates expressed per 100 person-years with exact two-sided 95% confidence intervals from the Poisson distribution; temporal dynamics binned at 3-month intervals by region.
  • Stratified descriptive analysis: febrile illness, VCD, hospitalized VCD, DHF and severe dengue tabulated by country and by age group (4–5, 6–11, 12–16 years); presented descriptively rather than with inferential comparisons.
  • Inference on serostatus structure: logistic regression on baseline seroprevalence confirmed effects of country (χ² = 494.5, P < 0.0001), age group (χ² = 35.6, P < 0.0001) and a country × age interaction (χ² = 36.4, P = 0.0009) — i.e., force of infection accumulates with age at country-specific rates.
  • Analysis stack: SAS 9.3+; no bespoke code, no simulation or mechanistic transmission model — the contribution is measurement quality, not modelling machinery.

① Transport the trial's gold-standard incidence surface off the 26 sites and onto the map

Extending this paperThe paper's own stated limitation is decisive: "these results are from a clinical trial without proportionate sampling by country, the populations represented may not reflect the general population." Right now the highest-quality dengue incidence measurement in existence — 30,620.6 person-years of uniformly ascertained, RT-PCR-confirmed cases — is stranded at 26 unlocated points. The extension is a transportability / target-population weighting layer: geocode the 26 centres, characterize each site's catchment on covariates the trial already conditions on (age structure, baseline seroprevalence, urban density, serotype history) and reweight site-specific incidence to the covariate distribution of each country's full paediatric population. That converts the trial's internally valid estimates into an externally valid, uncertainty-quantified national burden surface, and — because the trial's seroprevalence differs from the general population's — makes explicit how much of the Asia Pacific vs Latin America 2.7-vs-1.2 gap survives reweighting. The paper itself notes that a definitive answer on regional severity differences "would require population-based surveillance"; transportability is the cheaper first approximation.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean, high-value template for my GeoAI + spatial-epidemiology line: a formal machinery for pushing site-sampled clinical evidence onto continuous population geography. The same reweighting operator is exactly what my telehealth and primary-care accessibility work needs — clinic-sampled utilization data are never a random sample of the service area, and I have been treating that as a nuisance rather than as an estimable transport function. Building it here, on a dataset where the ground truth is unusually clean, gives me a reusable component for every "measured at facilities, needed over space" problem, and it pairs naturally with explainable ML: the covariate-shift weights themselves become an interpretable map of where the evidence does and does not generalize.

② Decompose the 4.4%-to-68.0% hospitalization spread into severity, care-seeking and spatial access

Extending this paperThe single most striking — and least explained — number in the paper is that hospitalized VCD ranged from 4.4% in Panama to 68.0% in Sri Lanka, with the authors attributing it to the absence of protocol admission criteria and to local clinical practice. That attribution is plausible but untested, and it matters enormously: it means hospitalization-based dengue burden estimates are contaminated by health-system geography. The extension is a three-way decomposition of the admission probability into (a) clinical severity, which the trial already measures independently via DHF/severe-dengue adjudication and which was not proportionally higher in Sri Lanka; (b) national admission-threshold culture; and (c) spatial accessibility of the admitting facility — travel time from each site's catchment to the nearest hospital with paediatric dengue capacity, computed with a 2SFCA/R2SFCA formulation on road networks. A hierarchical model with country random effects and an access covariate would say how much of the 15-fold spread is geography rather than biology.

Added value to my researchThis is directly my healthcare-accessibility agenda applied to an infectious-disease outcome, and it inverts the usual direction of my work in a productive way. Normally I compute 2SFCA accessibility and treat health outcomes as the dependent variable; here I would use a rigorously adjudicated severity measure as an instrument to isolate how much observed hospitalization reflects access rather than need. That is a validation opportunity for R2SFCA I rarely get: a setting where "true need" is measured to trial standard. It also produces a transferable correction factor for hospital-record-based disease mapping — a persistent methodological headache in my urban-health work with administrative data.

③ Turn the pandemic collapse into a quantified mobility–transmission elasticity

Extending this paperThe paper observes a synchronous drop in VCD at all sites from April 2020, and reasons — correctly and elegantly — that because Aedes aegypti flies on average only ~106 m (95% CI 87.68–123.69 m), it is human movement that transports the virus, so lockdowns should suppress transmission. But the paper stops at narrative. Because active surveillance methodology did not change during the pandemic, this is an unusually clean natural experiment, and the extension is to estimate the elasticity of dengue incidence with respect to human mobility: overlay country- and admin-level mobility indices for each 3-month bin in Fig. 2, control for the seasonal/climatic cycle and for the serotype-specific susceptibility pool, and fit an interrupted-time-series or synthetic-control model per region. The confound the paper itself flags — reduced febrile-illness reporting due to quarantine fears — is separable here, because the immunogenicity subset's seroconversion-based infection incidence is not fever-triggered and therefore not subject to care-seeking suppression. That contrast between the two incidence estimators is the identification strategy.

Added value to my researchA mobility elasticity for a vector-borne disease is a genuinely new parameter for my spatiotemporal modelling toolbox, and one I can estimate with the data types I already work with (mobile-phone records, GPS trajectories). More broadly this is the physics-informed framing I keep pushing: the mosquito's ~106 m flight range is a hard length-scale prior that should be encoded directly in a graph-learning model — local diffusion capped at the vector scale, long-range edges carried exclusively by human mobility flows. That is a concrete, physically grounded architecture rather than a generic spatial GNN, and dengue is an ideal testbed because the two length scales are separated by three orders of magnitude.

④ Downscale country-level serotype composition into a spatiotemporal serotype-replacement field

Extending this paperFigure 3 reports serotype composition at the country level, and the paper rightly notes this is one of the few standardized serotype distributions available anywhere ("the decision to evaluate the DENV serotype of dengue cases lacks standardization across countries"). But serotype replacement is a fundamentally local, wave-like process — Sri Lanka's 84.5% DENV-2 and the Philippines' 55.0% DENV-3 are averages over outbreak waves that swept through at specific times and places. The extension: reconstruct a spatiotemporal serotype-composition field by combining the trial's 560 serotyped cases with their dates and site catchments using spatiotemporal kernel density estimation, then test whether the fine-scale population density structure of each catchment predicts wave speed and serotype turnover. Because each serotype's susceptible pool is partly determined by the measured baseline serostatus, the field is not just descriptive — it can be constrained by an immunity-depletion term, giving a semi-mechanistic downscaling rather than pure interpolation.

Added value to my researchThis is my spatiotemporal KDE line applied to a genuinely multivariate compositional outcome — four serotypes summing to one, evolving in space and time — which is harder and more interesting than the univariate intensity surfaces I usually estimate. It forces a compositional-data extension of the kernel machinery (log-ratio transforms in a spatiotemporal setting) that would transfer straight to my urban-analytics work on land-use mix and job-housing composition. The immunity-depletion constraint is also the kind of soft physical prior my physics-informed learning agenda is built on: a conservation-like rule that keeps extrapolation honest where data are sparse.

⑤ Convert the burden map into an efficiency-versus-equity vaccine and vector-control allocation problem

Extending this paperThe paper closes by calling for "adaptive multipronged strategies that integrate vaccination, sustained vector control and enhanced surveillance," but offers no allocation machinery — it delivers the burden signal and stops. The extension is to make that call operational: take the paper's age-, country- and serotype-stratified incidence (including the finding that febrile-illness incidence falls steeply with age, 53.4 per 100 p-y at 4–5 years vs 20.3 at 12–16, while hospitalized VCD incidence is comparatively flat across age strata) as the demand layer, and solve a constrained allocation problem over doses and vector-control effort under a joint efficiency-and-equity objective. Two features make this non-trivial and specific to this paper: the seropositive/seronegative split (72.6%/27.4%) means benefit is serostatus-dependent, so allocation interacts with pre-vaccination screening capacity; and the age gradient means the optimal target age differs between minimizing total cases and minimizing hospitalizations. Coupling the allocation to an agent-based transmission model with the ~106 m vector kernel plus human mobility would let the optimizer exploit spatial spillovers rather than treat districts as independent.

Added value to my researchThis maps almost one-to-one onto my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization, but with a spatial externality that charging stations do not have: vaccinating one neighbourhood reduces transmission in its neighbours. Building the herd-effect term into my existing efficiency–equity Pareto framework is a real methodological upgrade — the objective stops being separable across locations, which changes both the optimization and the fairness accounting. It also connects my agent-based modelling and network community-detection work to a policy question with clear stakes, and the resulting allocation tool would generalize back to my facility-siting problems wherever service benefits spill across catchment boundaries.
