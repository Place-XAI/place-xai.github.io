Structure-alignment-driven cross-graph modeling for functional RNA design

Shengfan Wang · Jun Wang · Xiaojian Liu · Weimin Zhu · Jiaqi Huang · Yiming Xue · Junchi Yan · Hong-Bin Shen · Jie Song · Xiaoyong Pan Published 2026-07-30 DOI 10.1038/s43588-026-01029-2 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

AlignIF reframes RNA inverse folding: instead of conditioning on a single target 3D structure, it builds a multiple-structure alignment (MStA) ensemble of structurally homologous RNAs and couples them through cross-graph modeling plus hand-designed geometric features, so the model learns evolutionarily conserved structural patterns rather than one native sequence. It beats state-of-the-art baselines (RDesign, RiboDiffusion, gRNAde) on recovery, foldability and evolutionary fidelity, and — critically — designs entire RNA families instead of merely recapitulating the wild type. Wet-lab validation without any postdesign filtering: for Mango-I, 2 of 10 designed aptamers were brighter than wild type and 2 more bound TO1-biotin more tightly; for the harder iMango-III, all designs fluoresced, including one at ~30% sequence identity to the wild type; pistol ribozymes also showed measurable self-cleavage activity.

Why It Matters

  • Breaks the "one structure → one sequence" bottleneck. RNA 3D structures are dynamic and unstable, so conditioning on a single deposited conformer over-fits crystallographic noise. Conditioning on an aligned ensemble of homologous structures is a conceptual shift: the design target becomes a structural family, not a single snapshot.
  • Changes the success metric. The field's default score — native sequence recovery rate — rewards copying the wild type. AlignIF is explicitly evaluated on evolutionary fidelity (KL divergence and entropy difference against reference MSA profiles), i.e. whether the generated sequence distribution matches what evolution actually explores.
  • Wet-lab validated with no post-hoc filtering. Most generative-design papers screen thousands of candidates in silico first. Here a small designed set went straight to the bench and worked — the strongest available evidence that the learned prior is real, not a benchmark artifact.
  • Designs functional, not just foldable, RNA. Fluorescent light-up aptamers (Mango-I, iMango-III) and self-cleaving pistol ribozymes are function-bearing molecules with measurable readouts (fluorescence, Kd, cleavage rate) — a much harder bar than tertiary self-consistency.
  • Ships as usable infrastructure. Open code (GitHub, frozen tag v1.2), Zenodo datasets and splits, trained checkpoints, a one-command reproduction script, and a public web server.

Core Methods

  • Multiple structure alignment (MStA) as the conditioning object: for each design target, retrieve and structurally align an ensemble of homologous RNA 3D structures (US-align lineage), so the model sees a distribution of conformations/homologs rather than one deposited structure.
  • Cross-graph modeling: each aligned structure is a nucleotide-level graph (nodes = nucleotides, edges = spatial neighbours within a distance cutoff); message passing runs across graphs along alignment correspondences, so conserved structural positions exchange information and the model can read off which positions are structurally constrained versus tolerant.
  • Hand-designed geometric features: explicitly engineered structural descriptors are injected alongside learned embeddings — an interpretable, physics/geometry-informed prior rather than pure end-to-end representation learning.
  • Autoregressive/probabilistic sequence decoding with temperature control: 20 sequences per structure at T = 0.1 / 0.5 / 1.0; average log-probability correlates with recovery rate (Extended Data Fig. 1), giving a built-in confidence signal.
  • Three-tier evaluation: (1) native-sequence recovery, perplexity, Macro-F1; (2) foldability self-consistency — fold the designed sequence with AlphaFold3/RhoFold+ and compare to the target (scTM, scRMSD, scGDT_TS, scMCC), with native sequences as the upper bound; (3) evolutionary fidelity — KL divergence and |ΔH| between generated sequence profiles and reference MSA profiles, including specifically at base-pair positions.
  • Statistics: one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests against gRNAde (multistate); Spearman correlations with 95% CIs; all results averaged over 3 random seeds.
  • Ablation and sensitivity: Fig. 5 ablates architectural components; Extended Data Fig. 5 sweeps the neighbour-distance cutoff, showing average node degree grows roughly linearly with cutoff — an explicit graph-construction sensitivity analysis.
  • Bench validation: designed aptamers assayed by fluorescence and Kd titration with TO1-biotin plus circular dichroism (G-quadruplex confirmation); ribozymes assayed by pre-steady-state bimolecular cleavage kinetics — all without any in-silico prefiltering of the designs.

① Make the alignment itself learnable, not a fixed preprocessing step

Extending this paperAlignIF's MStA ensemble is assembled before the network runs: homologs are retrieved and structurally aligned by a classical aligner, then frozen as the conditioning object. That makes the model's ceiling the aligner's ceiling — a bad alignment silently poisons the cross-graph messages, and the paper archives the ensembles as fixed artifacts on Zenodo. The direct extension is to replace the hard correspondence matrix with a differentiable soft-alignment (cross-attention) layer that learns which nucleotides in homolog k should inform position i of the target, trained end-to-end against the design objective. Ablation Fig. 5 already isolates the ensemble's contribution; a learned-correspondence variant would show how much of the gain is the ensemble versus the alignment quality, and would let the method work on targets with poor or no structural homologs — currently its blind spot.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly the operator I need for cross-city transferable GeoAI. My models are usually fitted per city because street networks, POI graphs and service catchments are not node-aligned across regions. A learned soft-alignment layer over multiple city graphs would let a healthcare-accessibility model trained on one metro pass messages to structurally analogous neighbourhoods elsewhere — "structural homology" between urban subgraphs replacing the assumption of shared coordinates. It also gives MGWR a graph-native cousin: instead of a spatial bandwidth, a learned correspondence weight decides which other places inform the local estimate.

② Quantify the hand-designed features — turn the ablation into an attribution

Extending this paperThe abstract makes a striking admission for a 2026 deep-learning paper: performance depends on "carefully hand-designed features". Fig. 5 ablates them as a block, but never says which geometric descriptor buys which behaviour, nor whether the same information could be learned given more data. Extending the paper: run a per-feature attribution (Shapley-style or leave-one-feature-out across the 3-seed protocol) on the two test sets, stratified by RNA class and by nucleotide context (base-paired vs. loop vs. junction). The likely payoff is a compact, interpretable claim — e.g. that a handful of backbone/base-orientation descriptors carry most of the signal at base-paired positions, which is precisely where the paper's evolutionary-fidelity metric (Extended Data Fig. 3) is measured. That converts an engineering detail into a physical statement about RNA design.

Added value to my researchThis is the same argument I make for explainable ML in spatial prediction: engineered, physically meaningful covariates (density gradients, network centrality, entropy of land-use mix) often beat learned embeddings on small samples, and their attribution is the scientific product, not a diagnostic. AlignIF gives me a clean template for a paper structure I reuse: hybrid hand-designed + learned features, block ablation, then per-feature attribution stratified by spatial regime — directly applicable to my telehealth-uptake and primary-care-accessibility models where sample sizes are far below deep-learning comfort.

③ Optimize for family diversity explicitly, instead of hoping for it

Extending this paperThe paper's headline novelty — designing entire RNA families rather than recapitulating natives — is currently an emergent property obtained by sampling at temperature 0.1/0.5/1.0 and then measuring KL divergence against MSA profiles post hoc. Diversity is never in the loss. Extending it: add an explicit diversity-and-novelty objective — reward sequences that are far in identity from the wild type and retain predicted foldability, e.g. by fine-tuning the decoder with reinforcement learning against a composite reward (scTM from AlphaFold3 × pairwise-identity dispersion × MSA-profile match). The iMango-III result (all designs fluorescent, one at ~30% identity to WT) is proof that the deep-novelty regime is reachable; an explicit objective would let the user dial the identity target rather than discover it by temperature sweeping.

Added value to my researchDirectly transferable to my facility-siting and EV-charging optimization: planners do not want one optimum, they want a portfolio of near-optimal, structurally distinct configurations that survive political negotiation. Recasting the search as "generate a diverse family of solutions that all satisfy the accessibility constraint" — with a dispersion term in the reward — is the urban-analytics twin of designing an RNA family, and it pairs naturally with my efficiency-vs-equity Pareto framing.

④ Close the loop: from one-shot wet-lab validation to active design–test cycles

Extending this paperThe experimental section is a single open-loop round: 10 Mango-I aptamers, a handful of iMango-III designs, 7 pistol ribozymes, measured once, with no feedback into the model. Yet the paper already has every ingredient for a closed loop — a calibrated confidence signal (average log-probability correlates with recovery, Extended Data Fig. 1), cheap in-silico judges (AlphaFold3, RhoFold+), and quantitative bench readouts (fluorescence, Kd, cleavage rate). The extension is a batch active-learning campaign: use predictive uncertainty plus expected functional gain to pick each round's 10 constructs, fine-tune on measured Kd/activity, and report how many rounds it takes to exceed wild-type brightness reliably rather than 2-of-10. That also answers the question the paper cannot: is the ~30%-identity success a lucky draw or a reproducible regime?

Added value to my researchMy spatial work has the same open-loop pathology — we predict accessibility deserts, publish, and never measure whether an intervention moved the outcome. The AlignIF setup is a clean argument for active spatial experimentation: rank candidate telehealth-outreach or clinic-siting interventions by model uncertainty × expected equity gain, deploy a small batch, and refit. It reframes "validation data" as something we choose, which is a much stronger design for the policy-facing half of my agenda.

⑤ Export the "conserved motif" abstraction — and the fidelity metric — to spatial systems

Extending this paperAlignIF's deepest idea is not RNA-specific: align many instances of the same functional structure, and the positions that stay constant across the ensemble are the ones that carry function. Within the paper, that idea is only used to condition generation. A natural extension is to make the conserved patterns an explicit, inspectable output — emit a per-position conservation/tolerance map from the cross-graph attention, validate it against known functional elements (the G-quadruplex core of Mango aptamers confirmed by CD, the pistol pseudoknot cleavage site), and publish it as a structural-motif atlas. The paper's evolutionary-fidelity metrics (KL divergence, |ΔH| against MSA profiles) then become a general-purpose distributional validation protocol for any generative model whose output should match a reference ensemble rather than a single ground truth.

Added value to my researchTwo concrete imports. First, urban structural motifs: align street/POI subgraphs across many neighbourhoods with similar health outcomes and extract the positions that are conserved — the built-environment analogue of a functional motif, and a mechanistic complement to my correlational healthy-cities models. Second, the metric: my generative and simulation work (agent-based models, spatiotemporal KDE, synthetic mobility) is still judged by pointwise error against one realization; borrowing KL-divergence-and-entropy-difference-against-a-reference-ensemble gives me a principled way to say a synthetic city is distributionally right — which connects straight to my spatial-entropy line.
