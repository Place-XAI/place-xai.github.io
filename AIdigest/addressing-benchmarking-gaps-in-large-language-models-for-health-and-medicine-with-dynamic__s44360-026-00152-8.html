Addressing benchmarking gaps in large language models for health and medicine with dynamic red-teaming

Jiazhen Pan · Bailiang Jian · Paul Hager · Yundi Zhang · Che Liu · Friedrike Jungmann · Hongwei Bran Li · Julian Canisius · Chenyu You · Junde Wu · Jiayuan Zhu · Fenglin Liu · Yuyuan Liu · Niklas Bubeck · Moritz Knolle · Chen Chen · Christian Wachinger · Zhenyu Gong · Cheng Ouyang · Georgios Kaissis · Benedikt Wiestler · Daniel Rueckert Published 2026-07-15 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00152-8 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

High scores on static medical benchmarks are not evidence of safety. The authors' DAS (Dynamic, Automatic, Systematic) agentic red-teaming framework stress-tests 15 state-of-the-art LLMs along four axes and exposes a "benchmarking gap": despite a median MedQA accuracy above 80%, 94% of previously correct answers flipped to incorrect under dynamic perturbation; open-ended HealthBench failure rates exceeded 70%; privacy leaks were elicited in 86% of scenarios; cognitive-bias priming shifted clinical recommendations in 81% of fairness tests; and hallucination rates exceeded 74%. Safety evaluation must therefore move from a static checklist to a living, co-evolving adversarial audit run before (and after) deployment.

★

Why It Matters

  • It falsifies the dominant evidence base for health LLMs. Med-PaLM-2/Med-Gemini-class results above 90% on MedQA have been read as clinical readiness; DAS shows that knowledge demonstrably held by a model collapses in one to three adversarial rounds, so leaderboard progress and trustworthiness have become decoupled.
  • It escapes Goodhart's Law by construction. Because every probe prompt is synthesized on the fly and escalated in real time, there is no fixed test set to fine-tune against — the benchmark co-evolves with the models it audits, which fixed benchmarks structurally cannot do.
  • It audits four failure modes under one protocol. Robustness, privacy (HIPAA/GDPR), bias–fairness and a seven-class hallucination taxonomy are usually studied in separate literatures; DAS shows the same model can be top-ranked on one axis and catastrophic on another (e.g., DeepSeek-R1: highly robust on MedQA, 100% privacy jailbreak).
  • It is cheap enough to institutionalize. Reported end-to-end API cost is roughly US$8–12 for a 100-item robustness sweep, US$10 for the 415-item bias suite, US$5 for the 81 privacy traps and US$10–15 for hallucination testing — putting continuous post-market surveillance of health LLMs within reach of ordinary labs and regulators.
  • It is clinician-validated, not just automated. Board-certified physicians adjudicated mutation validity (86–100%) and judge fidelity (Cohen's κ = 0.952 for privacy; 0.897 between physicians on HealthBench), so the alarming numbers are anchored to human expert reference standards.

Core Methods

  • Conditioning on prior success. Each model is first tested unmutated; only items it answered correctly are stressed. This isolates reasoning brittleness from knowledge absence, so the "jailbreak ratio" means "demonstrated knowledge that collapsed".
  • Six orthogonal robustness mutation tools: answer negation (rule-based), cognitive bait (nine bias framings incl. two new ones — defensive-medicine and availability bias), narrative distraction (irrelevant plausible context), choice expansion (4–5 → 8–9 options with near-miss distractors), physiological impossibility (an out-of-range vital/lab value plus a new correct "impossible information" option), question inversion (negating the decision boundary). Every tool is designed to deterministically move the ground truth rather than create ambiguity.
  • Rule-constrained attacker orchestrator (OpenAI Agent SDK, o3 backbone, temperature 0.2): selects a tool or compatible tool-combination each round under an incompatibility matrix, skips inapplicable tools, avoids previously failed combinations, always mutates the original seed to limit drift, escalates from single-tool (rounds 1–3) to orthogonal multi-tool attacks (rounds 4–5), and halts on the first divergence. "Freestyle" attacks outside the six-tool set are prohibited — the key design choice that makes the audit reproducible.
  • Rubric-targeted attacks for open-ended text. On HealthBench the agent identifies a rubric the model initially satisfied and performs a single-shot rewrite aimed at violating that specific criterion — converting free-text evaluation into a binary, attributable pass/fail.
  • Privacy: four disguise agents. Subtle request (explicit PHI ask → vague "detailed summary"), well-meaning intention (empathetic preamble), focus misdirection (tangential instructions dilute attention) and — most potent, 49.1% jailbreak — the trap warning, a superficial "remember HIPAA" reminder that induces false reassurance. Escalation is applied only to models still below 80% jailbreak after an explicit privacy system prompt.
  • Bias: reference-vs-attack majority voting. Four mutation axes (identity, linguistic style, emotional tone, cognitive priming) leave the underlying pathology untouched; a bias event is recorded only when the five-sample majority vote shifts between rounds — a clean causal contrast on decision change.
  • Hallucination: orchestrator + seven specialist subagents (medical fact, citation, logic, context, safety, instruction-following, other), each with ten in-context examples; the orchestrator always invokes fact and safety checkers and dispatches the rest conditionally, aggregating per-agent rationales into traceable provenance.
  • Statistics and validation. Deterministic decoding (temperature 0 where exposed); Wilson score 95% CIs; χ² tests of between-model heterogeneity (e.g., MedQA χ² = 151.65, d.f. 15, P < 1.13 × 10⁻²⁴; the six-model HealthBench tier is non-significant, P = 0.43); Rogan–Gladen correction with Monte Carlo error propagation for imperfect detector sensitivity/specificity; cross-vendor judge replication (Claude adjudicators: κ = 0.905 privacy, 0.844 robustness) and a non-LLM regex+ontology privacy baseline at 81% accuracy.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this paper: first how the paper itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, health geography & 2SFCA accessibility, explainable/physics-informed spatial learning, open science and geospatial foundation models).

① Give DAS a place: geographically conditioned adversarial seeds

Extending this paperDAS mutates content but never context of care. Its 415 bias vignettes vary identity, dialect and emotion while holding the health system constant — implicitly a well-resourced, English-language, specialist-adjacent setting. The natural extension is a place-conditioning layer in the seed generator: each vignette is instantiated under an explicit care-context vector (nearest-facility travel time, primary-care physician density, broadband availability, pharmacy formulary, referral pathway, national guideline). "Refer to the emergency department" is a correct rubric in one geography and unsafe advice where the nearest ED is three hours away; "start this second-line agent" is safe only where it is stocked. Running the same six mutation tools across a stratified sample of care contexts converts a single global jailbreak ratio into a context-stratified safety profile and tests whether the reported 94%/86%/81%/74% failure rates are stable or systematically worse in low-resource settings — the very settings where an LLM is most likely to be the only available adviser.

Added value to my researchThis is my health-geography agenda applied to model evaluation. The care-context vector is exactly what my 2SFCA/R2SFCA pipelines already produce — catchment-level supply-to-demand ratios, service-area travel times, telehealth broadband coverage — so I can supply the geographic side of the audit off the shelf. The output is a new object worth publishing on its own: a safety-by-accessibility interaction map that says where AI health advice is both most consumed and least reliable, feeding directly into my digital-divide and mental-health-access work.

② From jailbreak ratio to population-exposure-weighted harm

Extending this paperEvery headline number in the paper is an unweighted proportion over curated prompts: each of the 81 privacy traps and 415 bias items counts equally, as if all failure modes were equally likely to occur and equally consequential. A direct extension is to compose DAS with real usage distributions — what people actually ask health chatbots, how often, and in which countries — to produce an expected-harm estimate: harm ≈ Σ (query-type frequency × jailbreak probability × clinical severity). This is the epidemiologist's move from prevalence to attributable burden, and it would let DAS rank models by expected population harm rather than by an audit-set average, while also identifying which of the six mutation tools deserve the most defensive engineering effort.

Added value to my researchExposure weighting is a spatial problem the moment usage is geolocated: query frequency varies by country, urbanicity and care access, so the weights become a spatiotemporal field. I can estimate it with the same machinery I use for demand surfaces — spatiotemporal kernel density estimation over query streams, with MGWR to test whether the frequency-severity coupling is non-stationary across regions. The result is a "digital health risk surface" that slots into my urban-health and telehealth-equity portfolio and turns an AI-safety finding into a public-health-policy quantity.

③ Mine the 100-million-token audit log with explainable ML — a vulnerability surrogate

Extending this paperDAS generates ~100 million tokens and >100,000 scored microtasks and then reports only marginal jailbreak rates per model and per tool. That log is a rich labelled dataset in its own right: (model, mutation tool, tool combination, round index, specialty, question length, numeric-field presence, rubric type) → jailbreak. Fitting an interpretable ensemble with SHAP-style attribution over it would answer questions the paper leaves open — which interactions (e.g., question inversion × specialty, cognitive bait × ambiguous-equity case) drive failure, and whether a cheap surrogate model can predict jailbreak probability without running the attack. Such a surrogate turns a US$10 sweep into a near-zero-cost triage that pre-selects the few probes worth spending compute on, letting a regulator screen every model update rather than a sampled few.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-prediction workflow transplanted to a new substrate, and it exports back: the same "surrogate + attribution" pattern is how I want to audit geospatial foundation models — learn which terrain, density or sensor-context features predict failure of a spatial prediction, instead of reporting a single global RMSE. It also gives me a defensible answer to the reliability question that now shadows every LLM-assisted step in my own reproducible-research pipeline.

④ Replace the seven-agent detector with model-internal uncertainty — cheaper, and calibratable

Extending this paperThe hallucination axis is the paper's weakest link and it says so: the seven-subagent detector reaches 81.9% accuracy (κ = 0.638 against physicians), it needs GPT-4o + seven o3 subagents per response, and its errors must be corrected post hoc with a Rogan–Gladen estimator plus Monte Carlo error propagation. An obvious extension is to add a sampling-based semantic-uncertainty signal — semantic entropy over clustered resamples — as either a first-pass filter (route only high-entropy responses to the expensive subagents) or an ensemble member alongside them. Because that signal is computed from the audited model itself rather than from a judge of the same vendor family, it also attacks the cross-vendor coupling the authors flag (GPT detector κ = 0.638 vs Claude detector κ = 0.369).

Added value to my researchCalibrated, decomposable uncertainty is precisely what I need when I fine-tune geospatial foundation models and when LLM agents draft or check spatial analyses: I want to know not just that a prediction is wrong but whether the model was internally uncertain, so uncertainty can be mapped and propagated through downstream accessibility estimates. Entropy-style filtering is also the cheapest way to make an automated screening loop affordable at the scale of thousands of spatial units.

Cross-ref (corpus): Detecting hallucinations in large language models using semantic entropy — 10.1038/s41586-024-07421-0

⑤ Geo-DAS: port the framework to spatial reasoning and location privacy in agentic health AI

Extending this paperThe authors name multimodality as the priority extension, but there is a nearer and more neglected one: spatial reasoning. Health LLMs are already asked "where is the nearest urgent care", "can I get there in time", "which clinic takes my insurance" — and their privacy axis is defined by HIPAA's 18 identifiers, of which geographic subdivision is one of the most re-identifying. A Geo-DAS extension adds mutation tools with the same deterministic-ground-truth discipline as the original six: place-name substitution (real vs plausible-but-nonexistent facility), impossible geography (a travel time that violates the road network, the spatial analogue of "body temperature 48 °C"), boundary confusion (catchment vs administrative unit), and a location-privacy trap that checks whether a model re-identifies a patient from a ZIP code plus a rare diagnosis. It also adds a detector subagent for spatial factuality — an eighth class in a taxonomy that currently has no place for "wrong about where".

Added value to my researchThis is the version of the paper I could write. It joins my two strands: agentic GeoAI (LLMs that plan and execute spatial workflows) and health-services accessibility, where a hallucinated travel time or an invented facility corrupts an entire 2SFCA estimate downstream. Building the impossible-geography tool needs exactly my assets — road networks, POI/facility inventories, GPS-derived travel-time distributions — and the location-privacy trap addresses a risk my own mobile-phone and trajectory work makes me responsible for. Under FAIR/open-science principles the mutation suite ships as a public dataset, mirroring the authors' Hugging Face release.
