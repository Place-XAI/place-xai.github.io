An ECG biomarker for sudden cardiac death discovered with deep learning

Ziad Obermeyer · Alexander Schubert · James Ross · Sendhil Mullainathan · Markus Lingman Published 2026-06-24 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10674-6 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

A deep model trained on an entire Swedish region's ECGs linked to death certificates flags a high-risk group of 2.2% of the sample with a 7.0% annual rate of sudden cardiac death — higher than the 4.6% rate in patients with reduced LVEF (1.9% of the sample), and 86.1% of these high-risk patients are invisible to LVEF. The model transfers zero-shot to a US health system (VF/VT AUC 0.822) and a Taiwanese arrest registry (arrhythmic-arrest AUC 0.767, with a near-chance 0.582 "placebo" AUC for non-arrhythmic arrests). Pairing the classifier with a generative model of the ECG beat morphs real low-risk waveforms into counterfactual high-risk ones, exposing a previously undescribed, visually obvious feature — a slurred terminal R wave in lead aVL — that independently predicts sudden cardiac death and points to diffuse myocardial fibrosis as a candidate mechanism.

★

Why It Matters

  • It breaks a 40-year clinical deadlock. Defibrillators have existed since 1980, but LVEF — the only biomarker in wide use — misses most sudden cardiac deaths and sends two-thirds of implanted devices to patients who never receive a life-saving shock. This model finds a net-new high-risk population: only 13.9% of its high-risk group had known reduced LVEF.
  • It rescues the patients with "normal" hearts. Among those with a measured and normal LVEF — a group for whom no risk prediction currently exists — the model isolates patients at 6.4% annual risk, above the 4.6% seen in reduced-LVEF patients. In the whole cohort, 41.2% of sudden cardiac deaths had no obvious risk factor at the time of their ECG.
  • It uses the cheapest test in medicine. A standard 12-lead ECG costs a fraction of cMRI, electrophysiology studies or genetic profiling — the modalities that dominate current SCD research — making population-scale screening plausible rather than aspirational.
  • Suggestive evidence of preventability, not just prediction. High-risk patients who happened to have defibrillators died 54.4% less than their predicted rate; the same regression framework "rediscovers" the known 50–88% trial effect in reduced-LVEF patients, a face-validity check that most prediction papers never attempt.
  • It turns a black box into a hypothesis. Rather than stopping at a saliency map, the generative morphing pipeline produces a human-legible waveform feature, which the authors then tie to electrophysiological first principles (wavefront scattering by diffuse fibrosis) and test against blinded cMRI review. This is machine learning used as a discovery instrument, not just a classifier.

Why Nature (Editorial Fit)

  • A new biological finding, not a new benchmark. The headline is a previously undescribed biomarker and a mechanistic hypothesis about sudden cardiac death — Nature rewards the discovery, and the AI is the microscope.
  • Methodological credibility that is unusually hard-won. A pre-specified 40% data lockbox, untouched from model development through peer review and opened only at provisional acceptance, plus a "placebo AUC" specificity test in Taiwan — the study design anticipates the standard objections to clinical ML.
  • Generalization across three continents, two vendors and two sampling rates. Zero-shot transfer from Philips-derived Swedish training data to GE MUSE data in the USA and Taiwan, and from death-certificate labels to VF/VT codes to adjudicated arrests, addresses the field's central worry about brittle medical AI.
  • Immediate translational stakes. The results define a concrete candidate population for a randomized defibrillator trial — the paper explicitly frames this as the next step.
  • A transferable AI-for-science template. "Predictive model + generative model → counterfactual instance → first-principles reasoning" is a recipe other fields can copy, which is exactly the kind of general contribution Nature's main journal prizes.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Training population
(Sweden)
All 441,614 ECGs performed 2010–2016 in Region Halland, a public regional health system in Sweden (12 patients opted out), linked to death certificates and complete electronic health records via the regional healthcare information platform. ECGs sampled at 500 Hz, retrieved as XML from a Philips IntelliSpace system. Ethics: Lund University review board, protocol 2016/517 (amendment 2024-02316-02).
Split & lockbox
Patient-level random split fixed before any analysis: 40% lockbox = 179,060 ECGs / 51,481 patients, untouched until provisional acceptance. Final model retrained on the accessed 60% = 262,554 ECGs / 75,157 patients. Main results are computed in the under-80 lockbox subset: 119,541 ECGs / 35,885 patients with one-year follow-up, and specifically the 113,072 ECGs / 35,417 patients without defibrillators. Censoring: 6,446 of 125,987 lockbox ECGs (12,969 of 247,286 in training) excluded from outcome metrics.
US validation
Dandelion Health cohort drawn from a large non-academic US health system (Sharp HealthCare, San Diego, CA): 251,858 ECGs performed in 2021–2022 across 139,613 patients under 80. GE MUSE storage; 83.3% at 500 Hz, 16.7% at 250 Hz (linearly interpolated to 500 Hz). Outcome available: VF/VT diagnoses only (cause of death not linkable).
Taiwan validation
National Taiwan University Hospital emergency-department registry, 2011–2019, distributed through Nightingale Open Science; Utstein-style reporting template. 4,268 patients under 80: 257 cardiac arrests + 4,011 random ED controls; detailed case review adjudicated 96 arrests (37%) as arrhythmic. Median ECG-to-ED interval 391 days (IQR 74–2,189). GE MUSE, 500 Hz.
Outcome definition
Primary label = death certificate indicating (i) cardiac or ill-defined cause and (ii) death outside hospital or within the first 24 h of admission, in the year after the ECG. Complemented by VF/VT diagnoses in EHRs (Sweden, USA) and adjudicated arrest cause (Taiwan).
Base rates
Sudden cardiac death in the year after an ECG: 0.6% overall in the lockbox; median follow-up 2,010 days. Of these deaths, 43.9% had LVEF measured premortem and 36.4% of measured LVEFs were ≤35%; 41.2% had no coronary disease, prior MI, heart failure or prior ventricular arrhythmia. Analyses focus on patients under 80 — 74.6% of all ECGs and 25.7% of all sudden cardiac deaths. US VF/VT base rate: 3.8%.
Availability
Deliberately uneven, and the paper says so. Swedish data and the Swedish-derived model weights are accessible only through a direct collaboration agreement with Region Halland (GDPR constraint). Dandelion Health data require a research partnership or the company's standard access pathway. Taiwanese data are freely available from Nightingale Open Science. All non-model code, including the morphing pipeline, is public.
Not reported
No patient-level geography, socioeconomic status, or residential context is used or released; the number of ECGs per patient, and the exact ICD-implantation counts in each cohort, are not stated in the main text (Table 1 summarizes the lockbox). We do not invent these numbers here.

Core Methods

  • Predictive architecture: a 64-layer ResNet (32 residual blocks; 2 convolutional layers per block, 128 filters, kernel size 16) over raw 12-lead waveforms, selected after benchmarking CNNs, transformers and LSTMs. Sanity-checked against published benchmarks for QRS/QTc intervals, atrial fibrillation, age and sex.
  • Multitask training objective: (i) sudden cardiac death over several horizons in the whole cohort; (ii) among those who died within a year, SCD versus other causes; (iii) reduced LVEF (≤35%) among those with measured LVEF. Predictions are then logit-calibrated on under-80 patients in the Swedish training set.
  • Analysis at the ECG level, not one ECG per patient, with standard errors clustered by patient — avoiding the survivorship bias that "most recent ECG" selection would introduce. All tests two-sided.
  • Threshold selection from trial ethics, not from statistics: the high-risk cut-off is the largest group whose lower 95% bound exceeds the median SCD rate in defibrillator-trial control arms (4.9%) — an explicitly decision-theoretic choice, yielding the 2.2% group at 7.0% annual risk (95% CI 4.9–9.5%).
  • Multi-definition outcome validation: death certificates (Sweden) → documented VF/VT (Sweden and USA) → chart-adjudicated arrest cause (Taiwan), plus a placebo AUC on non-arrhythmic arrests to prove specificity (0.582, significantly worse than 0.767, P < 0.001).
  • Observational treatment-effect probe: OLS of SCD (and all-cause mortality) on age, sex, a high-risk indicator, a defibrillator indicator and their interaction; coefficients read as absolute probability changes. Face validity established by replicating the known reduced-LVEF effect (67.5% reduction, within the 50–88% trial range).
  • Generative "morphing" for interpretability: a 512-dimensional VAE trained on single beats segmented from 10-s ECGs (NeuroKit2). Starting from 56 sampled real low-risk beats, the latent vector is repeatedly perturbed along the gradient of predicted risk and decoded — up to 2,000 iterations or until the synthetic beat reaches the 90th risk percentile — producing patient-specific counterfactual high-risk waveforms that hold non-risk variation fixed.
  • Quantifying the discovered feature: mean absolute first and second differences of aVL amplitude from R-peak to QRS end, regressed on SCD with and without conventional quantitative-ECG controls (rate, intervals, axes, QRS duration). Effect magnitude is comparable, in s.d. units, to left axis deviation — and additive to it.
  • Mechanistic follow-up: single-lead models (AUCs nearly matching the 12-lead model) suggest a diffuse myocardial process; blinded cMRI review found higher prevalence of subtle, diffuse late gadolinium enhancement in the riskiest 10%, supporting fibrosis-driven wavefront scattering as the candidate mechanism.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this paper: first how the paper itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, health geography & 2SFCA/R2SFCA accessibility, explainable & physics-informed spatial learning, facility-location optimization, open/FAIR reproducibility).

① The label is a health-system artifact: make ascertainment spatially explicit

Extending this paperThe paper reports one of the most instructive numbers in clinical ML almost in passing: conditional on model-predicted risk, US patients are six times more likely to be coded with VF/VT than Swedish patients, and the authors explicitly attribute this to measurement intensity and financial incentives — the same reason the US AUC (0.822) beats the Swedish one (0.717). The model's apparent performance is therefore partly a map of who gets tested, monitored and coded. A direct extension: treat ascertainment as a latent, place-varying process — model P(outcome coded | outcome occurred, site, catchment) with site- and region-level covariates (monitoring intensity, ED density, insurance mix, distance to cardiology services), and re-estimate PPV curves under corrected labels. Because the paper already spans three health systems with radically different measurement regimes, it is an unusually good testbed for a label-noise model whose nuisance parameter is geography of care, not random noise.

Added value to my researchThis is my telehealth/digital-divide and spatial-accessibility agenda expressed as a machine-learning nuisance term. Concretely: use R2SFCA access surfaces to primary and specialty care as covariates in a label-ascertainment correction, so that "this area has low observed disease" is decomposed into low incidence versus low detection. That single move upgrades every spatial disease-prediction model I build — the residual of a health-outcome model becomes interpretable as an access residual, and I get a principled way to compare risk scores across regions with unequal service provision.

② From "who is high risk" to "where, and can they reach a defibrillator?"

Extending this paperThe paper produces an actionable screening output — 2.2% of ECGs at 7.0% annual risk — and then stops at the patient level. But acting on it requires capacity that is spatially distributed: electrophysiology labs, implanting centres, outpatient monitoring, and, for the interim period before implantation, EMS response and public AED coverage (the paper's own case study describes a woman who died while waiting for a scheduled outpatient defibrillator). The natural extension is to join model-derived risk density to service geography and solve a joint problem: where to screen (which clinics' ECG streams to score first), where to expand implantation capacity, and how to route the newly flagged. Because risk here is generated by a model rather than observed, the demand surface is itself a prediction with uncertainty — a genuinely new twist on facility-location.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct transplant of my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization into cardiac care, with a harder demand model: predicted-risk density replaces observed demand, and the objective becomes expected deaths averted per implantation slot subject to an equity floor across catchments. It also gives my 2SFCA family a new supply type — procedural capacity with a waiting queue — where the distance-decay function must encode time-to-procedure, not just travel time.

③ Generalize the "morph" operator: counterfactual generation as a discovery instrument for space

Extending this paperThe paper's most portable contribution is not the biomarker but the protocol: train a risk model, train a generative model of the same signal, then walk the generative latent space along the risk gradient until a human can see what the classifier sees. Two extensions the paper leaves open. First, stability: the morphs come from 56 sampled beats and a single VAE, and the authors concede this analysis was run on the pre-lockbox data because it was computationally expensive — an ensemble of generators and predictors, with a formal test of which morph features recur across seeds, would separate discovery from artefact. Second, closing the loop with mechanism: the paper proposes linking the generator to computational electrophysiology models so a morph becomes an inferred parameter change (fibrosis density, conduction velocity) rather than a picture — turning an interpretability tool into simulation-based inference.

Added value to my researchI want this operator for space. My explainable-ML work leans on SHAP/PDP over engineered features, which can only rank inputs I already chose. Instead: train a generative model of the built environment (street-network patch, POI configuration, morphological signature), train a spatial risk model (accessibility deficit, mental-health outcome, crash risk), then morph a real low-risk neighbourhood into its counterfactual high-risk twin and look at what changed. That produces candidate urban-form biomarkers rather than attributions — a genuinely generative XAI for GeoAI, and one whose outputs a planner can read.

Cross-ref (corpus): Causal inference and counterfactual prediction in machine learning for actionable healthcare — 10.1038/s42256-020-0197-y

④ Fuse the waveform latent with place: is the residual risk spatially structured?

Extending this paperThe model sees only the myocardium's electrical signature. Yet sudden cardiac death is strongly patterned by exposure and deprivation — air pollution, heat, food environment, neighbourhood disadvantage — none of which enter the model, and 41.2% of the deaths carried no conventional clinical risk factor at all. An extension with real scientific payoff: join the ECG risk score (or, better, the penultimate-layer latent) to a residential place-based determinants index and ask two questions the paper cannot. Does place add independent predictive signal beyond the waveform, in the way LVEF was shown to (co-flagged patients reached 10.7%)? And is the model's error spatially autocorrelated — i.e. does the biomarker mean the same thing in a deprived neighbourhood as in an affluent one? Region Halland's population coverage makes this feasible in principle within a single health system.

Added value to my researchThis is my core spatiotemporal toolkit applied to a state-of-the-art clinical model: MGWR / spatially varying coefficients on the risk-score residual, spatiotemporal KDE of high-risk ECG clusters, and spatial-entropy measures of how concentrated the flagged population is. The deliverable is a general diagnostic I have wanted for years — a spatial audit protocol for clinical AI, reporting whether a deployed risk model's calibration drifts across neighbourhoods, which is the geographic analogue of the fairness audits the field already accepts.

⑤ The lockbox and the locked weights: a reproducibility template, and its unfinished half

Extending this paperThe 40% lockbox — created before any modelling, untouched through peer review, opened only at provisional acceptance, with the AUC moving 0.837 → 0.872 on retraining — is the most rigorous overfitting safeguard I have seen in an applied deep-learning paper, and it should be named and standardized. But the paper's other half is a reproducibility dead end: under GDPR, the Swedish-trained weights themselves can only be obtained through a collaboration agreement, so no one can independently re-run the headline model. The obvious extension is a federated / privacy-preserving successor: a multi-site ECG foundation model pretrained self-supervised across health systems, with site-local calibration heads and only gradients or distilled surrogates crossing borders — plus a public benchmark built on the openly available Nightingale Taiwan data so that external claims become checkable rather than merely stated.

Added value to my researchTwo things I can adopt immediately. (1) The lockbox becomes my pre-registration protocol for spatial prediction: hold out entire regions or time windows before modelling, since spatial autocorrelation makes random k-fold optimistic in exactly the way random ECG splits would have been here. (2) The federated-foundation-model design is the governance pattern my geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning work needs, where mobile-phone, trajectory and health data are similarly non-shareable — I get to keep the FAIR/open-science commitment (open code, open benchmark, open protocol) even when the primary data can never leave its jurisdiction.
