Empowering biomedical evidence exploration and synthesis with deep knowledge graph research

Zifeng Wang · Zheng Chen · Ziwei Yang · Xuan Wang · Qiao Jin · Yifan Peng · Zhiyong Lu · Jimeng Sun Published 2026-07-02 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01266-0 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

DeepEvidence is a deep research agent that searches, explores and aggregates evidence across heterogeneous biomedical knowledge bases and literature by coordinating multiple agents that combine breadth-first and depth-first research strategies. Crucially, it incrementally builds an evidence graph of key entities and observations, so every conclusion can be tracked, attributed and validated rather than taken on trust. It substantially outperforms generic AI agents on four open benchmarks, and on seven newly established benchmark tasks spanning drug discovery, preclinical experimentation, clinical trial development and evidence-based medicine. The message: long-horizon, graph-grounded agents — not one-shot retrieval — are what make automated evidence synthesis trustworthy.

Why It Matters

  • Makes the reasoning trace a first-class object. Most retrieval-augmented systems return an answer plus a bag of citations. DeepEvidence returns an evidence graph — entities and observations accumulated during the search — which turns "trust the model" into "audit the path". That is the difference between a demo and a tool a reviewer can accept.
  • Search strategy, not just search. Explicitly combining breadth-first (survey the landscape) and depth-first (chase a mechanism to its source) exploration is a statement that evidence synthesis is a planning problem. This is the part generic chat agents do worst.
  • Heterogeneous sources, not one corpus. The system reasons across structured knowledge bases (pathways, targets, ontologies, chemistry, trials, drug labels) and free-text literature simultaneously — the actual condition of biomedical evidence, where no single index contains the answer.
  • Ships benchmarks, not only a model. Seven new tasks aligned to real stages of the discovery pipeline (target identification → mechanistic reasoning → trial development → EBM) give the field an evaluation spine that mirrors how research actually progresses, with test data released publicly.
  • Transferable beyond biomedicine. The architecture — planner agents + heterogeneous source adapters + an incrementally grown, attributable evidence graph — is domain-agnostic. Any field where evidence is scattered across gazetteers, registries, and papers (urban health, environmental epidemiology, policy) can adopt it.

Core Methods

  • Deep research agent, not a single-pass RAG: the system performs longer-horizon reasoning and repeated literature exploration across many steps, in explicit contrast to generic AI agents that answer in one retrieval round.
  • Coordinated multi-agent collaboration: several specialized agents divide the work of searching, exploring and aggregating evidence, rather than one monolithic prompt loop.
  • Breadth-first × depth-first strategy mixing: BFS sweeps the space of candidate entities/hypotheses; DFS drills into a specific chain of evidence. Combining them is the paper's central algorithmic claim about how to explore evidence.
  • Incremental evidence-graph construction: key entities and observations are added to a graph as the search proceeds, giving a persistent, inspectable state that supports tracking, attribution and validation of the research trajectory — the mechanism behind the paper's transparency claim.
  • Heterogeneous-source aggregation: structured knowledge bases (pathways, targets, chemistry, trials, ontologies) and unstructured literature are queried and fused within the same reasoning process, with programmatic access mediated by biomedical data APIs.
  • Two-tier evaluation: (i) head-to-head against generic AI agents on four established open benchmarks, (ii) against seven purpose-built tasks organized along the discovery pipeline, evaluated per-stage in Figs. 2–5.
  • What is not visible from the open text: the exact agent role taxonomy, the graph schema, the stopping criterion for exploration, and the verification/self-critique procedure. These sit in Methods and Supplementary Tables 1–11.

① Give the evidence graph a geography: a place-aware DeepEvidence

Extending this paperDeepEvidence's evidence graph is built from entities and observations — genes, targets, drugs, trials — with no spatial or population dimension. Yet half of its sources are inherently placed: ClinicalTrials.gov records carry site coordinates, openFDA adverse-event reports carry geography, and the epidemiological literature it reads is about populations somewhere. A direct extension: add a place node type and a cohort/exposure node type to the graph schema, so an evidence chain can read compound → target → trial → site → catchment population. This lets the agent answer the question its current schema structurally cannot: where does this evidence hold, and for whom? — surfacing external-validity gaps (e.g. a mechanism validated only in trials clustered in three metropolitan regions) as a graph property rather than a caveat a human must remember to add.

Added value to my researchThis is the architecture I want for health-geography evidence synthesis. My accessibility work (2SFCA/R2SFCA, telehealth divides, mental-health service areas) is bottlenecked by exactly this: evidence about "what works" is scattered across health-services journals, facility registries and census geographies that no single index joins. A place-typed evidence graph is a literal implementation of the space–human coupling I study — and it turns a systematic-review process that currently takes months into an auditable, re-runnable pipeline that carries its provenance with it.

② Make the BFS/DFS switch a learned, explainable policy — and audit it

Extending this paperThe paper's central algorithmic claim is that combining breadth-first and depth-first exploration beats either alone — but the combination is presented as a design choice, not as something diagnosed. Extending it: instrument the agent so that every BFS↔DFS transition is logged against the evidence-graph state at that moment (frontier entropy, source disagreement, citation redundancy, novelty of the last observation), then fit an explainable model over those features to answer when depth pays and when it wastes budget. The output would be a compute-allocation law for deep research agents — "go deep when source disagreement exceeds X" — plus an ablation that finally isolates how much of the reported gain comes from strategy mixing rather than from having more tools.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML methodology transplanted from spatial prediction to agent behaviour: SHAP-style attribution over a decision trace instead of over a raster. The same instrument-then-attribute pattern is what I use to ask which spatial covariates actually drive a model's accessibility prediction — and it gives me a principled way to budget compute when I run these agents at scale over hundreds of city-level literature queries rather than one.

③ Close the loop: from evidence synthesis to evidence generation

Extending this paperDeepEvidence stops at synthesis — it tells you what the literature and knowledge bases support. The obvious next move, and one the corpus already points at, is to let the evidence graph propose and then execute the analysis that would resolve its own open edges. Where the graph contains a contradiction or a dangling hypothesis, the agent should emit a testable specification (dataset, cohort, estimand, confounders) and hand it to an analysis agent — closing the loop from reading to computing. The evidence graph is unusually well suited to this because it already encodes why a question is open, which is precisely the context an autonomous analysis agent otherwise lacks.

Added value to my researchFor me this becomes a GeoAI research loop: an agent that reads the accessibility literature, notices that the distance-decay function for mental-health services has never been estimated for rural US counties, then specifies and runs the R2SFCA + MGWR analysis against my POI, mobile-phone and census layers. That is agent-assisted spatial discovery, with the evidence graph serving as the memory that keeps a long multi-stage geospatial workflow honest and reproducible.

④ Stress-test the evidence graph: adversarial and equity red-teaming

Extending this paperThe paper's benchmarks measure whether the agent gets things right. They do not measure what it does when the evidence base is skewed, retracted, or adversarial — a preprint later withdrawn, an industry-funded trial over-represented, or a disease whose literature is concentrated in high-income settings. Extending it: build a dynamic red-teaming layer over the seven tasks that perturbs the source pool (inject retracted papers, mask a knowledge base, resample the literature to alter its geographic composition) and measure how the evidence graph responds. A trustworthy system should show its conclusion degrading visibly — thinner support, flagged provenance — not silently maintaining confidence.

Added value to my researchThis maps straight onto my health-equity concerns: an evidence agent that inherits the geographic bias of the literature will confidently recommend interventions validated only in well-studied urban cores, reproducing the digital and service divides I spend my time measuring. A perturbation protocol that reports which populations the evidence actually covers is a reusable equity audit — for AI evidence tools and for my own spatial models alike.

Cross-ref (corpus): Addressing benchmarking gaps in large language models for health and medicine with dynamic red-teaming — 10.1038/s44360-026-00152-8

⑤ Treat the evidence graph as a FAIR research object, not a scratchpad

Extending this paperThe paper releases test data and code, but the evidence graph itself — the artefact that makes the work distinctive — has no stated schema, identifier scheme or export format. Extending it: specify the graph as a versioned, citable research object with persistent identifiers on every node (DOI, PMID, trial NCT, UniProt/ChEBI accession), signed provenance on every edge, and a diff mechanism so a query re-run six months later shows exactly which evidence changed. That would make an agent's synthesis reviewable in the same way a dataset is — and would let independent groups merge graphs instead of each rebuilding one.

Added value to my researchThis is my open-science / FAIR agenda given a concrete target. The same versioned-graph discipline is what my multi-source fusion work needs, where a result depends on a particular vintage of OSM, a particular census release and a particular mobility panel — a signed provenance graph turns "reproducible in principle" into "re-runnable with a diff". It is also the natural substrate for fine-tuning a geospatial foundation model on attributed rather than scraped evidence.
