Inequities in geographic access to emergency obstetric and newborn care in Greater Accra

Kerry L. M. Wong · Winfred Dotse-Gborgbortsi · Duah Dwomoh · Cephas Avoka · Shravya Shetty · Pratibha Sriram · Gautam Prasad · Malcolm Kane · Mark Young · Charlotte Stanton · Aduragbemi Banke-Thomas Published 2026-07-22 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00479-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across all 29 districts of Greater Accra, the median travel time to the nearest public comprehensive EmONC facility was 29.7 min under peak traffic — sitting right on the 30-min clinical danger threshold. Aggregate coverage looks reassuring (≈90% of women aged 15–49 within 30 min of public care, 93% including private), but that average hides a sharp wealth gradient: median travel time falls from 40.3 min in the poorest quintile to 15.4 min in the wealthiest (Pearson r = −0.48). Traffic itself is a driver of inequity — 5.7% of women had at least one reachable public facility off-peak but none during peak hours, and peri-urban districts such as Ga South, Shai Osudoku and Ningo/Prampram bear almost all of the deficit.

★

Why It Matters

  • Access is time-varying, not a fixed map property. The paper shows that the same woman at the same address can have one reachable public comprehensive EmONC facility at 2 a.m. and zero at 7 p.m. Static accessibility maps — the standard product of health-geography analysis — structurally overstate coverage in congested African cities.
  • It quantifies who is left behind, in people not percentages. 92,952 women (4.6%) of childbearing age were both in the lower-wealth tercile (RWI Q1–Q3) and more than 30 min from public comprehensive EmONC under peak traffic — a directly actionable planning number for the Ghana Health Service.
  • It shows the private sector does not fix peri-urban gaps. Adding 31 private facilities to the 28 public ones raises citywide coverage only from ~90% to ~93%, and in Ga South Municipal it changes nothing (still zero reachable facilities within 30 min) — because private supply concentrates where wealth already is.
  • Urban proximity ≠ urban advantage. Greater Accra is >90% urban with ~2,000 health facilities, yet the "urban health advantage" evaporates in the peri-urban fringe where population growth outruns EmONC capacity. This directly challenges the rural-focused framing of access-to-care policy in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • The 30-min threshold is clinically, not conventionally, chosen. The authors anchor on ≤30 min because emerging evidence links longer referral travel to elevated odds of stillbirth and maternal death in urban LMIC settings — making the metric a proxy for survival, not convenience.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Study extent
Greater Accra Region (GAR), Ghana — 29 districts, 3,245 km² (Ghana's smallest region by area, most populous by headcount). Region boundary as defined by Ghana's Ministry of Local Government and Rural Development. GAR population 5.5 million (2021 census, 17.7% of Ghana), >90% classified urban; density rose 1,236 → 1,681 persons/km² between 2010 and 2021.
Health facilities
59 comprehensive EmONC facilities — 28 public + 31 private. Screened from Ghana Health Service routine data as facilities averaging ≥3 cesarean births per month in 2023, then surveyed in person in June–July 2024 with the UNFPA/AMDD Rapid EmONC Assessment Tool (2018); retained if open 24 h/day and able to perform cesarean section (proxy for comprehensive EmONC), validated against Ghana Health Service technical staff. GAR overall holds close to 2,000 health facilities (Centre for Health Information Management), so the 59 are the emergency-capable subset. Full facility list in Supplementary Data 1.
Population
WorldPop constrained gridded population, 100 m × 100 m, 2020 — women aged 15–49 ("WoCBA"). Study-area total 2.0 million women aged 15–49. WorldPop disaggregates census counts/projections with land cover, roads and night-time lights via Random Forest, constrained to building footprints and calibrated to UN Population Division 2020 estimates with census-derived age–sex multipliers.
Wealth
Meta Relative Wealth Index (RWI) at 2.4 km × 2.4 km micro-regions, ML-derived from satellite imagery, mobile-phone networks, topography and de-identified connectivity data (Chi et al., PNAS 2022). Used as within-country quintiles Q1 (least wealthy) → Q5.
Travel time
Google Maps Platform Directions API — motorized driving time from each grid-cell centroid to nearest / 2nd / 3rd nearest facility. Underpinned by a graph-neural-network ETA model (Derrow-Pinion et al., CIKM 2021) fusing crowdsourced GPS traffic, 2–4-week historic patterns, authoritative speed-limit/restriction data and live driver feedback. Two traffic scenarios: peak = weekdays 18:00–20:00, non-peak = weekends 01:00–03:00.
Analysis grid
S2 level-14 cells (~600 m × 600 m) tiling all of GAR; the 100 m population raster is intersected with S2 cells and apportioned by area of intersection. The exact number of S2 cells is not stated in the text.

Number of S2 cells and number of API queries are not reported. Only motorized travel is modeled, although GAR women split ~54% motorized / 46% non-motorized for routine care — walking/ambulance access is therefore out of scope. Core Methods

  • Facility ground-truthing before geography. Rather than trusting a facility registry, the team screened on a functional criterion (≥3 cesareans/month in 2023), then field-surveyed every eligible facility with the UNFPA Rapid EmONC tool for signal functions, staffing, equipment and essential medicines — accessibility is computed only to facilities verified as actually capable and open 24/7.
  • Cesarean capacity as the comprehensive-EmONC proxy, matching the 15-Nigerian-cities protocol, with a validation pass against Ghana Health Service technical staff to bound misclassification in both directions.
  • S2 discrete global grid as the computational unit. Level-14 S2 cells give near-equal-area tiles on the sphere (~600 m), chosen as an explicit accuracy/compute trade-off; population is areally apportioned from the finer 100 m WorldPop raster into these cells.
  • Congestion-aware routing instead of friction surfaces. Origin–destination driving times come from the Google Maps Directions API's GNN-based ETA model, evaluated separately for a worst-case (weekday evening) and best-case (weekend small hours) traffic state — turning traffic into an experimental factor rather than a nuisance.
  • Two indicator families, three thresholds, three ownership regimes. (i) travel time to the 1st/2nd/3rd nearest facility — capturing referral fallback depth, not just nearest-facility proximity; (ii) count of unique facilities reachable within 15/30/60 min — capturing choice and surge capacity. Both computed for public, private, and public+private, under both traffic scenarios.
  • Population-weighted coverage aggregation. District statistics are median travel time over encapsulated S2 cells plus the share and absolute number of women aged 15–49 under each threshold, summing affected cells to headcounts.
  • Socio-spatial equity layer. S2 cells binned into RWI quintiles; quintile-specific median travel times, a loess trend at cell level, and Pearson correlation with two-sided 95% CI. The authors explicitly caveat that spatial autocorrelation makes those CIs anti-conservative — an unusually honest treatment of pseudo-replication.
  • Peak-vs-non-peak differencing as the inequity mechanism. The novel analytic move: identify women whose reachable facility count crosses from ≥1 to 0 between traffic scenarios, isolating a population whose access is destroyed by congestion rather than by distance.

① From two traffic snapshots to a continuous 24×7 accessibility field

Extending this paperThe paper's most original finding — 5.7% of women lose all reachable public EmONC when traffic peaks — rests on exactly two sampled traffic states (weekday 18–20h, weekend 01–03h). Obstetric emergencies do not schedule themselves into those windows. The natural extension is a continuous accessibility tensor A(cell, facility, hour-of-week, season): sample the Directions API on a temporal design (or emulate it with a learned surrogate to avoid quota cost), then report an expected coverage weighted by the empirical hour-of-day distribution of labor onset and referral departure. That converts a binary peak/non-peak contrast into a probability that a randomly timed emergency finds zero reachable facilities — a far stronger policy statistic, and one that could reveal that the true "access cliff" population is well above 5.7% once weekday mornings, market days and rainy-season flooding are included. A surrogate ETA model trained on the sampled API responses would also free the design from proprietary query limits.

Added value to my researchThis is the missing temporal axis in my 2SFCA/R2SFCA work: catchments are almost always computed on a single static impedance matrix. A time-resolved R2SFCA where both the distance-decay and the catchment boundary breathe with the hour of week would let me re-express telehealth substitution as "hours per week during which physical primary care is unreachable but virtual care is not" — turning the digital-divide question into a directly comparable temporal accessibility deficit. My spatiotemporal KDE toolkit is the natural estimator for smoothing that tensor.

② Close the loop: proximity → competition → realized utilization

Extending this paperThe paper measures supply-side proximity only. It counts how many of the 59 facilities a cell can reach, but never asks how many other cells are reaching the same facility, nor whether the facility can absorb them — even though the survey collected exactly the staffing, equipment and medicine data needed to build a capacity weight. Extending it: turn the reachable-facility counts into a capacity-constrained, congestion-aware R2SFCA, where each facility's supply is its surveyed cesarean throughput, demand is the WorldPop women-15–49 surface scaled by district-specific fertility, and the impedance is the paper's own peak/non-peak travel-time matrix. The prediction is sharp and testable: several central districts that look richly served (Okaikwei North reaching 18 facilities within 30 min) may show worse supply-to-demand ratios than the count suggests, while Ga South's zero stays zero. Validating the resulting scores against Ghana Health Service facility-birth records would close the proximity → utilization loop that the paper explicitly leaves open.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean, high-stakes test bed for my R2SFCA line in a setting where the outcome variable is mortality rather than convenience. Crucially it gives me a rare case where facility capacity is ground-surveyed rather than proxied by bed counts — letting me quantify how much the usual capacity proxy distorts 2SFCA scores, a methodological result transferable straight back to my US primary-care and mental-health accessibility studies.

③ Explain the wealth gradient instead of only reporting it

Extending this paperThe paper's equity finding is a single global number: r = −0.48 between RWI and travel time, with the authors themselves warning that spatial autocorrelation makes the CI anti-conservative. That correlation is descriptive and spatially stationary — it cannot say where wealth matters most or why. Extending it: fit MGWR on the S2 grid with covariates for RWI, road density, built-up/informality index, distance to arterial corridors and population density, so each driver gets its own bandwidth; then run an explainable ML ensemble (SHAP) on the same features to separate "poor because far" from "far because poor". This directly tests the paper's own hypothesis that peri-urban land-use and road-network deficits, not household wealth per se, are the mechanism — and replaces one global correlation with a district-level attribution map that Ghana Health Service can act on. Coupling to a deprivation/informality layer would identify whether the deficit tracks informal settlement growth specifically.

Added value to my researchSquarely my MGWR + explainable-ML methodological core, applied where the standard critique of GWR (unstable coefficients on autocorrelated data) can be stress-tested against an honest, pre-declared autocorrelation caveat. It also gives me a Global South counterpart to my US health-accessibility models, testing whether the spatial non-stationarity structures I find in American metros survive translation to a rapidly urbanizing African region.

④ Turn the diagnosis into a siting-and-referral optimization

Extending this paperThe paper ends at diagnosis — it names Ga South, Shai Osudoku and Ningo/Prampram as deficit districts but never computes what would fix them. Extending it: pose an explicit multi-objective facility-location problem over the GAR road network — minimize population-weighted peak travel time (efficiency), minimize the Q1–Q3 vs Q4–Q5 travel-time gap (equity), and maximize robustness to the failure or closure of any single facility (resilience) — with decision variables spanning three realistic levers: upgrading an existing basic facility to cesarean capability, siting a new comprehensive facility, or pre-positioning ambulances at congestion chokepoints. Because the paper already publishes the travel-time geodatabase on figshare, the Pareto frontier is computable today. The interesting question is whether the cheapest lever for the 92,952 underserved women is a new hospital at all, or upgrading two peri-urban facilities plus timed ambulance staging — a result that would reframe Ghana's infrastructure-heavy policy discourse.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct transplant of my EV charging-station efficiency-vs-equity optimization into a life-critical health domain, with one added axis (resilience) and a far more legible objective. The formulation — discrete siting on a network under a learned, time-varying impedance — is identical; only the loss function's units change from kWh to expected maternal deaths averted, which makes it a strong demonstration that the method generalizes beyond mobility infrastructure.

⑤ A transferable EmONC-accessibility foundation model for African conurbations

Extending this paperAccra now joins Lagos and 14 other Nigerian cities, Grand Nokoué and Grand Conakry as one-off, hand-built accessibility studies — each re-running a bespoke pipeline and each dependent on paid, rate-limited Google Maps queries that most African health ministries cannot afford at scale. The decisive extension is to stop building one city at a time: fine-tune a geospatial foundation model on the pooled figshare travel-time geodatabases from these studies, using satellite imagery, OSM road topology, WorldPop density and RWI as inputs and the API-derived peak/non-peak travel-time surface as the label. That yields a zero-API accessibility emulator — a city that has never bought a single Directions API call could obtain a first-order, congestion-aware EmONC accessibility map, with the physical constraint that travel time along a path is monotone and satisfies the triangle inequality baked in as a soft physics prior on the routing graph. The paper's Accra database becomes the held-out validation city.

Added value to my researchThis unites three of my threads at once — fine-tuning geospatial foundation models, physics-informed graph learning, and open science/FAIR: the deliverable is not a paper but a reusable, auditable public good for LMIC health planning, trained entirely on openly deposited derivatives. It also gives me a principled answer to a question my accessibility work keeps hitting — how far a distance-decay/impedance representation learned in one urban system transfers to another — measured here across cities that differ radically in road structure and congestion regime.
