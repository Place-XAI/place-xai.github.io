Intercity inequality in carbon emission reductions from vehicle electrification in China

Jianing Liu · Longfei Zheng · Huibin Du · Zizheng Liu · Peng Zhang · Fenjie Long · Pengjun Zhao · Priti Parikh · Zhifu Mi Published 2026-06-24 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00465-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Plug-in electric vehicles (PEVs) do not just cut carbon — they relocate it. Tracing over 245 million vehicle registration records through plant-level grid operations across 285 Chinese prefecture-level cities, the study shows that in 2020 the most developed city group exported 41.8% of its PEV-related fuel-cycle emissions to less developed cities, so that per-PEV emissions in the receiving groups exceeded those of comparable conventional vehicles by 16.9%–52.0%. Roughly 136 cities became net carbon importers, and under a full fuel-cycle accounting the top 20 cities capture 80% of national net reductions (versus 40 cities under on-road accounting alone). This intercity inequality (Gini of net reductions) stays elevated through 2030 as PEV stocks diverge, then declines only as the grid decarbonizes — with carbon-importing cities needing on average US$2.54 billion per year in compensation from 2025–2060 under the net-zero pathway.

★

Why It Matters

  • It breaks the "EV = local climate win" accounting habit. Because PEV operational emissions migrate from the tailpipe to the power plant, the city that adopts is not the city that emits. The paper makes this spatial decoupling measurable rather than rhetorical, at the exact administrative unit (prefecture-level city) where China's carbon targets are actually implemented.
  • Electrification can locally increase emissions. High-GDP (62.7%) and upper-middle-GDP (38.0%) cities gain large reductions, middle-GDP cities gain little (7.5%), while lower-middle-GDP (+52.0%) and low-GDP (+16.9%) cities end up with per-PEV emissions above conventional vehicles once transferred grid emissions are attributed to them.
  • Accounting boundary changes the equity verdict. Pump-to-wheel accounting suggests reductions of 69.5%–100% and a broad distribution of benefit; full fuel-cycle accounting collapses that to 40.0%–57.7% and concentrates benefit in ~20 cities. The same policy looks progressive or regressive depending on the boundary drawn.
  • It puts a price on the injustice. The imported CO2 burden grows from 1.6 Mt (2020) to 78.8–92.1 Mt by 2040; monetized at scenario carbon prices, carbon-importing cities need an average US$2.54 bn yr-1 (peak US$4.77 bn in 2055, ≈6.2% of China's 2024 energy-conservation and environmental-protection expenditure) — a concrete fiscal transfer design problem, not an abstract fairness claim.
  • Carbon quotas are anchored to stale baselines. Because responsibility allocation uses historical stationary baselines, a technology-driven emission surge in power-exporting cities is a systemic blind spot — directly threatening SDG 1 and SDG 10 while national mitigation "succeeds".

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Vehicle fleet panel
Over 245 million vehicle registration records for China, 2005–2021, from the Ministry of Transport of China, giving annual/monthly city-level registrations with vehicle model and energy type (BEV, PHEV, HEV, ICEV). Combined with historical total vehicle stock published by prefecture-level statistical bureaus.
Spatial scope
285 Chinese prefecture-level cities with available 2020 GDP per capita — the consistent analytical sample used across Figs. 3–5, split into 5 quintile groups (low → high GDP per capita) and, for the carbon-cost heat map, into 40 evenly sized quantile groups.
Time window
Historical base year 2020 (registration data 2005–2021); scenario projections to 2060, with inequality reported through the 2030 peak and post-2040 decline.
Power-plant data
China Emissions Accounts for Power Plants (CEAP) and the Global Coal Plant Tracker supply 2020 plant-level operational and emissions data; plant geolocations are compiled to build within-province, city-level emission-distribution weights (share of a city's fossil power-plant CO2 in its province).
Electricity transfers
Provincial electricity transmission for 2020 from the Annual Compilation of Power Industry Statistics (China Electricity Council); interprovincial transfer shares estimated with a quasi input–output model (Supplementary Note 2).
Travel activity
City-level annual vehicle kilometres travelled (AVKT) from the China Household Finance Survey 2019 (CHFS 2019); provincial averages substituted where city coverage is insufficient. The exact number of cities requiring this substitution is not stated in the main text.
Socioeconomic
China City Statistical Yearbook — 2020 GDP per capita and other socioeconomic covariates used for city grouping and for propensity-score matching of Bass diffusion parameters (income, population, land area, mean temperature, mean slope).
Future power system
SWITCH-China generator-level capacity-expansion and hourly-dispatch model supplies future plant-level generation/CO2 and interprovincial transmission under three scenarios (BAU, DNNE, NZE); carbon caps and carbon prices in Supplementary Tables 13–14.
Emission factors
GREET.Net model (2023 version), re-parameterized for China: fuel carbon contents, material acquisition (steel, aluminium, copper), annual fuel economy, curb mass and mass distribution, and the consumption-based electricity mix. Representative vehicles: sedan 1.38 t curb weight / 160,165 mi lifetime, SUV 1.59 t / 183,363 mi.
Adoption forecast inputs
IEA Global EV Outlook 2024 for future BEV/PHEV share of new registrations; saturation benchmark of 400 vehicles per 1,000 people; assumed average SUV sales share 28.5%.
Availability
Compiled data and code on Code Ocean (10.24433/CO.7306366.v1); detailed registration records only from the corresponding authors on reasonable request. Source Data XLSX files accompany Figs. 1–5. Basemaps from Tianditu (GS(2024)0650); maps made in ArcGIS 10.5.

Core Methods

  • Fleet reconstruction with Weibull survival: city-level stock by energy type is rebuilt as stockt-1 + registrations − scrappage, with vehicle-type-specific scrapping rates — ICEV/HEV (scale 14.05, shape 4.5), BEV (7.91, 1.3), PHEV (11.80, 3.0). Sedans and SUVs are not differentiated in scrappage (a stated data limitation).
  • Bass diffusion forecasting with propensity-score transfer: innovation (p) and imitation (q) coefficients are fitted for the 10 largest PEV markets in 2020 (Chengdu, Shenzhen, Zhengzhou, Guangzhou, Tianjin, Beijing, Weifang, Shanghai, Hangzhou, Liuzhou; stocks 38,000–280,000), then transferred to immature cities by propensity-score matching on income, population, area, temperature and slope. Fitted R2 > 0.95 for most cities.
  • Life-cycle emission accounting (GREET, China-recalibrated): life-cycle CO2 = well-to-pump + pump-to-wheel + vehicle cycle. The fuel cycle alone accounts for 76.4%–94.3% of the total, justifying the paper's fuel-cycle focus.
  • Plant-level carbon transfer model: a city-to-city transfer matrix Ta,b,t distributes each city's PEV well-to-pump emissions to generating cities via (i) an interprovincial share estimated from a quasi input–output model of the transmission network and (ii) an intraprovincial weight equal to city b's share of provincial fossil power-plant CO2. Emissions "after transfer" = before transfer + transferred in − transferred out.
  • Net emission reduction and Pareto concentration: NetER = fuel-cycle savings from displaced ICEVs (excluding the 6.9% foreign WTP share) − PEV emissions after transfer; Pareto curves then quantify how few cities capture 80% of the national reduction under on-road versus fuel-cycle boundaries.
  • Gini with negative values: inequality of NetER across cities is measured with a Gini variant that handles negative observations consistently while preserving [0,1] normalization — necessary because many cities have negative net reductions.
  • Scenario design (SWITCH-China): BAU (2025 carbon intensity frozen, 14th Five-Year-Plan minimum non-fossil build-out), NZE (front-loaded, peak ~2030, neutrality by 2060 with CCS), DNNE (back-loaded, peak ~2030, neutrality after 2060 with negative-emission technologies). Model extensions: 2020 plant inventory + geolocation, PEV charging demand added to regional load, national power-sector CO2 caps, provincial non-fossil capacity targets.
  • Carbon-cost redistribution: net imported emissions × annual scenario carbon price gives the compensation each importing city would require, mapped onto 40 GDP-per-capita quantile groups held fixed at 2020 membership for comparability.
  • Uncertainty: differentiated city-specific PEV saturation levels (which widen inequality, so the baseline is conservative); reduced-form energy-price semi-elasticities (shift 2060 national PEV stock by only 1.5%–2.1%); and Monte Carlo propagation of four uncertainties — city SUV shares, PHEV charge-depleting vs charge-sustaining behaviour, future BEV/PHEV shares and AVKT change — with narrow 95% CIs on the inequality metrics.

① Replace annual average-emission attribution with hourly, congestion-aware marginal attribution

Extending this paperThe transfer matrix Ta,b,t is annual and average-based: interprovincial shares come from a quasi input–output model and intraprovincial shares from each city's annual fraction of provincial fossil-plant CO2. But charging is a temporal act — a Shenzhen PEV plugged in at 21:00 is served by a different marginal unit than one at 13:00, and transmission corridors bind. A direct extension: keep the paper's city resolution but drive the attribution with hourly SWITCH-China dispatch and shadow prices on transmission constraints, so that transfers are computed from marginal rather than average emission factors and congestion rents are visible. This would test whether the 41.8% export share is an artifact of annual averaging, and whether the 136 net-importing cities change identity under congestion — plausibly turning the paper's static inequality into a diurnal one that charging policy can actually move.

Added value to my researchThis is a natural home for my spatiotemporal kernel density and physics-informed graph learning agenda: the transmission network becomes a graph on which flow conservation and capacity limits are hard physical priors, and the learned operator maps (city charging load profile, grid state) → attributed emissions. It also upgrades my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization from a purely siting problem to a joint siting-and-scheduling problem, where the equity objective is the intercity carbon burden the paper quantifies rather than local access alone.

② Carry the transfer framework from CO2 to exposure, mortality and environmental justice

Extending this paperThe Discussion itself flags the obvious next step — extending the plant-level tracing to SO2 and PM2.5. That extension is far more than a pollutant swap: CO2 is well-mixed and its "burden" is an accounting construct, whereas co-pollutants create a physically located health burden. Coupling the paper's city-to-city transfer matrix to atmospheric dispersion and exposure-response functions would convert the carbon inequality result into an attributable mortality and morbidity transfer: developed cities that gain clean air by adopting PEVs simultaneously export premature deaths to coal-exporting cities. The paper's own five city groups already supply the equity stratification; adding a population-weighted exposure layer would make the environmental-justice claim causal rather than analogical, and would let one ask whether the health transfer peaks earlier or later than the 2030 carbon-inequality peak.

Added value to my researchThis lands squarely in my health geography line. The exported-exposure surface becomes a demand-side covariate in 2SFCA/R2SFCA analyses: cities absorbing transferred generation face rising respiratory and cardiovascular demand precisely where primary-care and mental-health supply is thinnest, so I can build a "burden-versus-capacity" index that overlays imported pollution with service-area accessibility. It also gives my healthy-cities work a rare instance where an urban policy's health cost is spatially separable from its beneficiary — an ideal test case for spatial-lag and MGWR specifications of policy spillover.

Cross-ref (corpus): Reduced urban air pollution and mortality from the transition to new energy vehicles in China — 10.1038/s44360-026-00120-2

③ Endogenize adoption: from an exogenous Bass curve to an infrastructure- and reliability-aware diffusion model

Extending this paperPEV stock trajectories are forecast with a Bass diffusion model whose p and q are borrowed from ten mature cities via propensity-score matching, with a uniform saturation benchmark of 400 vehicles per 1,000 people. This makes adoption exogenous to everything the paper cares about: charging-station density, grid reliability, electricity price and the local carbon intensity that determines whether a PEV even helps. A strong extension replaces the Bass curve with a spatially explicit, explainable ML adoption model whose features include charging-point supply, outage frequency, residential parking availability and local generation mix, allowing feedback — cities with unreliable, coal-heavy grids adopt more slowly, which further concentrates PEV stock and widens the very inequality the paper measures. The paper's own uncertainty analysis hints at this: differentiated saturation levels already amplify inequality, so the baseline is conservative by construction.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct application of my explainable machine learning for spatial prediction work — SHAP/GWR-hybrid attribution over city-level adoption would show which local conditions gate diffusion and where the marginal charger buys the most adoption. It also feeds my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization with an empirically grounded demand response curve instead of an assumed saturation constant, and connects to my multi-source data fusion practice (POIs for charger locations, mobile-phone and trajectory data for actual VKT, replacing the survey-based AVKT the paper must rely on).

Cross-ref (corpus): Power supply disruptions deter electric vehicle adoption in cities in China — 10.1038/s41467-024-50447-1

④ Go beyond a single Gini: decompose the carbon-transfer network into who exports to whom

Extending this paperInequality is summarized by one Gini coefficient per year per scenario (with a negative-value correction). A scalar cannot distinguish "a few extreme exporters" from "a broad gradient", nor tell a governor of Hebei whether her burden comes from Beijing or from Shandong. The paper already computes the full city-by-city matrix Ta,b,t — so the extension is nearly free: treat it as a directed weighted carbon-transfer network, apply community detection to recover de facto "carbon regions", and decompose inequality into within-community versus between-community components (Theil) alongside spatial-entropy and polarization measures. That would reveal whether intercity carbon injustice is an agglomeration-internal phenomenon (core cities exporting to their own periphery, a governance problem solvable within a metropolitan compact) or a long-haul interprovincial one requiring national fiscal transfer — a distinction the Gini erases entirely.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly my network community detection + spatial entropy toolkit applied to a new flow type. My work on urban agglomeration and regional collaboration and on job–housing networks uses the same machinery on commuting flows; carbon-transfer flows are a structurally analogous directed network, so the community partitions can be compared directly — do carbon-transfer communities coincide with commuting or economic-collaboration communities? A mismatch would be a genuinely novel finding: the regions we govern together are not the regions we pollute together.

Cross-ref (corpus): Measuring inequality beyond the Gini coefficient may clarify conflicting findings — 10.1038/s41562-022-01430-7

⑤ Turn the US$2.54 bn compensation figure into a designed, agent-based transfer mechanism

Extending this paperThe paper computes the compensation that carbon-importing cities would need (net imported emissions × carbon price) but stops short of asking whether any mechanism could deliver it. That is the decisive policy gap: exporting cities have no incentive to pay, quotas are anchored to stationary historical baselines, and the transfer is projected to peak at US$4.77 bn in 2055. An extension would model city governments as strategic agents — each choosing PEV subsidy intensity, charging build-out and grid-decarbonization investment under a national cap — and compare candidate institutions: consumption-based quota reallocation, an intercity carbon-payment market, earmarked renewable-build subsidies for exporting cities, or simply re-baselining quotas on transfer-adjusted emissions. Monte Carlo over carbon-price paths and adoption trajectories would then identify which mechanism is robust rather than merely optimal, converting the paper's diagnosis into an implementable design.

Added value to my researchDirect use of my agent-based modeling and Monte Carlo methods on a spatially explicit policy problem, and an extension of my urban agglomeration and regional collaboration line into fiscal-institution design. It is also a strong open-science/FAIR vehicle: because the authors released compiled data and code on Code Ocean, the mechanism layer can be built as a reproducible, versioned module on top of their published pipeline — exactly the "publish the extension, not just the paper" pattern I want to standardize in my group.
