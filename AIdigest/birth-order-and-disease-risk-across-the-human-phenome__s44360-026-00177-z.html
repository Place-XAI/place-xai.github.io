Birth order and disease risk across the human phenome

Benjamin Kramer · Steven A. Kushner · Andrey Rzhetsky Published 2026-08-03 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00177-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Birth order is associated with disease risk across nearly the entire clinical phenome, not just the handful of conditions studied before. Scanning 569 ICD-defined diseases in 10.27 million siblings from 5.13 million two-child families, 150 of 418 testable diseases reach Bonferroni significance. First-borns carry excess risk for neurodevelopmental and immune-allergic conditions (other/unspecified PDD code group OR = 0.57, autism OR = 0.74, food allergy OR = 0.80); second-borns for substance abuse (OR = 1.19), gastrointestinal and infectious conditions (herpes zoster OR = 1.35). A between-family matched scan and an internally controlled within-family sibling contrast agree (r = 0.66; 84.7% of significant hits directionally concordant), and effects survive state fixed effects, full-sibling restriction, stricter clinical rematching and healthcare-use adjustment — though effect sizes are modest (median OR ≈ 0.89 / 1.10).

★

Why It Matters

  • Turns a century-old, fragmented literature into one atlas. Birth-order research since Galton and Adler has been disease-by-disease; this is the first phenome-wide scan, so effect sizes across organ systems finally become directly comparable on a single scale.
  • A universal exposure with population-scale consequences. Individual ORs are modest, but every person has a birth order — modest relative risks applied to an entire pediatric population translate into nontrivial absolute burden, and to screening-prioritization value for clinicians and family counselling.
  • Design template for non-randomizable familial exposures. Pairing a high-powered between-family matched scan with a within-family sibling contrast (plus prespecified positive/negative controls) gives a reusable triangulation recipe for any exposure that cannot be assigned — birth order, birth spacing, parental age, sibship size.
  • Directly confronts the standard critiques. Reproductive stoppage, differential healthcare use, parental-age confounding, age–period–cohort entanglement and geographic confounding are each tested rather than hand-waved: e.g. first-borns average only 24.0 vs 22.9 claim days (4.5% difference), and visit-adjusted ORs correlate at r = 0.99 with unadjusted ones.
  • Reveals directional structure, not noise. The near-symmetric 79:71 split between first-born and second-born excess (binomial P = 0.568) is itself the strongest argument against blanket ascertainment bias — a surveillance artifact would skew toward first-borns.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Primary source
Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims and Encounters (CCAE), annual releases 2003–2024 — inpatient, outpatient and pharmacy claims covering approximately 200 million unique covered lives in US employer-sponsored plans. Raw releases are SAS7BDAT files; the team built extracted databases of demographics (sex, birth year, enrollment family ID), enrollment histories and ICD-9-CM/ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes.
Screening funnel
66,054,423 family identifiers contained ≥1 age-window candidate; 27,975,854 families had ≥2 valid-sex, valid-birth-year candidate children; after parent inference and eligibility filters, 5,135,006 two-child families = 10,270,012 individuals.
Analytic cohorts
Between-family matched scan: 1,616,881 matched sibling pairs (3,233,762 individuals). Stricter clinical rematch: 529,760 pairs (1,059,520 individuals). Within-family sibling comparison: 5,135,006 families, one pair each. Reproductive-stoppage analysis: 10,016,101 complete-case families (not restricted to two-child families).
Phenotypes
569 diseases defined by ICD-9-CM/ICD-10-CM code groupings adapted from an established claims phenotyping system (Jia et al., Nat. Comput. Sci. 2023). 418 met the ≥500-case threshold (between-family); 541 met the ≥100 discordant-sibling-pair threshold (within-family). Diseases were assigned to 17 clinical domains fixed before analysis. Parental psychiatric history used a curated list of 302 ICD-9-CM + 252 ICD-10-CM psychiatric codes.
Time / cohort span
Claims 2003–2024; children born 1978–2013; age at last observation ≥12 years; ≥365 days of enrollment visibility per child.
Geography
Individuals carry a three-digit ZIP code (ZIP3), mapped to a dominant county FIPS via the HUD USPS ZIP–TRACT crosswalk (residential-ratio weighted); county population from the US Census Bureau Vintage 2023 file co-est2023-alldata.csv supplied census region (NE/MW/S/W) and the urbanization tertile. Standard errors clustered at county level; a separate specification adds 50 state indicators + DC.
Software stack
Cohort extraction in Python 3.11 (pandas, sqlite3, multiprocessing); statistics in R 4.3 (survival, sandwich, lmtest); figures in matplotlib 3.8; matching ran on 45 parallel workers with fixed seed 2025.
Availability
Individual-level data are NOT public. MarketScan is proprietary and licensed; access requires a data use agreement executed directly with Merative — the authors state they held no privileged access. Summary-level results are in Supplementary Tables 1–15 plus a source data file; all analysis code is open at github.com/benjaminkramer510/BirthOrderPhenome2026.
Not reported
The paper does not report a count of distinct ZIP3s/counties represented, nor per-state sample sizes, nor a race/ethnicity breakdown of the analytic cohort (it only notes that MarketScan enrollees are predominantly working-age, higher-income and disproportionately White). No exact figure is given for the number of families dropped at each filter beyond the counts above.

Core Methods

  • Family reconstruction from claims: parents inferred as the youngest male and female family members ≥15 years older than the oldest candidate child, with age at youngest child's birth in 18–69 years; exactly two remaining children required (applied before individual eligibility filters, so families with a third ineligible child are not misclassified as two-child families).
  • Between-family matched cohort (high-powered scan): greedy nearest-neighbour caliper matching pairing a first-born from family A with a second-born from family B — exact on sex, birth year, census direction and urbanization tertile; calipers on follow-up (±50 days), paternal age (±10 y), maternal age (±10 y), sibling gap (±2 y); distance = 2.0·|Δdays| + 1.0·|Δfather age| + 1.0·|Δmother age| + 0.5·|Δgap|. Residual parental-age SMDs (0.30 / 0.36) are handled as categorical regression covariates rather than by matching alone.
  • Within-family sibling contrast (internally controlled): conditional logistic regression (clogit) stratified on family ID, absorbing everything shared by siblings — parental genetics, household SES, geography, family health attitudes — powered only by disease-discordant pairs. Four age–period–cohort parameterizations fitted; cohort + calendar period is primary.
  • Phenome-wide inference: weighted logistic regression per disease on collapsed covariate-pattern frequency tables, county-clustered HC1 robust SEs; Bonferroni (P < 1.20 × 10⁻⁴ for 418 tests) plus Benjamini–Hochberg FDR q < 0.05. Poisson log-link and complementary log–log refits were direction-concordant for all 418 diseases.
  • Robustness battery: state fixed effects (r > 0.99), full-sibling restriction (r = 0.998), stricter clinical rematch with ±1-year parental-age calipers and exact Charlson matching (r = 0.93), and a visit-count-adjusted refit (r = 0.99) — seven specifications summarized in one heatmap.
  • Domain-level pooling: normal–normal empirical-Bayes partial-pooling meta-analysis over disease-level log-ORs and CI-derived SEs, fitted by maximum likelihood (scipy.optimize), estimating global mean, between-domain and within-domain heterogeneity, and shrunken domain posteriors.
  • Effect-modification analysis: birth-order × sibling-gap interactions across all 418 diseases (127, or 30.4%, Bonferroni-significant), targeted interactions with sex, paternal age, maternal age and calendar period, plus an exploratory gradient-boosted T-learner producing standardized family-level second-born − first-born risk differences (explicitly framed as modifier discovery, not individualized causal prediction).
  • Bias probes: prespecified 5 positive controls (allergic rhinitis, food allergy, asthma, acne, substance abuse) and 7 negative controls (type 1 diabetes, cystic fibrosis, Addison disease, Ehlers–Danlos, Turner syndrome, acute sinusitis, acute URI); plus family-level logistic models of reproductive stoppage (autism OR = 0.870 for having a second child; ADHD OR = 1.011; PDD code group OR = 1.056 — opposite to the stoppage prediction).

① Give the phenome-wide atlas a geography: spatially varying birth-order effects

Extending this paperGeography enters this study only as nuisance — census direction, an urbanization tertile, county-clustered standard errors and a state fixed-effects check that the authors read as reassuring (r > 0.99, "geographical confounding has a negligible influence"). But a near-perfect global correlation is precisely what you would see if strong local heterogeneity averaged out nationally. The direct extension: refit the 150 Bonferroni-significant diseases as spatially varying coefficient models (MGWR, or a spatial random-slope Bayesian hierarchy on the ZIP3→county geography the paper already built via the HUD crosswalk), asking whether the first-born autism excess is uniform or concentrated where developmental-behavioural specialists are dense, and whether the second-born herpes-zoster excess tracks household crowding. The paper's own data structure supports this at no extra licensing cost — it simply never asked the question spatially.

Added value to my researchThis converts a national epidemiological result into exactly the object my GeoAI–health-geography line consumes: a map of coefficient surfaces, not a scalar OR. It also gives me a rare validation target for MGWR at a sample size (1.6M matched pairs) where bandwidth selection is usually infeasible, and a clean template for adding family-structure covariates to my urban-health models, where birth order has never been a candidate predictor.

② Replace the scalar visit-count control with a spatial-accessibility control (2SFCA-adjusted phenotyping)

Extending this paperThe paper's strongest threat is diagnostic ascertainment, and its defence is a single scalar: first-borns average 24.0 distinct claim days vs 22.9 for second-borns, and refitting with log(visits) barely moves the estimates (r = 0.99) — though the authors honestly note the autism OR shifts 0.799 → 0.880, i.e. visits behave partly as a mediator. A visit count, however, conflates propensity to seek care with the opportunity to receive it. Extension: build 2SFCA / R2SFCA supply-accessibility scores to paediatricians, developmental-behavioural specialists and allergists at the ZIP3 level, enter them as an effect modifier, and test whether the first-born neurodevelopmental excess narrows in high-access areas (biology-dominant) or widens (surveillance-dominant). This is a falsifiable discriminator between the paper's two competing interpretations that the current design cannot make.

Added value to my researchThis is the sharpest use of accessibility metrics I have seen available: 2SFCA stops being a descriptive equity index and becomes an identification instrument — a measured confounder in a causal design. If it works here, the same construction ports to every claims-based prediction model I build, and gives my telehealth/digital-divide work a mechanism for quantifying diagnostic rather than merely service inequity.

Cross-ref (corpus): Access to care affects electronic health record reliability and AI-driven disease prediction — 10.1038/s44360-026-00054-9

③ Make the sibling-gap dose–response an environmental experiment

Extending this paperThe gap × birth-order interaction is the paper's most mechanistically loaded result — 127 of 418 diseases (30.4%) show Bonferroni-significant modification, allergic rhinitis attenuates monotonically with wider spacing (consistent with the microbial "old-friends" framing) while autism shows a U-shape peaking at 4–6 years. Yet the gap is treated as a purely family-internal quantity. Extension: interact sibling spacing with place-based exposure fields the family actually lived in — residential density, greenspace, daycare supply, ambient PM2.5, and household crowding proxies — to test whether close spacing confers allergy protection only where the microbial-sharing environment is rich. That would upgrade the paper's "consistent with" language into a genuine gene-free mechanistic test, and would explain the U-shape as an exposure-window interaction rather than an unexplained curve.

Added value to my researchSpacing × environment is a natural spatiotemporal interaction problem: my kernel-density and spatiotemporal-exposure machinery can reconstruct the early-life exposure window for each sibling separately, since the two siblings share a household but not a calendar. That gives me a within-household natural experiment for greenness and air-quality effects with the household confounders differenced out — a design I have wanted for the urban-nature/mental-health line.

④ Upgrade the exploratory T-learner into an explainable, spatially aware heterogeneity engine

Extending this paperThe authors fit a gradient-boosted T-learner for just seven high-priority diseases and explicitly label it exploratory modifier discovery, declining causal forests or BART because birth order has no propensity mechanism. That caution is right, but it leaves the heterogeneity layer thin: the modifier set is family-structural (gap, sex composition, parental age, comorbidity indices) with geography reduced to region and urbanization. Extension: scale the T-learner to all 418 diseases, add the county-level covariates the paper already has the crosswalk for, and attach SHAP / accumulated-local-effects attribution so the modifier discovery is auditable rather than a black box — then stress-test stability across states, since a modifier that flips sign across regions is a confounding signature, not an effect.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction agenda applied to a causal contrast rather than a prediction target. The concrete deliverable is a disease × modifier attribution matrix over 418 phenotypes — the kind of high-dimensional, spatially indexed output where my ensemble + attribution stack earns its keep, and where stability-across-geography becomes an honest proxy for transportability.

⑤ Test transportability beyond the employer-insured frame — and into the telehealth era

Extending this paperThe authors name the limitation plainly: MarketScan captures employer-insured, predominantly working-age, higher-income, disproportionately White enrollees, and the two-child-family restriction (chosen for clean first/second-born identification) introduces its own selection. They call for replication in Nordic registries or Medicaid. A sharper extension is to make transportability estimable rather than aspirational: reweight the 418 disease-level estimates by post-stratification to a national target population, and add the axis the 2003–2024 window makes newly available — the encounter channel. Telehealth restructures who gets diagnosed with what and when; if the first-born diagnostic advantage is partly a surveillance effect, it should compress in the post-2020 telehealth-heavy strata, providing an internal natural experiment inside the paper's own calendar-period variable.

Added value to my researchThis lands squarely on my telehealth and intercity-healthcare-system work: birth order becomes a test probe for diagnostic equity across care channels and city roles. If second-borns are systematically under-diagnosed for neurodevelopmental conditions in low-access regions and telehealth narrows that gap, the effect is measurable with the same intercity-flow framework I already use for telemedicine and specialist referral networks — turning a family-structure epidemiology result into a health-system accessibility indicator.
