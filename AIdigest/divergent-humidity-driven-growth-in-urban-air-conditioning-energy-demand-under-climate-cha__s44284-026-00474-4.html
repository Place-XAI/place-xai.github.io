Divergent humidity-driven growth in urban air-conditioning energy demand under climate change

Xinchang ‘Cathy’ Li · Lei Zhao · Zhiwen Luo · Keith Oleson · Yifan Cheng · Xiaoxiong Xie · Alvin C. G. Varquez · Mitsuna Sekiya Published 2026-07-24 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00474-4 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Coupling a newly built dehumidification scheme in the CESM urban building energy model with a global Earth system simulation, the authors project that urban dehumidification (latent heat load) energy demand rises 47% globally by 2090–2099 under SSP3-7.0 (0.15 → 0.22 W m−2 summer mean). Crucially, its share of total AC demand diverges spatially — rising in 59% of global urban area and falling in 36% — because cities warm and (de)humidify at different rates, so any projection extrapolated from today's temperature–energy relationship will be wrong in one direction or the other. Humidity also makes AC demand 2–7× more temperature-sensitive on humid days, and drives 2–3× higher demand on mildly hot but very humid days — demand spikes that temperature-only forecasts simply cannot see.

★

Why It Matters

  • Breaks the temperature-only paradigm of energy projection. Global cooling-demand projections almost universally use dry-bulb temperature or cooling degree days. This paper shows the humidity term embedded in those historical relationships is non-stationary: as latent heat load fraction shifts, extrapolation over- or under-estimates demand depending on the local competition between warming and humidification.
  • Energy extremes need not coincide with climate extremes. The largest humidity-driven surcharge occurs in the 27–31 °C band, not at the hottest temperatures — so grids planned around temperature extremes and buildings designed with Extreme Meteorological Years will be blindsided by mild-but-muggy load spikes.
  • The burden lands on the global south. Latent heat load already dominates AC demand (fraction up to 94%) between 30° N and 30° S, exactly where urbanization, AC adoption and economic growth are fastest — while it is under 8% in mid-to-high northern latitudes where most design standards were written.
  • Reconciles a chaotic literature. Local studies had reported humidity effects on building energy anywhere from a few percent to >96% (Extended Data Table 1). A single globally coherent, physics-based model gives the first comparable, cross-city baseline.
  • A reusable model capability, not just a result. The dehumidification parameterization is merged into the CLMU/CTSM code base and released openly, so latent heat load becomes a standard diagnostic for future Earth-system climate-risk work.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Nature of the data
This is a simulation-generated dataset, not an observational compilation. The "data" are outputs of a global land-only CESM2 / CTSM run with the CLMU5 single-layer urban canopy model and its building energy model (BEM), extended here with a new dehumidification scheme.
Spatial coverage
Global, all urban land units at 0.9375° latitude × 1.25° longitude (~100 km). Note honestly: the paper reports no headline count of cities — analysis is per urban grid cell, and named cities (Hubli, São Paulo, Phoenix, Miami, Yangon, Sincelejo, Dubai, Bologna) are illustrative diagnostics, not a sample frame.
Time period
Projection run 2015–2099; analysis contrasts the first decade (2015–2024) with the last decade (2090–2099), using daily means of urban 2-m temperature, RH, specific humidity and sensible/latent AC energy flux. Validation runs cover 2000–2014.
Scenario & forcing
SSP3-7.0 (regional rivalry, high emissions). Atmospheric forcing for the projection comes from a fully coupled CESM2 simulation; validation runs are forced by GSWP3 (3-hourly, 0.5°). Non-climatic factors (AC adoption rate, technology, urban extent, building stock) are held at present-day levels to isolate the climate signal.
Urban surface input
U-Surf — a global 1 km spatially continuous urban surface property dataset (morphology, radiative and thermal properties), aggregated to model resolution; chosen because it reduces anthropogenic-heat overestimation relative to the CLMU default dataset.
Validation data
Gridded global 1 km hourly anthropogenic heat flux (Varquez et al., Sci. Data 2021) for 15 countries/regions where validation data exist; plus published local BEM case studies for latent heat load fraction (Extended Data Table 2). AC adoption rates come from the explicit adoption parameterization of Li et al. (2024) — e.g., 16% Brazil, 5% India at present day.
Model settings
Indoor setpoints: temperature 27 °C, relative humidity 65% (per ASHRAE 62.1); infiltration 0.3 ACH; BEM time step 30 min; single thermal zone with infinite-capacity HVAC; vapor-compression dehumidification assumed; condensate routed to urban runoff (adds <0.004% flow to the world's 50 largest rivers).

CESM simulation results on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.20563953); source data provided with the paper; model code on GitHub (CTSM, dehumidification branch). Open access (CC BY 4.0).

Core Methods

  • New dehumidification parameterization in CLMU: the original BEM assumed completely dry indoor air. The authors add humid-air density and specific heat, track indoor specific humidity, and compute latent heat load as the energy to condense excess moisture, F_lat = (H·ρ_humid·l_v/Δt)·(q_iB − q_set), triggered only when the temperature setpoint is also exceeded (mirroring real thermostat-driven AC control).
  • Setpoint approach: RH setpoint of 65% converted per time step to a specific-humidity threshold from indoor temperature and pressure; sensible and latent components solved separately, then both scaled by the grid cell's AC adoption rate.
  • Water conservation: the removed condensate is drained to urban surface runoff and passed to the CTSM river model, closing the water budget rather than discarding moisture.
  • Two-stage validation: (1) spatially explicit comparison of simulated AC-driven anthropogenic heat flux against an observation-based global AHF product in 15 countries/regions, with a "no-dehumidification" control run as the counterfactual; (2) comparison of simulated latent heat load fractions with detailed local BEM case studies.
  • Diagnostic metric — latent heat load fraction: latent ÷ total AC demand (%). Because both terms are scaled by the same AC adoption rate, the ratio is independent of AC adoption, so conclusions survive future adoption growth — an elegant way to isolate climate from socioeconomics.
  • Sensitivity analysis: daily QC–Tu sensitivity computed as daily mean demand divided by the temperature excess over the decade's cooling-onset minimum, then stratified by RH to expose exponential growth with humidity.
  • Controlled humid/non-humid contrast: demand on humid (RH > 70%) versus non-humid (RH ≤ 70%) days is compared within the same 1 °C temperature bin, holding sensible load roughly constant so the humidity effect is cleanly identified.
  • Design-space diagnosis: joint Tu–RH density distributions for six contrasting cities reveal the mechanisms (warming past the AC-trigger threshold vs. genuine humidification vs. drying) behind each city's shift.

① Downscale the latent heat load field from ~100 km to the neighborhood

Extending this paperThe paper's decisive quantity — the latent heat load fraction — is produced on a 0.9375° × 1.25° grid with a single "average building" per urban land unit. That is coarser than any city it names: Hubli, Miami and Sincelejo are each one pixel, so the intra-urban contrast between a dense impervious core and a vegetated periphery (precisely the contrast that governs local moisture availability) is averaged away. A direct extension: train a generative, uncertainty-aware downscaling emulator that maps (coarse Tu, RH, sensible/latent split) + 1 km U-Surf morphology + satellite LST and vegetation fraction → sub-kilometre latent and sensible load fields, with a calibrated spread rather than a point estimate. The paper already carries the 1 km surface dataset as input, so the conditioning covariates exist; what is missing is the learned super-resolution operator, and its uncertainty is scientifically as valuable as its mean because the water cycle is the least certain part of the projection.

Added value to my researchThis is a textbook target for fine-tuning a geospatial foundation model: a physics-generated coarse field plus multi-source urban covariates, with a clean physical consistency constraint (downscaled latent + sensible must aggregate back to the parent cell). Downstream it hands my health-geography work something it has never had — a neighborhood-scale dehumidification burden surface that can be intersected with housing quality, income and cooling-facility access, and interrogated with explainable ML (SHAP over morphology variables) to say which urban form features buy or cost dehumidification energy.

② Turn one climate realization into a robustness map of "who must design for dehumidification"

Extending this paperThe headline divergence — 59% of urban area up, 36% down in latent heat load fraction — comes from a single SSP3-7.0 realization of a single Earth system model, and the authors themselves flag that water-cycle projections are far less certain than temperature projections. The obvious and important extension is a multi-model, multi-member ensemble run of the same dehumidification scheme, then a re-framing of the result from a deterministic map to a probability-of-sign map: for each city, what fraction of realizations agree that dehumidification becomes more important? Cities where the sign is robust deserve immediate code and standard revision; cities where internal variability flips the sign need adaptive, no-regret designs instead. This converts a striking figure into a decision-grade product and directly answers the paper's own stated limitation.

Added value to my researchMethodologically this is my Monte Carlo + spatial entropy toolkit applied to an ensemble field: entropy of the sign distribution becomes a spatial "decision-difficulty" index, and its clustering identifies regions where adaptation planning should be pooled across cities rather than done city-by-city — the same logic I use for urban-agglomeration and regional-collaboration analysis. It also gives me a rigorous template for reporting uncertainty as a mappable layer in my own spatial-prediction work, rather than a confidence interval buried in a table.

③ From energy demand to health burden: map the unmet dehumidification demand

Extending this paperThe paper computes the energy needed to hold indoor RH below 65% — and explicitly notes that real AC systems often lack humidity control, so observed consumption falls short of the modeled requirement. That gap is not a modeling nuisance; it is the public-health signal. Extending this paper: define unmet dehumidification demand = simulated latent heat load × (1 − AC adoption with humidity control), resolve it over the 30° N–30° S band where latent fraction reaches 94%, and treat it as an exposure variable for damp-indoor health outcomes (mould, respiratory illness, sleep disruption, mental-health effects of sustained humid heat). Because latent heat load fraction is provably independent of AC adoption, the paper's own metric can be recombined with adoption and electricity-access data without circularity — an unusually clean coupling opportunity.

Added value to my researchThis is squarely my health-geography line. The unmet-demand surface becomes the demand weight in a "cooling-refuge 2SFCA": supply = publicly accessible air-conditioned facilities (clinics, libraries, malls, transit halls), demand = population × unmet dehumidification burden, catchments delineated with R2SFCA on real travel times. That produces a humid-heat analogue of my primary-care accessibility work and a concrete siting criterion for cooling centres — and, in low-adoption cities, it reframes an energy-planning result as an environmental-justice one.

④ Close the loop on greening: the moisture cost of urban cooling

Extending this paperIn the discussion the authors observe that dehumidification extends urban climate–energy feedbacks into the water cycle, and that urbanization tends to warm-and-dry (lowering latent fraction) while urban greening may reverse this. That is a testable trade-off the paper never runs: vegetation lowers sensible load through evaporative cooling but raises latent load through evapotranspiration. The extension is a scenario suite in the same CLMU framework — sweep canopy cover, irrigation and pervious fraction — and produce a global map of the net sign of greening on total AC energy, separating cities where greening is a genuine energy win from humid cities where it merely converts a cooling bill into a dehumidification bill. Nothing in the paper's machinery prevents this; it needs only the vegetation-scenario forcing and a paired-run design like the "with/without dehumidification" control the authors already built.

Added value to my researchThis gives me a rigorous, physics-grounded objective function for multi-objective urban greening optimization — the structural twin of my EV-charging efficiency-versus-equity work, but with objectives {sensible energy saved, latent energy added, heat-exposure equity, water use}. Explainable ML over the scenario ensemble would then attribute the net effect to specific morphology and climate covariates, yielding transferable siting rules rather than a single-city recommendation.

⑤ Distil the BEM into a physics-informed load-forecasting operator for grids

Extending this paperThe paper's most operationally useful finding — demand spikes on mildly hot, highly humid days, peaking in the 27–31 °C band rather than at temperature extremes — cannot be exploited by system operators, because the evidence lives inside an Earth system model running at ~100 km and decadal horizons. The extension: distil the updated BEM into a differentiable surrogate whose latent-load branch hard-codes the psychrometric law (load ∝ ρ·H·lv·(q − qset)) and whose sensible branch is learned, trained across the paper's global grid cells so it transfers to cities with little metered history. Wrap it in an active-learning loop that queries the expensive simulator only where the surrogate is uncertain, and the paper's climate-scale insight becomes a day-ahead humidity-aware load forecast — plus a principled way to generate the "future weather files" the authors call for.

Added value to my researchThis is my physics-informed graph learning agenda in a new domain: nodes are urban grid cells (or substations), edges carry the atmospheric and network coupling, and the conservation/psychrometric constraint is imposed as a soft physical prior so the model extrapolates into the unseen hot-humid regime instead of interpolating history. It also transfers cleanly back to my spatiotemporal prediction work — the same "hard-code the known physics, learn the residual" recipe is what I want for spatiotemporal KDE and for exposure models where extrapolation, not fit, is the real test.
