Understanding language model scaling for protein fitness prediction

Chao Hou · Di Liu · Aziz Zafar · Yufeng Shen Published 2026-07-13 DOI 10.1038/s43588-026-01010-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Bigger is not better for protein fitness prediction. Protein models score mutations through the sequence likelihood p(sequence), but that likelihood is pushed around by model size, training corpus and stochastic training elements — factors that have nothing to do with real fitness. Performance is best when the predicted wild-type sequence likelihood sits in a moderate range, where p(sequence) tracks the evolutionary patterns visible in homologs; at extreme likelihoods the model assigns nearly all mutations a uniformly low or uniformly high score and the fitness landscape flattens into noise. Because larger models systematically push proteins toward higher likelihood, scaling can carry a protein out of the useful regime — which explains the observed performance decline beyond a certain model size, and makes wild-type likelihood a practical per-protein model-selection signal.

Why It Matters

  • Explains an anomaly the field could only describe. The "scaling wall" for protein language models was known empirically — larger checkpoints scoring worse on ProteinGym — but unexplained. This work supplies a mechanism: the scoring quantity itself, p(sequence), drifts with model capacity independently of biology.
  • Turns a diagnosis into a usable rule. Wild-type sequence likelihood is computable before you have any experimental fitness data, so it becomes a zero-label, per-protein model-selection criterion: pick the model whose predicted wild-type likelihood falls in the moderate band rather than the largest model available.
  • Reframes what "likelihood" means. A high p(sequence) signals that the model has memorised or over-condensed the sequence family, not that it understands the fitness landscape — a distribution-saturation failure mode with clear analogues in every likelihood-scored foundation model.
  • Direct clinical and design consequences. The same p(sequence) scores drive missense variant-effect prediction for disease genes (the paper dissects PTEN, a cancer/autism gene with saturation-mutagenesis data) and drive protein design ranking. A silently mis-scaled score propagates into variant interpretation.
  • A cautionary note for scaling-law orthodoxy. It supplies a concrete counterexample to "larger models perform better across tasks" in a domain where the pretraining objective is only a proxy for the downstream quantity of interest.

Core Methods

  • Likelihood-based mutation scoring. Every model is used in the standard zero-shot way: score a mutant by the shift in sequence likelihood p(sequence) relative to wild type (masked-marginal / pseudo-likelihood scoring as implemented in ProteinGym), then correlate with experimental DMS fitness.
  • Decomposition of likelihood bias. The paper isolates three non-biological drivers of predicted p(sequence) — model size, training dataset composition, and stochastic elements of training/inference (Fig. 1) — showing the score moves even when the underlying fitness landscape cannot.
  • Wild-type likelihood as the controlling variable. Prediction accuracy (Spearman correlation with DMS) is regressed against the model-predicted wild-type sequence likelihood, revealing an inverted-U / moderate-optimum relationship rather than monotone improvement (Fig. 3).
  • Saturation diagnostics at the extremes. At very low or very high wild-type likelihood the mutation-score distribution collapses — nearly all substitutions receive uniformly low or uniformly high likelihood — which is shown directly on the PTEN mutational distribution (Fig. 2) and generalised across proteins.
  • Two-resolution performance analysis. Performance is separated into mean mutation effect per protein versus mutation effects per residue (Fig. 4), distinguishing global calibration failure from local, position-specific failure.
  • Biophysical grounding. Model-predicted residue likelihoods are compared against biophysical context (structure/solvent context via ColabFold + MDTraj) and empirical mutation sensitivity (Fig. 5), testing whether the model's confidence reflects physics or corpus statistics.
  • Evolutionary-pattern benchmark. Homolog alignments (MMseqs2; conservation/co-variation baselines in the spirit of EVmutation/EVE) define the "evolutionary pattern" target; general models are scored on how well their p(sequence) reproduces it (Fig. 6) — the paper's proposed explanatory axis.
  • Statistics & tooling. LOESS/locally weighted regression (Cleveland 1979) for the trend curves; Matplotlib and seaborn for visualisation; all model inference run with unmodified ProteinGym code to keep comparisons fair.

① Promote wild-type likelihood from a diagnostic into a routing layer

Extending this paperThe paper observes that accuracy peaks at moderate wild-type p(sequence) and stops at "practical guidelines". The obvious extension is to operationalise it: build a per-protein model router that, given only the unlabelled wild-type sequence, computes each candidate model's wild-type likelihood, places it on the paper's inverted-U curve (Fig. 3), and selects — or likelihood-weights an ensemble of — the models sitting in the moderate band. Because the routing feature requires no DMS labels, the whole selector can be fit on the paper's own Zenodo deposit and then evaluated as a ProteinGym submission: does a routed mixture of moderately-confident small models beat the single largest checkpoint? That converts a scaling caveat into a deployable inference-time system, and would test whether the moderate-likelihood band is a stable, transferable constant or itself protein-family dependent.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly the missing piece in my geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning line. When I fine-tune a remote-sensing or mobility foundation model and then apply it city-by-city, I currently have no label-free way to know whether the model is in its competent regime for this city. The analogue of wild-type likelihood is the model's likelihood/reconstruction score on the unlabelled local context — the city's own imagery, POI mix or flow matrix. Very high scores mean the city looks like the pretraining corpus (Manhattan-like, over-represented) and the model has collapsed to a prior; very low means out-of-distribution. A moderate-likelihood router would let me pick a per-city model without any local ground-truth survey — a direct answer to the transferability problem that blocks scaling GeoAI accessibility models to data-poor regions.

② Make likelihood saturation a measurable quantity, not a described one

Extending this paperThe paper's mechanism at the extremes is distributional collapse: nearly all mutations receive uniformly low or uniformly high likelihood, so the fitness landscape is flattened (visible in the PTEN distributions, Fig. 2). Right now this is shown graphically. Extending it: define a scalar landscape-resolution index — e.g. the entropy or effective dynamic range of the predicted mutation-score distribution per protein, or the Gini/spread ratio between predicted and experimental score distributions — and show that this single number, not model size, is what predicts Spearman performance. That would replace the paper's qualitative "extreme likelihood" language with a threshold anyone can compute, and would let the community re-plot every ProteinGym leaderboard entry on a resolution axis instead of a parameter-count axis.

Added value to my researchI have been using spatial entropy as a descriptor of urban configurations; this idea flips it into a diagnostic for my own predictive surfaces. When an explainable ML model predicts an accessibility, telehealth-uptake or mental-health-service-demand surface, the same failure appears as a flattened prediction surface: the model outputs nearly the same value everywhere and the map looks smooth and confident while carrying no discriminative information. A "surface-resolution entropy" computed per city or per catchment would flag this before I interpret an MGWR coefficient map or hand a 2SFCA surface to a health-policy audience — a cheap, principled guard against over-smoothed pseudo-precision.

③ Attack the proxy-objective gap directly: train on the target, not the likelihood

Extending this paperThe deepest reading of this paper is that the pretraining objective is a proxy: models optimise p(sequence) but are graded on fitness, and scaling improves the proxy while degrading the target. The extension is to close that loop — take the paper's own moderate-likelihood finding as a calibration constraint and post-hoc recalibrate (temperature/quantile mapping of the mutation-score distribution toward the empirically observed DMS distribution), or fine-tune with a small labelled DMS head and ask whether the inverted U flattens. If a few hundred labelled mutants per family removes the size penalty, the "scaling wall" is a calibration artefact, not a capacity limit — a far stronger and more falsifiable claim than the paper currently makes.

Added value to my researchThis is the generic pathology of every foundation model I want to reuse: pretrained on a reconstruction proxy, judged on a policy-relevant target. In my telehealth and primary-care accessibility work, an imagery- or mobility-pretrained encoder optimises pixel/trajectory reconstruction while I need it to rank service deserts. The recipe transfers verbatim — quantile-map the model's predicted deprivation/accessibility distribution onto the observed one before use, and measure whether a small, cheaply-collected local label set removes the transfer penalty. It also gives me a defensible answer to a reviewer's standard question: why not just use the largest available foundation model?

④ Corpus representation as the hidden confounder — an equity audit of the pretraining set

Extending this paperThe paper names training dataset as one of the three non-fitness drivers of p(sequence), but treats it mainly as a nuisance term. Extending it: quantify, for each benchmark protein, its homolog abundance and redundancy in the UniProt-derived pretraining corpus (MMseqs2 cluster size, taxonomic breadth, sequence-identity spread), then test whether corpus over-representation — not parameter count — is the true cause of the excessive wild-type likelihood that pushes a protein out of the moderate band. That would predict a specific, checkable asymmetry: well-sampled model organisms and heavily-studied human disease genes degrade first as models scale, while orphan and viral proteins keep improving. Since variant interpretation is clinically consequential, this is a representation-bias audit with real stakes.

Added value to my researchThis is my digital-divide argument in molecular clothing, and I can lift the design wholesale. Geospatial foundation models are pretrained on corpora that over-represent wealthy, dense, well-mapped cities (rich OSM coverage, frequent high-resolution imagery, dense mobile-phone panels) and under-represent rural and low-income regions — precisely the places where accessibility analysis matters most for health equity. The protocol maps one-to-one: measure each study area's representation in the pretraining corpus, then test whether model competence is a function of representation rather than model size. That produces a publishable geographic representation audit for GeoAI, and gives a quantitative basis for arguing that scaling alone will not close spatial-health-equity gaps.

⑤ Local support beats global scale: a neighbourhood-conditioned scoring operator

Extending this paperFig. 6 is the paper's causal claim — general models succeed to the extent they reproduce evolutionary patterns in homologs — which implies that what matters is not global capacity but the quality of the local support set around the target sequence. The extension is architectural: instead of scaling a single global model, build a retrieval-conditioned scorer that, per protein, retrieves its homolog neighbourhood (MMseqs2 / Foldseek), and conditions scoring on that neighbourhood — an MSA-Transformer-style idea pushed to explicit, tunable retrieval, where the paper's inverted-U curve is used to choose how much neighbourhood to retrieve. Prediction: matched local support recovers the performance that scaling destroys, and the moderate-likelihood band becomes a knob (retrieval depth) rather than a fixed property of the checkpoint.

Added value to my researchThis is the molecular restatement of the first law of geography, and it validates the direction I already argue for: local, neighbourhood-conditioned models beat globally-pooled giants when the phenomenon is non-stationary. Concretely it motivates a retrieval-conditioned GeoAI predictor — for a target neighbourhood, retrieve its k most similar neighbourhoods across cities (by density, network form, service mix) and condition prediction on that retrieved support, which is MGWR's kernel logic re-implemented as learned retrieval and freed from contiguous-space adjacency. It also sharpens the physics-informed graph-learning agenda: the retrieval set defines where the learned operator is trustworthy, and the paper supplies the diagnostic (likelihood in the moderate band) for detecting when the support has run out.
