Towards conversational artificial intelligence for disease management

Valentin Liévin · Anil Palepu · Wei-Hung Weng · Khaled Saab · David Stutz · Yong Cheng · Kavita Kulkarni · S. Sara Mahdavi · Joëlle Barral · Dale R. Webster · Katherine Chou · Avinatan Hassidim · Yossi Matias · James Manyika · Ryutaro Tanno · Vivek Natarajan · Adam Rodman · Tao Tu · Alan Karthikesalingam · Mike Schaekermann Published 2026-06-17 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10764-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

An agentic LLM system (AMIE, a fast dialogue agent plus a slow long-context management-reasoning agent) moves conversational medical AI beyond diagnosis into longitudinal disease management. In a randomized, blinded virtual OSCE over 100 multivisit scenarios × 3 visits against 21 primary care physicians, AMIE was non-inferior on all 15 evaluation axes and significantly better on plan appropriateness (e.g. 95% vs 72% at visit 1), preciseness of treatments (96% vs 62% at visit 1) and guideline alignment / explicit citation (100% vs 92–86% referencing). On the new RxQA medication benchmark (600 pharmacist-validated questions), AMIE beat PCPs on the harder subset (57.9% vs 47.8% open-book), yet peak accuracy stayed below 75% for both — the authors explicitly state the system is not ready for real-world care.

★

Why It Matters

  • Shifts the evaluation frontier from diagnosis to management. Diagnostic dialogue has been heavily benchmarked; management reasoning — choosing investigations, dosing, watchful waiting, follow-up, and revising all of it as the disease evolves — had almost no rigorous LLM evaluation because it is intrinsically context-specific (two physicians can legitimately disagree).
  • Tests continuity, not a single snapshot. Each scenario spans three visits with the same patient actor, with new symptoms, treatment responses and test results injected between visits — a direct probe of the fragmented-care problem where patients see different clinicians at each encounter.
  • Preciseness is the concrete clinical delta. AMIE moved from "prescribe an antibiotic" to a named drug, dose, route, duration and monitoring plan — the difference between a directionally correct suggestion and an actionable clinical directive.
  • Traceability is built into the generation, not bolted on. Every plan item carries a generated citation to the source guideline, produced through in-context reasoning under decoding constraints — making the plan auditable against NICE/BMJ text.
  • Ships a new, hard benchmark. RxQA (600 questions from two national formularies, revised by board-certified pharmacists, difficulty-rated) counters the saturation of legacy medical QA benchmarks, and the OpenFDA half is openly released.
  • Honest about the ceiling. Patient actors are not patients; scenarios had definitive answers; visits were 1–2 days apart rather than weeks; UK guidelines were used with Canadian/Indian PCPs. The paper frames itself as an art-of-the-possible demonstration, not a deployment claim.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Guideline corpus
627 full-text clinical guidelines = 527 UK NICE guidance documents + 100 BMJ Best Practice documents, totalling 10.5 million tokens. Of these, 50 NICE + 50 BMJ were directly relevant to the study scenarios; the remainder are distractors. Typical document length 30–50 pages. Both PCPs and AMIE had access to the same corpus.
OSCE scenarios
120 multivisit scenarios (20 validation + 100 evaluation), each with 3 visits. Evenly split between clinical providers in India and Canada, and across 5 specialties × 20 scenarios each: cardiology; pulmonology; obstetrics/gynaecology/urology; gastroenterology; neurology & musculoskeletal. Many scenarios deliberately include information inconsistency or multimorbidity.
Human participants
21 board-certified PCPs (median 9 years post-residency, IQR 5–12), 21 validated patient actors, and 30 specialist physician raters (median 7 years post-residency, IQR 4–12) — 3 specialists per specialty × geography combination, with every rating task done in triplicate.
RxQA benchmark
600 multiple-choice questions derived from two national drug formularies — OpenFDA (USA) and the British National Formulary (UK). Per jurisdiction: 100 "short" + 200 "long" questions selected such that Gemini needed the label to answer. Revised by 4 board-certified pharmacists per jurisdiction, who also rated difficulty; the analysis split is n = 282 lower-difficulty and n = 318 higher-difficulty. Human baseline: 3 PCPs per jurisdiction, closed-book then open-book.
Model / training data
Backbone Gemini 1.5 Flash (2M-token context; ablations also use Gemini 2.0/2.5 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Pro, DeepSeek-V3, o3, GPT-5). Post-training used open datasets MedQA, MultiMedQA, MIMIC-III plus real-world medical dialogues and newly generated multivisit simulated dialogues. Retrieval index built with Gecko 1B text embeddings over generated guideline abstracts; 256,000 tokens (≈6 guideline documents on average) allocated to in-context knowledge per query.
Time structure
Scenario narratives unfold over weeks–months, but the actual inter-visit interval was 1–2 days (minimum 24 h) — a stated limitation that likely flattered human memory performance.
Availability
All 120 scenario details and AMIE output are in the Supplementary Information (PDF + CSV); PCP output and ratings are shared for 2 sample scenarios only. The OpenFDA portion of RxQA is open (github.com/Google-Health/rxqa); the BNF portion cannot be redistributed for licensing reasons — readers must contact BNF. Model code and weights are not released, citing safety of unmonitored clinical use.
Not stated
The paper does not report the number of dialogue turns per visit, the total token/compute cost of the study, per-scenario patient demographics, or a quantitative audit of latent reasoning-trace errors (explicitly left to future work).

Core Methods

  • Dual-process agentic architecture. Explicitly modelled on Kahneman's system 1 / system 2: a dialogue agent (fast, empathetic, low-latency, orchestrates the system) invokes an Mx agent (slow, heavy inference-time compute) as a tool; the Mx agent returns the management plan that the dialogue agent then communicates.
  • Persistent multivisit agent state. A modular structure — current patient summary, current differential diagnosis, current management plan, visit number — updated by an asynchronous background reasoning subroutine and shared by both agents, so context survives across the three encounters.
  • Chain of reasoning in dialogue. Three low-latency steps per turn: plan response → generate response → refine response against quality criteria, each conditioned on both the full dialogue history and the persistent state.
  • Coarse retrieval then long-context reasoning. Because 10.5M tokens exceeds Gemini's 2M window, a Gecko-1B embedding retriever indexes guidelines by title and pre-generated clinically-oriented abstracts; the Mx agent emits up to five natural-language queries, and the top documents (≈6, budgeted at 256k tokens by auto-evaluation) are reasoned over in context rather than through a chunked RAG pipeline — deliberately avoiding chunking choices, compounding retrieval errors and extra latency.
  • Structured generation as a control surface. A target reasoning structure is written as Python code with docstrings, auto-converted to a JSON schema and enforced as decoding constraints. The trace is forced through analyse patient → set objectives → plan and cite, guaranteeing valid plan structure and citation formats within a single model call (which also amortizes document encoding).
  • Ensemble refinement of plans. The Mx agent drafts four candidate management plans, then refines and merges them into a final plan conditioned on the retrieved guidelines, under a ≤1 minute response-time budget to keep the two agents synchronized.
  • Post-training pipeline. Supervised fine-tuning on simulated multivisit doctor–patient dialogues, medical QA, EHR summarization and real dialogues, followed by RLHF/RLAIF with a reward model trained on human- and LLM-generated pairwise preferences over dialogue responses and case-management recommendations.
  • Randomized blinded multivisit OSCE. Each patient actor completes each scenario twice — once with AMIE, once with a PCP — in randomized order, same actor across all three visits, text-chat only; PCPs could take notes and review full prior transcripts.
  • Two-tier evaluation instrument. Patient actors apply GMCPQ + subsets of PACES and the new MXEKF rubric; specialists apply full MXEKF, PACES and an expanded diagnosis-and-management rubric (15 axes, 9 yes/no and 6 binarized from 4–5-point scales), all in triplicate with median/majority aggregation.
  • MXEKF rubric construction. Ten of twelve empirically derived key features of management reasoning were retained (contrast and selection, preferences/constraints, shared decision-making, monitoring and adjustment, competing priorities, illness-specific knowledge, clinician roles, relationship, prognostication, encounter organization); process knowledge and management scripts were dropped as infeasible. Preference rates computed over 51 axis × visit × rater-perspective combinations.
  • Statistics. Two-sided McNemar tests with question- or case-level pairing and false-discovery-rate correction throughout; 95% binomial CIs on preference rates.
  • Fully simulated ablation study. An end-to-end AI recreation of the OSCE with auto-evaluation isolates the contributions of post-training, the dialogue agent and the Mx agent — and shows the scaffolding's benefit shrinking as the backbone improves.

① Geographize the guideline corpus: turn "swap NICE for WHO" from a sentence into an experiment

Extending this paperThe Discussion makes a striking but untested claim: a clinic in a low- or middle-income country "could easily switch from NICE guidance or BMJ Best Practice to World Health Organization guidelines." Nothing in the study verifies this — all 627 documents are UK-authored, while the PCPs practised in Canada and India, which the authors themselves flag as a confound. The natural extension is a guideline-swap factorial design: hold the 100 scenarios fixed and re-run AMIE with (a) NICE/BMJ, (b) WHO, (c) national guidelines of the scenario's country of origin, then measure how much of the 95%-vs-72% appropriateness margin survives, and whether the Mx agent's citation behaviour degrades when guidance is sparser, older or contradictory. Because the corpus is just in-context text, this is a cheap ablation with high policy value — and it converts guideline provenance into an explicit, measurable covariate rather than an acknowledged limitation.

Added value to my researchThis is the missing geographic axis for my health-geography work: guideline provenance becomes a spatial attribute, and "management quality under locally-appropriate guidance" becomes a mappable surface. I can build a guideline-context sensitivity index per region and overlay it on my accessibility layers — asking not only "can a person reach care in 30 minutes?" but "would an AI-assisted encounter here be grounded in guidance that actually fits this setting?" That directly instruments the telehealth digital-divide question with something sharper than broadband coverage.

② Put AMIE inside a spatial accessibility model: conversational AI as an elastic supply term

Extending this paperThe paper's stated motivation is PCP shortage and care fragmentation, yet its evidence is entirely aspatial — 100 scenarios floating free of any catchment, travel time or provider-to-population ratio. The extension: couple the measured per-visit non-inferiority rates to a real service-area model. Treat the AI arm as a supply source whose capacity is bounded not by physician-hours but by compute, connectivity and escalation bandwidth, then simulate how many people move from "underserved" to "served" under different escalation thresholds (what fraction of visits must be handed to a human, and where do those humans have to be?). That would turn a benchmark result into a coverage estimate — the quantity health systems actually procure against.

Added value to my researchThis is a first-class extension of my 2SFCA/R2SFCA program. Conventional 2SFCA has one supply term per facility; here supply becomes two-tier and substitutable — an AI tier with near-zero marginal capacity but a non-zero escalation rate, and a physician tier with hard capacity limits. The escalation rate estimated from studies like this one is exactly the parameter that couples the tiers, letting me formulate a telehealth-augmented R2SFCA where the catchment for the AI tier is bounded by broadband and device access rather than road distance. Combined with intercity healthcare-network structure, this gives a concrete way to ask which regions gain most from an AI-assisted primary-care layer.

③ Break the 1–2 day compression: real longitudinal continuity as the actual test

Extending this paperThe single most consequential limitation the authors name is temporal: scenario narratives span weeks or months, but visits were 1–2 days apart, which they admit probably inflated human performance by removing the memory decay that real follow-up imposes. The persistent agent state, by contrast, is lossless by construction. A rigorous extension would run a time-dilated arm — genuine multi-week intervals, plus an information-decay manipulation on the human side (notes available vs withheld) — to separate reasoning advantage from memory advantage. Second, since the agent state is a structured object, one can inject realistic record incompleteness: missing labs, results arriving out of order, a prior visit logged elsewhere. That converts a clean simulation into a stress test of the fragmentation problem the paper claims to address.

The agent state is effectively a per-patient trajectory object, so continuity of care can be measured as a property of that trajectory — and aggregated spatially into a continuity surface the same way I build spatiotemporal kernel-density surfaces. It also flags a data-quality trap I care about for spatial prediction: encounter records are missing non-randomly by access, so any AI-driven risk model I fit on such data inherits an access-shaped bias that will masquerade as geography.

④ Audit the reasoning traces: structured decoding as an explainability instrument

Extending this paperThe paper builds something unusual and then declines to exploit it. Because decoding constraints force the trace through analyse → objectives → plan-and-cite, every AMIE plan comes with a machine-readable derivation and per-item citations — and Extended Data Fig. 5 shows a latent reasoning error that never propagated into an otherwise appropriate plan. The authors explicitly say a quantitative analysis of traces was out of scope. That is the extension: build a trace auditor that verifies each generated citation against the cited guideline span, classifies latent errors by type, and measures whether latent-error rate predicts downstream plan failure. Adding an adversarial layer — scenarios engineered so that two retrieved guidelines conflict — would test whether the citation machinery reports the conflict or silently picks a side.

Added value to my researchThis maps cleanly onto my explainable ML for spatial prediction agenda. The paper's key design insight — constrain the structure of the explanation at generation time rather than reconstructing it post hoc — is exactly what post-hoc SHAP/attribution on spatial models fails to deliver. I want the analogue for GeoAI: a spatial predictor whose output is schema-constrained to emit (evidence region, mechanism, source-layer citation) triples, so an accessibility or risk prediction can be traced to the OSM/census/remote-sensing layer that produced it. The verify-the-citation step also becomes a reusable QA gate for my multi-source data-fusion pipelines.

⑤ Make plans feasible, not just correct: constrain RxQA and the Mx agent by local supply

Extending this paperAMIE's headline strength is preciseness — naming the exact drug, dose, route and duration. But the paper itself notes that drug shortages are a persistent global problem and that real order entry is constrained by formularies, order sets and pharmacist oversight, none of which were present. A precise prescription for a medicine that is out of stock in the patient's district is worse than a vague one, because it looks actionable. The extension: add a availability/feasibility constraint layer to the Mx agent — local formulary, stock levels, price, and the travel distance to a dispensing pharmacy — and re-score plans on deliverability alongside guideline alignment. RxQA gains a matching subset in which the "correct" answer depends on what is actually obtainable, which is the question a prescriber in a supply-constrained setting really faces.

Added value to my researchThis is where the paper meets my facility-access and optimization work most directly. Deliverability is a spatial quantity: it is a function of pharmacy locations, stock, and the same catchment geometry I model for clinics and charging stations. I can reuse my efficiency-versus-equity optimization framing to ask where a plan-feasibility constraint binds hardest, and to identify districts where improving dispensing access yields more health return than improving consultation access. It also supplies a genuinely new decision variable for agent-based simulation: the prescription is no longer an endpoint but a request that must survive a supply network.
