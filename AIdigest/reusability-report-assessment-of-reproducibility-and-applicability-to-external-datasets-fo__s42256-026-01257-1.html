Reusability report: Assessment of reproducibility and applicability to external datasets for RXNGraphormer

Chonghuan Zhang · Majian Zeng · Qianghua Lin · Kuangbiao Liao Published 2026-06-10 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01257-1 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

An independent team re-ran RXNGraphormer — a pretrained graph–transformer encoder with a delta-molecular reaction representation designed for cross-task generalization (Xu et al., Nat. Mach. Intell. 2025) — using only its released code, pretrained checkpoint and benchmark data. All major regression and sequence-generation results were consistently reproduced, including the relative difficulty ordering of out-of-sample splits. Pushed onto external high-throughput experimentation (HTE) datasets, the pretrained encoder adapted efficiently and predicted well with minimal fine-tuning; on an external sequence benchmark beyond USPTO, forward prediction held up but retrosynthesis degraded under distributional shift. Reusability, in other words, is task-asymmetric: pretrained representations transfer, generative inversion does not transfer equally.

Why It Matters

  • Reproducibility is tested, not asserted. Most "unified foundation model" claims in scientific ML are never independently re-executed. Here a third-party group ran the published artefacts end-to-end and reports that the workflow is stable and transparent — a rare positive control for the field.
  • Separates reproducibility from reusability. Reproducing a paper's own benchmarks and reusing its model on someone else's data are different bars. This report clears the first and then measures exactly where the second starts to fail.
  • Localizes the failure mode. The degradation is not global: forward reaction prediction survives the move off USPTO, while retrosynthetic (inverse) generation is the sensitive component. That is diagnostic information a benchmark leaderboard cannot give.
  • Shows pretraining pays off cheaply. On standardized HTE datasets the pretrained encoder reached strong performance with minimal fine-tuning, supporting the "pretrain once, adapt to small curated experimental campaigns" economics that laboratory groups actually need.
  • Names the real bottleneck: representation and data harmonization. The authors conclude that harmonized reaction representations, curated data and domain-specific refinement — not architecture — govern whether a released model is usable elsewhere.
  • Ships its own reusable artefacts. Scripts on Zenodo, processed splits on figshare, a public GitHub reproduction repository — the report is itself an example of the practice it evaluates.

Core Methods

  • Strict re-execution: run the released implementation and pretrained checkpoint unmodified on the original benchmark datasets, re-deriving both the regression results and the sequence-generation results reported by Xu et al.
  • Pattern-level (not just point-level) validation: check that the relative difficulty ordering of out-of-sample evaluations is preserved, not merely that headline numbers land close — a stronger reproducibility criterion than metric matching.
  • Transfer with minimal fine-tuning: adapt the pretrained graph–transformer encoder to external HTE yield datasets generated under standardized experimental conditions, testing how much task-specific refinement the frozen/lightly-tuned representation actually needs.
  • Clean-vs-noisy data contrast: pair standardized HTE campaigns with a literature-derived (Reaxys-curated) amide-coupling benchmark, isolating data provenance/curation as a variable distinct from chemistry.
  • Out-of-domain sequence stress test: evaluate forward and retrosynthetic generation on ORDerly, i.e. outside the USPTO distribution the model was trained on, to expose asymmetric robustness between the forward and inverse tasks.
  • Delta-molecular reaction representation retained: the assessed architecture keeps the original pretrained graph–transformer encoder plus delta-molecular reaction embedding; the report's contribution is evaluation design, not architectural change.
  • Reproducible reporting stack: notebooks and scripts released with a Zenodo DOI, processed splits on figshare, GitHub repository for the reproduction pipeline; figures produced with Matplotlib.

① Turn the report's pass/fail verdict into a quantified reusability envelope

Extending this paperThe report establishes a binary-ish result — reproduction succeeded, HTE transfer succeeded cheaply, retrosynthesis degraded — but never says how far the model can be pushed before it breaks. The natural extension is to replace the verdict with a dose–response curve of reusability: parameterize the shift between the source (USPTO / original benchmarks) and each target set with an explicit distance measure (reaction-class overlap, reagent-vocabulary divergence, yield-distribution Wasserstein distance, scaffold novelty), then regress the observed performance drop on that distance. That produces a predictive rule — "for a target set at shift level d, expect performance p and require n fine-tuning examples" — which is what a practitioner deciding whether to adopt a released checkpoint actually needs, and which the current qualitative statement cannot deliver. Running it across all five external datasets already assembled would cost almost no new computation.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly the missing instrument when I fine-tune geospatial foundation models across cities. Instead of asking "does the model trained on US urban imagery/POI structure work in a Chinese prefecture-level city?", I would fit a shift-versus-degradation curve using measurable spatial covariate distances (built-form entropy, density regime, POI-category composition, road-network motif distribution) and get an a priori transfer budget. It converts my telehealth/primary-care accessibility models from "retrain everywhere" to "predict transferability, then retrain only where the envelope says it is necessary" — a direct saving on the labelled-data bottleneck that dominates health-geography work.

② Explain why retrosynthesis breaks: causal attribution of the transfer failure

Extending this paperThe single sharpest observation in the report — forward prediction survives the move off USPTO while retrosynthetic prediction is sensitive to distributional shift — is reported as a phenomenon, not diagnosed. Extending the paper means asking which of three confounded causes is responsible: (a) the inverse problem is intrinsically one-to-many, so any shift in the reagent/condition prior collapses the decoder's search; (b) the tokenization and reaction-representation mismatch between USPTO-style SMILES strings and ORDerly's curated records — the report's own conclusion flags "harmonized reaction representations" as decisive; or (c) simple coverage: the target retrosynthesis space contains templates never seen in pretraining. A controlled ablation — re-canonicalize ORDerly into USPTO's exact representation convention, then re-run — would separate representation artefact from genuine chemical generalization failure, and would settle whether the reusability limit is a data-standards problem or a model problem.

Added value to my researchThe same triage discipline is what my explainable spatial ML agenda needs. When a 2SFCA-based accessibility model or an MGWR surface trained in one metropolitan region underperforms in another, I currently report the degradation; I rarely decompose it into (a) genuine spatial non-stationarity, (b) schema/representation mismatch between data providers (POI taxonomies, census-tract vs. grid geometry, differing service-catchment definitions), and (c) coverage gaps. Adopting this paper's forward-vs-inverse contrast suggests a concrete analogue: my forward task (predict demand given supply geometry) may transfer while my inverse task (site facilities to hit an equity target) does not — and that asymmetry deserves explicit testing rather than assumption.

③ Add an uncertainty layer so the model refuses instead of silently degrading

Extending this paperA reusability report that finds partial failure implicitly demands a self-aware version of the model. RXNGraphormer as assessed emits point predictions with no abstention mechanism, so a chemist reusing it on a shifted dataset receives confidently wrong retrosynthetic routes. The extension is to wrap the unmodified pretrained encoder in a calibrated uncertainty head — deep ensembles, evidential regression on the yield track, and sequence-level entropy or conformal prediction sets on the generation track — and then re-score every experiment in Fig. 3 by selective accuracy: performance on the subset the model is willing to answer. The report's own datasets provide a ready-made benchmark for whether uncertainty tracks the true shift, and this preserves the authors' commendable rule of not modifying the original architecture: the head sits on top.

Added value to my researchSelective prediction is what makes GeoAI usable for public-health policy. A mental-health accessibility surface or a telehealth digital-divide index that is confidently wrong in exactly the rural, sparsely-sampled tracts that most need it is worse than no map. Porting this idea gives me an uncertainty-aware accessibility raster: every grid cell carries a calibrated interval, and cells outside the training support are flagged as "insufficient evidence" rather than shaded. That is a far more defensible artefact to hand a health department than a smooth continuous surface, and it composes naturally with my spatiotemporal KDE and MGWR pipelines.

④ Close the loop: use the reusability map to decide which experiments to run next

Extending this paperThe report is purely retrospective — it audits a released model against datasets that already exist. Its most valuable latent output, however, is a map of where the pretrained encoder is weak, and that map is precisely an experiment-design signal. Extending the paper: couple the fine-tuning protocol to an active-learning loop in which the model's uncertainty over an unexplored reagent/substrate grid selects the next HTE plate, so a small number of new standardized measurements is spent exactly where transfer is worst. Because the authors already work with three standardized HTE campaigns and a public reproduction pipeline, this converts a static reusability verdict into a closed-loop data-production policy — and would directly test the report's own conclusion that curated data, not architecture, is the binding constraint.

Added value to my researchTranslated to my work, this is uncertainty-driven survey and sensor siting. When a spatial accessibility or urban-health model is least reliable in specific neighbourhood types, that is where the next round of mobile-phone-derived validation, household survey sampling, or ground-truth facility audit should be spent. It reframes multi-source data fusion (mobile phone, social media, remote sensing) from opportunistic to designed: an acquisition policy conditioned on model uncertainty, which is also the cleanest justification I can give a funder for a targeted data-collection budget in an EV-charging or primary-care siting study.

⑤ Generalize the report itself into a portable reusability protocol for spatial models

Extending this paperThe most transferable thing here is not the finding but the evaluation design: (i) re-run released artefacts unmodified; (ii) verify pattern-level agreement, not just headline metrics; (iii) transfer to clean standardized external data; (iv) transfer to noisy literature-derived data; (v) transfer out of the training distribution entirely; (vi) deposit every processed split and script with a DOI. Extending the paper means abstracting this into a domain-agnostic reusability rubric with scored dimensions — artefact completeness, environment pinning, representation portability, task-asymmetric robustness, data-redistribution honesty (this report is exemplary on the last, explicitly stating that licensed Reaxys records could not be shared) — and then applying the same rubric across the corpus's other reusability reports to see whether the scientific-ML field's failure modes are shared or field-specific.

Added value to my researchThis is a publishable instrument for my open-science / FAIR line: a Reusability Report protocol for GeoAI. Spatial ML has an acute version of the problem — models depend on projection, resolution, administrative-boundary vintage, and POI taxonomy, so "reproducible code" often still fails to run on another city's data. A GeoAI rubric would add spatial-specific axes (CRS and resolution declaration, boundary-vintage pinning, spatial cross-validation rather than random splits, transferability across density regimes) and could be piloted by re-executing my own published accessibility models in a new metropolitan region. It converts reproducibility from a compliance checkbox into a research contribution, and it directly strengthens the credibility of the 2SFCA/R2SFCA and telehealth-divide work I want policy actors to rely on.
