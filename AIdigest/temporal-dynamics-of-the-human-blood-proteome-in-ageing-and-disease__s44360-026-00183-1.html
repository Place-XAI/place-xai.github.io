Temporal dynamics of the human blood proteome in ageing and disease

Wei Wu · Zhao Yang · Linling Cheng · Juncheng Fan · Chi Zhang · Youming Yuan · Yifan Bu · Xuan Deng · Pingping Jia · Yan Liu · Yuhan Zhang · Yue Qi · Xianghong Dong · Wenjia Hu · Qichen Sun · Can Liao · Zheng Xu · Wanqiu Ding · Xinli Hu · Jinzhuo Wang · Rui-Ping Xiao · Jing Liu Published 2026-08-10 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00183-1 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across a 20-year, four-timepoint longitudinal cohort of 1,298 adults profiled for 10,776 protein markers, the circulating proteome is not uniformly stable: temporal stability varies enormously protein-by-protein, unlike the relatively uniform stability of routine clinical markers. Highly stable proteins are strongly genetically shaped, enriched for circulation-mediated functions such as immune regulation, and largely conserved across sex and life stage. Used as biological fingerprints, they sharpen a sparse proteomic ageing clock, separate lifelong-stable from volatile risk markers for cardiovascular disease and dementia, and define 21 proteins whose personalized homeostatic baselines, once deviated from, independently predict mortality.

★

Why It Matters

  • Reframes "normal range" as a personal quantity. Population reference intervals assume interchangeable people; this work shows that for a large share of the proteome each individual has a private setpoint, so the clinically meaningful signal is deviation from your own baseline, not deviation from the population mean.
  • Gives biomarker discovery a missing reliability axis. A protein that predicts disease but drifts month to month is a poor screening target. Splitting markers into lifelong-stable vs volatile tells the field which associations survive a single blood draw — a triage criterion that thousands of proteomic association studies currently lack.
  • Makes ageing clocks cheaper and more deployable. Selecting high-stability proteins yields a sparse panel that improves biological-age prediction and its link to ageing outcomes — moving from 10,000-plex research assays toward a panel small enough to be assayed at scale.
  • Turns homeostasis into a measurable, prognostic construct. Deviation from personalized baselines across metabolic, immune and tissue-remodelling axes independently predicted mortality, while (in the extended-data analysis of 26 routine clinical markers) only hs-CRP did — evidence that proteomic drift captures early homeostatic failure that routine chemistry misses.
  • A 20-year Chinese cohort validated in UK Biobank. Cross-cohort, cross-platform (SomaScan → Olink) replication is what elevates this from a single-population observation to a transferable claim about human biology.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Primary cohort
CMCS Proteomics Project (CMCS-PP), nested in the Chinese Multi-provincial Cohort Study. The parent CMCS enrolled 21,953 participants aged ≥35, free of major chronic disease at baseline in 1992. A Beijing-community subset banked blood at the 2002, 2007, 2012 and 2022 visits; 2,621 participants were selected for proteomic profiling (criteria: archived 2002 sample + complete follow-up for cardiovascular events and mortality).
Analytic sample
1,298 individuals in the headline longitudinal analysis, spanning middle-to-later adulthood into older age. 527 participants provided samples at all four visits (Extended Data Fig. 6 upset plot); the remainder contributed 2–3 visits.
Proteomic depth
10,776 protein markers per sample across four time points — an aptamer-based SomaScan-class panel described in the paper as covering roughly half the human proteome. Summary-level results for all 10,776 proteins are released in Supplementary Information.
Time span
~20 years of follow-up (2002 proteomic baseline → 2022 visit); disease/mortality follow-up from 2002 onward, with incident-event analyses run from 2012 onward.
Validation cohort
UK Biobank — the Olink Explore 1.5K COVID-19 repeat-imaging subcohort, n = 1,463 participants with three proteomic assessments (Instance 0 baseline, Instance 2 from 2014+, Instance 3 from 2019+). PHI was recomputed for all three visit pairs.
Cross-platform check
SomaScan-CMCS vs Olink-UKB PHI agreement: SCC = 0.701 (P = 1.22 × 10⁻¹¹⁶) for the 782 proteins with high inter-platform measurement consistency (IMC, SCC > 0.29), and only SCC = 0.119 for the 462 low-IMC proteins — i.e. agreement tracks assay comparability, honestly reported.
Derived panels
571 proteins with PHI > 0.7 entered the survival analyses; 21 proteins define personalized homeostatic baselines; ProtAge20 (20 constituent proteins) anchors the ageing clock, with AGER, WFDC2 and CA14 (PHI > 0.5) selected as replacements in the PHI-informed version.
Comparator markers
26 longitudinally measured routine clinical markers (lipids, glucose, hs-CRP, etc.) used as the stability and prognosis benchmark; event subsets after exclusions: n = 822 (incident CHD), n = 835 (incident heart failure), n = 854 (all-cause mortality).
Access
De-identified CMCS-PP proteomic and phenotypic data are available to qualified investigators via a data-use request to the corresponding author, reviewed by a data access committee, with analyses run inside a secure cloud enclave (CMCS Data Use Agreement). Code: github.com/ww20hust/individual-specificity.

Core Methods

  • Repeat-measure stability index (PHI). For each of 10,776 proteins, quantify how much of the total variance is between-individual and persistent across visit pairs — an intraclass-correlation-style "individuality/homeostasis" index computed over 2002/2007/2012/2022 pairs, then assembled into a proteome-wide PHI atlas (Fig. 2).
  • Stratified conservation testing. Recompute PHI within sex and life-stage strata and across time intervals to test whether stability rankings are conserved, isolating the specific proteins whose stability shifts at life-stage transitions.
  • Determinant modelling (Fig. 3). Regress PHI against genetic determinants (heritability / pQTL-style evidence, cf. refs. 13, 18) and exposomic determinants (lifestyle exposome, cf. ref. 19), showing high-PHI proteins are genetically anchored.
  • Functional enrichment (Fig. 4). Map high- vs low-PHI proteins onto physiological roles and tissue origins (UniProt, Human Protein Atlas, SomaModules-style pathway enrichment), recovering enrichment of circulation-mediated functions such as immune regulation.
  • PHI-informed ageing clock (Fig. 5). Rebuild a proteomic biological-age model following the Goeminne et al. framework, but restrict/reweight candidate features by PHI (e.g. swapping in AGER, WFDC2, CA14 with PHI > 0.5 and large model coefficients), then test the sparse panel against ageing-related outcomes.
  • Intra-individual ranking & predictability (Fig. 6). Show high-PHI proteins preserve within-person rank order across visits and are better predicted from a person's own earlier measurement — the operational definition of a usable "fingerprint".
  • Personalized baseline + deviation survival models. Define each participant's own homeostatic baseline for the 21-protein set, compute deviations, and fit two-sided Cox proportional-hazards models (age- and sex-adjusted) for incident CHD, heart failure and all-cause mortality, with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR across the 571 PHI > 0.7 proteins and likelihood-ratio tests comparing Cox R² between base and deviation-augmented models.
  • Cross-platform replication. Recompute PHI in UK Biobank Olink data, split proteins by inter-platform measurement consistency, and report agreement separately for high- and low-IMC sets rather than pooling — a conservative validation design.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this paper: first how the paper itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, spatial accessibility & 2SFCA/R2SFCA, explainable and physics-informed spatial learning, multi-source data fusion, open/FAIR science).

① Geo-reference the PHI atlas: from person-specific setpoints to place-specific setpoints

Extending this paperThe paper's decisive move is to replace the population reference interval with a personal homeostatic baseline — but the baseline is treated as an aspatial property of the individual. The CMCS-PP participants are a defined Beijing community sample followed at four dated visits between 2002 and 2022, exactly the period over which Beijing's air quality, built environment and greenspace changed dramatically. A direct extension: geocode residence at each visit and re-estimate PHI with a place random effect, asking whether the variance the paper attributes to "individual specificity" partly encodes where a person has lived. The testable claim is that low-PHI (volatile) proteins carry a much larger neighbourhood-level variance component than high-PHI proteins — which would convert the paper's stability axis into a readout of environmental sensitivity and give the Fig. 3 exposome panel a spatial denominator it currently lacks.

Added value to my researchThis gives my spatial-exposome work an unusually strong outcome: instead of regressing a cross-sectional biomarker on neighbourhood attributes, I would regress a 20-year within-person variance component on them — a design far less vulnerable to residential self-selection. Methodologically it is a natural MGWR/spatial-random-effects problem where the dependent variable is stability rather than level, and it supplies a biologically anchored endpoint for the "healthy city" analyses I run with remote-sensing and POI covariates.

② Rebuild the exposomic axis of Fig. 3 from 20-year satellite and mobility exposure histories

Extending this paperFig. 3 pairs PHI with "genetic and exposomic determinants", and the referenced exposome work (Isaac et al., medRxiv 2025) is questionnaire-based lifestyle exposure — self-reported, coarse in time, and blind to ambient conditions. Because the cohort has four dated draws, one can instead reconstruct each participant's continuous 2002–2022 exposure trajectory from remote sensing and administrative layers (PM2.5 and O3 reanalysis, NDVI/greenspace, land-surface temperature, night-time lights, road-network noise proxies) and test whether cumulative or volatile exposure predicts which proteins lose their setpoint. The sharpest version asks a dose–response question the paper cannot currently ask: does a person whose exposure history is itself unstable show systematically depressed PHI across the immune and tissue-remodelling axes the paper implicates in mortality?

Added value to my researchThis is my multi-source data-fusion pipeline pointed at a molecular endpoint — spatiotemporal kernel density and trajectory-based exposure reconstruction feeding a proteomic response. It also lets me test an idea I care about methodologically: that exposure variability, not just exposure mean, is the health-relevant quantity, which is precisely the kind of hypothesis a four-timepoint proteomic cohort can adjudicate and a cross-sectional study cannot.

③ The sparse panel implies a siting problem: where must repeat-testing capacity exist for personal baselines to be usable?

Extending this paperThe paper's translation — a 20-protein clock and 21 proteins defining personal baselines — has an operational precondition it never states: a personal baseline requires at least two draws, years apart, from the same person. That is a health-system geography problem, not a biology problem. Extending the paper: take its own panel and simulate deployment, asking what fraction of a population can realistically accumulate a usable baseline given the actual spatial distribution of phlebotomy and cold-chain-capable facilities, and how much prognostic value is lost when the second draw is missing or badly timed. The paper's Extended Data Table 2 (participants by number and timing of assessments) is the seed of this analysis — 527 of the cohort gave all four samples, and the attrition pattern is itself informative about who baselines will and will not be available for.

Added value to my researchThis maps directly onto my 2SFCA/R2SFCA and facility-siting work, but with a twist I have not yet exploited: the accessibility target is repeat access over decades, not one-time proximity. That demands a longitudinal accessibility measure — supply-demand ratios evaluated over a person's residential history — and creates an efficiency-versus-equity siting objective directly analogous to the EV-charging optimization I have run, with baseline-coverage equity as the fairness term.

④ Treat deviation-from-baseline as a dynamical system on a protein graph, not a scalar in a Cox model

Extending this paperThe mortality result is obtained by putting a per-protein deviation term into age- and sex-adjusted Cox models with FDR correction — protein by protein, independently. Yet the paper's own framing is systemic homeostasis across metabolic, immune and tissue-remodelling axes, which is a statement about coupled dynamics. Extending it: model the 571 PHI > 0.7 proteins as nodes on a co-regulation graph and fit a continuous-time latent state model (neural ODE or state-space, which handles the paper's irregular 2002/2007/2012/2022 spacing natively) in which each person has a personal equilibrium and disease corresponds to drift away from it. Conservation-style constraints — total flux across coupled axes, monotone ageing drift — can be imposed as soft priors so the model extrapolates rather than memorizes, and attribution methods then say which axis destabilized first for a given person.

Added value to my researchThis is the same machinery as my physics-informed graph learning for spatial systems, with the adjacency redefined from geography to protein co-regulation — a rare chance to test whether the priors I use for spatial diffusion transfer to a biological network. The irregular-interval handling is directly reusable for my own sparsely sampled spatiotemporal panels (mobile-phone and social-media derived series), and the attribution layer is a clean explainable-ML case where the ground truth (mortality) is unambiguous.

⑤ Make the CMCS→UK Biobank transfer a formal transportability problem, and make the atlas a FAIR public asset

Extending this paperThe validation is a correlation between PHI computed in a Beijing SomaScan cohort and PHI computed in a UK Biobank Olink subcohort (SCC = 0.701 for the 782 high-IMC proteins, and only 0.119 for the 462 low-IMC ones). That honest split is also an admission: the paper cannot currently separate assay disagreement from genuine population difference in ancestry, environment and health system. Extending it: apply explicit transportability estimation — reweighting on the covariate distributions that differ between the two populations — to obtain a PHI estimate that is portable rather than merely correlated, and report per-protein transport error so downstream users know which of the 21 baseline proteins survive relocation. Since the CMCS-PP data sit behind a data-access committee and a cloud enclave, the natural companion is a federated re-estimation protocol plus a versioned, machine-readable PHI atlas keyed to UniProt IDs — the summary-level results already exist in Supplementary Information but only as a PDF/XLSX appendix.

Added value to my researchCross-population transportability is the biomedical twin of the problem I face constantly in GeoAI: a model trained on a few cities that must hold in an unseen city. Formalizing it here gives me a reusable reweighting recipe for transferring spatial accessibility and health-prediction models across regions, and the FAIR/enclave design question — how to publish a reusable resource when the raw data can never leave — is exactly the open-science architecture I want to standardize for my own restricted mobility and health datasets.
