Global analysis of country-level factors associated with chatbot usage for health

Philipp Schoenegger · Beatriz Costa-Gomes · Pavel Tolmachev · Lukas Wiedemann · Xiaoxuan Liu · Deborah Morgan · Viknesh Sounderajah · Christopher Kelly · Michael Bhaskar · Dominic King · Mustafa Suleyman Published 2026-07-15 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00174-2 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across 1.7 million de-identified Microsoft Copilot health conversations in 109 countries/regions, how much a country uses conversational AI for health and what it asks about have largely non-overlapping predictors. Usage intensity (health share of all conversations, 4.4%–15.35%, mean 8.7%) is most strongly associated with low population-level confidence in hospitals (r = −0.41, P < 0.001; β = −0.49 in the full model) — an institutional-trust signal, not a development signal. Composition instead follows a development and health-system gradient: richer, older countries shift from broad health-information and research queries toward specific symptom, navigation and paperwork intents, with the UHC index the single largest coefficient in the study (β = 0.91 for medical-paperwork share). The design is ecological and cross-sectional — associations describe countries, not individuals.

★

Why It Matters

  • Turns chatbot logs into a global health-system sensor. Consumer AI conversation logs become a near-real-time, 109-country readout of unmet health-information need — a digital-trace complement to surveys that arrive years late (the Wellcome trust items used here date from 2018).
  • Separates two questions that are usually conflated. "Who uses AI for health" and "what they use it for" are shown to be driven by different country-level machinery: trust drives volume, system structure drives content. Policy responses therefore diverge.
  • Reframes AI health use as a symptom of system friction. Higher AI health intensity where confidence in hospitals is lower, and more medical-paperwork queries where UHC is stronger, both read as AI absorbing the residual burden a health system leaves on its users — administrative in well-covered systems, informational in weaker ones.
  • Gives a deployment map, not just a description. If navigation and paperwork intents concentrate in wealthy, ageing, AI-ready countries while broad literacy queries concentrate elsewhere, the same chatbot needs materially different safety, localization and referral behaviour by market.
  • Honest about its ceiling. Ecological, cross-sectional, one vendor's platform, non-shareable data — the paper explicitly forbids individual-level or causal reading, which is itself a useful template for the coming wave of platform-log health studies.

Primary corpus
1.7 million health-related conversations sampled from consumer Microsoft Copilot (enterprise, educational and commercial accounts excluded). This is a subset of total Copilot traffic in the window, not the full population.
Time window
January–March 2026 (3 months).
Spatial coverage
109 countries and regions (ISO 3166-1), all World Bank income groups and all major world regions; inclusion cut-off = ≥1,000 health conversations per country over the 3 months. Regression sample drops to N = 93 under listwise deletion (16 countries excluded); bivariate correlations use N = 100–108.
Unit of analysis
Country/region only — all conversation-level data aggregated before analysis; no individual- or conversation-level modelling.

Health share of all Copilot conversations, I = H/T × 100. Observed range 4.4% to 15.35%, mean 8.7%, median 8.6%.
Outcome 2 — composition

The 8 kept categories cover >95% of health conversations; health information & education alone is 43%. Excluded: coverage & benefits, digital tools/fitness apps, other health/fitness, not-health (<5% combined).
Predictors
14 country-level indicators from 6 public sources, entered as 15 regressors (log GDP per capita enters linearly and quadratically) in three blocks: development/demographics, health system, institutional/trust.
Reference years
Most indicators 2021–2025, merged by "recent 5-year" rule (primary) with 3-year-recent, 3-year-average and 5-year-average as robustness merges (N = 70 / 36 / 74). Exception: Wellcome trust and confidence items are from 2018 — 8 years before the Copilot data, so they capture pre-existing attitudes.

Core Methods

  • Privacy-preserving trace construction: two-stage pipeline — automated PII scrubbing, then LLM summarization of each transcript into a short English description capturing topic and intent without reproducing user words. All analysis runs on the summaries under an "eyes-off" model.
  • Intent classification: each health conversation assigned to one of 12 validated categories; the 8 largest (>95% of volume) are retained and converted into country-level intent shares — a compositional outcome vector per country.
  • Two dependent constructs: intensity = health share of all conversations; composition = the 8-vector of intent shares. Both aggregated to country before any modelling (ecological design, stated explicitly).
  • Hierarchical OLS with standardization: all variables z-scored within the complete-case sample (N = 93); log GDP per capita plus a mean-centred quadratic term to admit nonlinearity; 15 regressors entered in three blocks with R² and ΔR² reported per block (intensity model: R² = 0.532, adj. R² = 0.441; block 3 adds the largest increment, ΔR² = 0.141).
  • Robust inference: HC3 heteroskedasticity-consistent standard errors chosen over HC0 because HC3 performs well at small N (justified via Long & Ervin); all tests two-sided; bivariate Pearson correlations reported alongside multivariate coefficients for transparency.
  • Multiplicity control: Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across the eight intent-share regressions, with PFDR reported.
  • Temporal merge sensitivity: four rules for collapsing multi-year indicators to one cross-sectional value (recent-3y, recent-5y, average-3y, average-5y); recent-5y selected as primary because it maximizes retained N.
  • Diagnostics: variance inflation factors for multicollinearity, Cook's distance for influential countries, leave-one-country-out sensitivity, plus a 2020-vs-2018 substitution test for the Wellcome confidence measure.
  • Pre-declared robustness rubric: a coefficient counts as robust only if significant at P < 0.05 under HC3 and (intensity) same-direction significant in ≥1 alternative merge, or (intent shares) surviving FDR. Non-qualifying coefficients are reported but explicitly flagged — hence "strong" evidence for composition, "moderate" for the intensity–trust link.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this paper: first how the paper itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, telehealth & digital divides, 2SFCA/R2SFCA accessibility, explainable spatial ML, multi-source data fusion).

① Break the country pixel: subnational downscaling of AI health-seeking

Extending this paperThe paper's entire result surface lives at one resolution — the nation — and it says so plainly ("they describe countries and regions, not the individuals within them"). But confidence in hospitals, physician density and UHC all vary more within large countries than between many small ones; a single national intensity value for Brazil or India averages away exactly the gradient of interest. The extension: recover the sub-national geography of the same signal by disaggregating conversation counts to first-level administrative units (or to the coarsest geohash the privacy pipeline can survive), then re-run the identical three-block model with subnational analogues of the 14 predictors. Two payoffs — an ecological-fallacy stress test of whether the trust→intensity coefficient reproduces within countries, and a map that policy actors can actually act on. Where subnational conversation counts cannot be released, a spatial small-area estimation layer (national totals as constraints, subnational covariates as predictors) can produce credible-interval-bearing estimates rather than nothing.

Added value to my researchThis is my modifiable-areal-unit and downscaling problem in a new data type. It gives me a route to treat AI health-query intensity as a spatial demand surface — a proxy for unmet primary-care need that can be plugged directly into R2SFCA as a demand weight, replacing the population-count denominator that assumes every resident needs care equally. Pairing it with mobile-phone-derived hospital utilization would let me test whether high AI health-seeking marks the same neighbourhoods as depressed hospital utilization — a "digital substitution" indicator for accessibility deserts.

② Replace the 2018 trust survey with a continuously measurable trust proxy

Extending this paperThe study's headline predictor is its weakest link by the authors' own accounting: confidence in hospitals comes from the Wellcome Global Monitor 2018, eight years before the Copilot window, and the 2020 substitution test points the same way but loses significance. That single lag is probably why the intensity finding is graded "moderate" while composition is "strong". The extension: build a contemporaneous, continuously updated institutional-trust index from the conversations themselves — the intent taxonomy already separates second-opinion-seeking, symptom re-checking and navigation-around-the-system behaviours that are behavioural expressions of low trust. Validate that constructed index against the 2018/2020 Wellcome items where both exist, then use it as the time-varying predictor. That converts a cross-sectional, stale covariate into a monthly panel, and opens the door to a lagged/panel design where causal direction is at least arguable rather than disclaimed.

Added value to my researchTrust is the missing latent variable in almost all of my accessibility work: 2SFCA assumes people use the nearest available supply, which is false wherever the nearest facility is distrusted. A behaviourally derived, spatially resolvable trust index becomes a third dimension of accessibility alongside availability and proximity — a "willingness-to-use" multiplier on supply. It also gives my telehealth/digital-divide line an instrument: places where digital health-seeking runs hot while facility trust runs cold are precisely where telehealth substitution is doing hidden work.

Cross-ref (corpus): A global survey on trust, digital health literacy and health information quality — 10.1038/s44360-026-00102-4

③ From 15 OLS coefficients to an explainable, interaction-aware model of the intent mix

Extending this paperThe paper models eight compositional outcomes with eight separate linear OLS fits over 15 partly collinear predictors at N = 93 — a setting where a quadratic GDP term is the only concession to nonlinearity and where "many correlated development indicators share explanatory power without any one dominating" (their words about the 43%-share information-and-education intent). Two principled upgrades. First, treat the eight shares as what they are — a composition summing to one — and model them jointly with a Dirichlet-regression or additive-log-ratio approach, so predictors trade shares against each other instead of eight fits pretending independence. Second, fit a gradient-boosted ensemble with SHAP interaction values alongside the OLS: at N = 93 this is a descriptive, not predictive, exercise, but it would show whether the UHC→paperwork effect is a main effect or an interaction with ageing and AI readiness — a question the coefficient plot in Fig. 3 cannot answer.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean, small-N testbed for my explainable-spatial-ML methodology: compositional outcomes plus collinear socioeconomic drivers is exactly the structure of land-use mix, mode split and facility-type mix problems I work on. Getting the SHAP-on-compositional-outcomes workflow right here — with FDR-style multiplicity discipline borrowed from this paper — transfers directly to my MGWR-plus-ML pipelines, where I routinely need to defend nonlinear attributions on samples of a few hundred spatial units.

④ Close the loop: does answering the query change the health-system outcome?

Extending this paperThe paper stops at association between national context and query behaviour; it never observes what happens after the conversation. Yet its own composition finding implies a testable service model — where UHC is strong, users bring administrative burden to the chatbot; where trust is weak, they bring diagnostic uncertainty. The extension: pair this observational country map with randomized deployments in contrasting quadrants of it (high-intensity/low-trust vs low-intensity/high-UHC), measuring whether an intent-tailored assistant shifts downstream utilization, appointment completion or paperwork resolution. That upgrades the paper from a cross-sectional description into a site-selection instrument for trials — its intensity and intent maps become the sampling frame that tells you where a given intervention should be expected to bite.

Added value to my researchThis is the design I want for telehealth equity work: use a spatial demand surface to choose intervention sites, then evaluate with a geographically stratified randomization instead of convenience samples. It also gives me a way to connect my service-area delineation methods to outcomes — if I can define catchments where AI health-seeking is high and facility access is low, those catchments become the natural randomization units for a digital-first primary-care intervention.

⑤ Audit the measurement instrument itself: the classifier is a geographic confounder

Extending this paperEvery outcome in this study is produced by an LLM pipeline — scrub, summarize into English, classify into 12 intents at 84% exact-match accuracy — and the paper defends its cross-linguistic validity by citing general LLM multilinguality rather than by measuring it. That is a real exposure: if classifier recall for "healthcare navigation" is systematically lower in lower-resource languages, the observed development gradient in intent composition is partly an instrument gradient, and the effect would point in exactly the direction reported. The extension: run a stratified, per-language validation with clinician annotators, publish per-language confusion matrices, and propagate that measurement error into the regressions (errors-in-variables or a simulation-extrapolation correction) so each β carries a classifier-uncertainty band. Report how much of the GDP gradient survives.

Added value to my researchThis is the reproducibility and FAIR-data question I care about, in its hardest form: neither the data nor the code can be released, so the only auditable surface is the measurement instrument. Formalizing an instrument-bias audit protocol for LLM-derived variables — per-stratum validation, error propagation into downstream spatial models, uncertainty bands on coefficients — is directly reusable when I use foundation models to label remote sensing, POIs or social-media text before feeding them into spatial regressions. Unaudited AI-derived features are already the biggest silent risk in GeoAI pipelines, mine included.
