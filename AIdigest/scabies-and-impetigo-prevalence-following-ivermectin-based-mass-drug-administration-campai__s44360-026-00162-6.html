Scabies and impetigo prevalence following ivermectin-based mass drug administration campaigns in Solomon Islands and Fiji

Hannah Andrews · Susanna Lake · Sarah Andersson · Aminiasi Tavui · Kelera Matavesi · Aminiasi Koroivueta · Josefa Koroivueta · Rejieli Vuniduvu · Mareta Vuki · Varanisese Rosa · Julie Zinihite · June Tavalia · Ayleen Sosopu · Johnina Huniehu · Sanira Ghemu · Donald Rotu · Matthew Parnaby · Tessa Hughes · Anneke Grobler · Daniel Engelman · Lucia Romani · Meciusela Tuicakau · Mike Kama · John Kaldor · Aalisha Sahukhan · Oliver Sokana · Andrew Steer Published 2026-07-29 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00162-6 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

The first real-world, nationwide delivery of ivermectin-based mass drug administration (MDA) for scabies produced large prevalence reductions in two Pacific Island countries. In Solomon Islands, after two annual rounds, scabies fell from 23.2% to 3.9% (relative risk reduction 83.3%) and impetigo from 9.2% to 1.6% (RRR 82.1%). In Fiji, after a single round, scabies fell from 6.2% to 3.4% (RRR 44.2%) and impetigo from 4.3% to 1.2%. Crucially, benefit appeared both above and below the WHO 10% MDA threshold, but a residual burden persists — prevalence remains above 10% in children under 5 in both countries, and coverage (not biology) was the binding constraint in dense urban areas.

Why It Matters

  • Moves scabies control from trial to national program. Previous ivermectin MDA evidence came from single-arm trials in populations of 2,000–30,000. This is the first published report of national-scale delivery of scabies MDA as routine public health practice, in two countries, with independent before-and-after population surveys.
  • Validates — and stretches — the WHO 10% threshold. Solomon Islands (baseline 23.2%) sat well above the threshold where MDA is strongly recommended; Fiji (6.2%) sat below it and still saw a 44% relative reduction. That supplies the missing evidence for the 2–9% prevalence band, where policy has been silent.
  • Confirms the scabies → impetigo → invasive-disease causal chain at population scale. Impetigo fell alongside scabies — a reported relative risk reduction of 82.1% in Solomon Islands, and from 4.3% to 1.2% in Fiji — supporting the mechanism by which scabies control should reduce downstream streptococcal renal and rheumatic heart disease.
  • Names the real bottleneck: urban coverage, not drug efficacy. Fiji's national first-dose coverage was only 26% and impact was minimal in the urban Central Division; Solomon Islands lifted Honiara to 84% in round 2 via workplace, school and gathering-place delivery, and saw substantial reduction there. Delivery geography is the lever.
  • Equity signal is explicit. Burden and residual burden concentrate in children under 5, rural residents, and (in Fiji) the iTaukei population — precisely the groups that a coverage-blind national average would hide.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Study design
Population-based, cross-sectional, before-and-after national prevalence surveys (non-randomized, no comparator arm), nested in ministry-led MDA campaigns supported by the World Scabies Program.
Participants surveyed
Solomon Islands: 9,170 (baseline, Sep 2021) · 10,060 (post-MDA1, Oct 2023) · 9,730 (post-MDA2, Apr–May 2025), 90 clusters at each time point.
Fiji: 10,866 (baseline, 86 clusters) · 9,696 (post-MDA, 90 clusters, Feb–Jun 2024). Fiji baseline splits into 6,614 from 70 clusters (Central/Western/Eastern, Nov 2021–Mar 2022) and 4,252 from 16 clusters (Northern Division, Jul 2020, reused from the Big SHIFT trial). Sum across the five survey rounds ≈ 49,522 individual skin examinations (computed from the reported per-round n).
Sampling frame
Two-stage random cluster sampling within evaluation units — 9 evaluation units in Solomon Islands (provinces, with Rennell & Bellona merged into Honiara City Council) and 9 in Fiji (divisions/subdivisions, each ~100,000–250,000 people). Stage 1: clusters (villages, informal settlements, urban administrative units) drawn with probability proportional to population size using ENA/SMART randomization software. Stage 2: random households; all household members invited.
Intervention exposure
Oral ivermectin 200 μg kg−1, two doses 7–14 days apart per round; 5% permethrin cream substituted for people under 90 cm in height, pregnant women, women breastfeeding infants <7 days, severely ill people and those on warfarin. Solomon Islands: round 1 Jul 2022–Mar 2023, round 2 Feb–Dec 2024. Fiji: one round Oct 2022–Jun 2023.
Coverage data
Individual-level MDA registration records collated by distribution teams, denominated against national census. Solomon Islands first-dose coverage: 49% (round 1), 68% (round 2), with Honiara reaching 84% in round 2. Fiji first-dose coverage: 26%, varying by division. Per-province/division breakdowns are in Extended Data Tables 1 and 6.
Census denominators
Solomon Islands 2019 Population and Housing Census (National Statistics Office) and Fiji 2017 Population and Housing Census (Fiji Bureau of Statistics) — used both for coverage denominators and for weighting the two-part Fiji baseline.
Case definition
Clinical examination by trained non-expert nurses using the simplified 2020 IACS criteria, limited to hands, forearms, feet and lower legs. No dermoscopy, skin scrapings or microscopy. Impetigo defined as papular/pustular/ulcerative lesions with erythema, including infected scabies.
Collection platform
REDCap mobile app (versions 3, 4 and 5) on Android tablets; demographics captured were age, date of birth, sex and ethnicity.
Time window
Program inception Jan 2020 → Jun 2025 (Fig. 2 timeline). Post-MDA surveys conducted 6–12 months after each round; follow-up beyond 1 year has not yet been assessed.
Geographic data
Only cartographic — Fig. 1 basemaps from Free Vector Maps (Solomon Islands) and Adobe Stock (Fiji). Honest note: no gridded population, satellite, POI or GPS dataset is used; there are no cluster-level coordinates published, and no household-level geolocation is reported.
Availability
Data are not deposited openly: available on request to the corresponding author, subject to approvals, data-use agreements, source permissions and cost recovery, managed under Murdoch Children's Research Institute processes (requests actioned within 3 months). Analysis code likewise available on reasonable request. Not FAIR-compliant in the open-data sense.

Core Methods

  • Before-and-after national prevalence estimation: independent population-based cross-sectional surveys at baseline and 6–12 months after each MDA round; clusters and households re-randomized independently at each round, so the design is repeated cross-section rather than a panel.
  • Two-stage cluster sampling with PPS: clusters drawn with probability proportional to population size within each evaluation unit (ENA/SMART), then random household selection with whole-household enrolment — giving each individual an equal inclusion probability regardless of cluster size.
  • Standardized non-expert clinical diagnosis: nurses completed a 3-day training program (modified from a previously evaluated program) and applied simplified IACS 2020 criteria to a restricted body-site examination — a deliberate trade of sensitivity for field scalability, applied identically at all time points.
  • Cluster-adjusted inference: all prevalence estimates and 95% CIs adjusted for village-level clustering via a cluster-adjusted regression model; robust standard errors clustered at village level to absorb intra-cluster correlation.
  • Effect estimation via binomial GLMs: absolute risk reduction from a GLM with identity link + binomial distribution; relative risk reduction from a GLM with log link + binomial distribution (Wacholder-style binomial regression).
  • Bootstrap CIs for RRR: non-parametric bootstrap with 1,000 replicates resampled by village cluster, preserving the cluster structure.
  • Census-based reweighting: Fiji's heterogeneous baseline (Northern Division sample twice the size of others) reweighted using national census population shares; otherwise no sampling weights, since PPS + whole-household enrolment already approximates self-weighting.
  • Coverage estimation: individual MDA registration records aggregated to province/division and divided by the most recent census population (2019 SI, 2017 Fiji) — first-dose coverage is the reported metric.
  • Software: all analysis in Stata 18.0; field data capture in REDCap.
  • Stratified subgroup analysis: prevalence broken out by age group, sex, urban/rural location, administrative unit, ethnicity (Fiji), and severity (moderate-to-severe scabies) — the stratification that reveals the residual under-5 and rural burden.

① Turn coverage–impact from a province-level table into a continuous spatial dose–response surface

Extending this paperThe paper's most policy-relevant relationship — how much prevalence reduction you buy per percentage point of MDA coverage — is currently only legible by eyeballing Extended Data Tables 1 and 6, i.e. 9 provinces and 4 divisions. Yet the underlying data are far finer: individual MDA registration records exist, and 90 survey clusters per round carry village identifiers. Extending the paper means geocoding cluster and registration records to build a cluster-level coverage → post-MDA prevalence dose–response function, estimated with a spatially varying coefficient model (MGWR) so the marginal return on coverage is allowed to differ between Honiara's dense settlements and Malaita's dispersed villages. That would answer the question the paper poses but cannot resolve: is 68% national coverage sufficient everywhere, or does the required threshold itself scale with settlement density? Transportability methods for MDA effects are the natural statistical scaffolding — they extrapolate a measured intervention effect into unsampled localities using local covariates rather than assuming homogeneity.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean template for the explainable-spatial-ML line: a low-dimensional, policy-legible outcome (prevalence) regressed on an intervention intensity (coverage) with spatially non-stationary coefficients plus SHAP attribution. It lets me test whether MGWR bandwidth carries epidemiological meaning — the bandwidth becomes an estimate of the spatial radius of herd protection, not a tuning artifact. It also gives my accessibility work a rare outcome-validated case: unlike most 2SFCA studies, here there is a measured health outcome to calibrate an access metric against.

② Recast urban MDA delivery as a facility-location and catchment-optimization problem

Extending this paperThe paper identifies the failure mode precisely: rural village structures enabled high coverage, while Fiji's urban Central Division managed only fragmentary coverage and minimal disease reduction; Solomon Islands fixed this in Honiara ad hoc — workplaces, schools, gathering places — and hit 84%. But "we added delivery points" is a narrative, not a design. Extending the paper: formalize campaign delivery as a capacitated maximal-coverage location problem over the urban street network, where candidate sites are workplaces, schools, markets and health posts, each with a service capacity and a distance-decay participation curve estimated from the registration records themselves. Solve for the minimum number and placement of delivery points needed to reach 80% coverage in Honiara, Suva and Lautoka, and — because two doses 7–14 days apart are required — extend to a two-visit routing problem where the second-dose return schedule is part of the optimization. Access-inequity mapping in dispersed low-income conurbations offers the methodological analogue.

Added value to my researchDirectly reuses my EV-charging-station efficiency-vs-equity optimization machinery in a public-health guise: swap charging demand for treatment-eligible population, swap queueing capacity for distributor throughput, and the same Pareto-front analysis yields an efficiency–equity frontier for campaign siting. It also extends R2SFCA into a genuinely temporal form — a campaign catchment that exists only during a delivery window and must be visited twice is a moving-target accessibility problem my static formulations do not yet handle.

③ Build a resurgence-forecasting metapopulation model for the post-MDA residual burden

Extending this paperThe paper is explicit about its temporal blind spot — follow-up stopped at 12 months, so "sustained long-term impact has not yet been assessed" — and about its residual: scabies prevalence still exceeds 10% in under-5s in both countries even after successful campaigns. Those two facts together define the most urgent extension: a metapopulation SIS/SIR transmission model in which villages are nodes, household contact intensity sets within-node transmission, and inter-island passenger movement sets between-node seeding, calibrated to the three Solomon Islands time points and the two Fiji points. Under-5s enter as a high-transmissibility reservoir compartment rather than merely a reported stratum. Such a model converts the paper's cross-sections into a forecast: when and where does prevalence cross back over 10%, and does targeted re-treatment of the under-5 reservoir beat another whole-population round? Fine-grained exposure measurement — as in wearable-GPS studies of water contact for schistosomiasis — shows how the contact term could be measured rather than assumed.

Added value to my researchThis is my SIR/agent-based modeling line applied to a disease whose transmission is genuinely contact-geographic (household crowding, shared bedding) rather than airborne — a much better testbed for spatiotemporal kernel density estimation of transmission intensity than respiratory diseases, because the kernel bandwidth has a physical interpretation in metres and sleeping arrangements. Coupling an ABM with a network community-detection step on inter-island mobility would let me identify MDA "elimination units" — sets of villages that must be treated together to prevent reinfection leakage — a spatial-community concept I have applied to job-housing networks and can transfer wholesale.

④ Fuse earth observation with the survey clusters to build a housing-and-crowding risk surface

Extending this paperThe paper repeatedly invokes overcrowding and resource-poor living conditions as the driver of scabies transmission, and finds rural residence and iTaukei ethnicity to be risk markers — but it never measures housing directly. Every risk covariate in the analysis is an administrative label. Extending the paper: attach to each of the ~90 survey clusters a set of remotely sensed built-environment covariates — building footprint density, mean structure area, roof material class, settlement compactness, distance to the nearest health facility — derived from high-resolution imagery, then fit an interpretable gradient-boosting or GAM model predicting cluster prevalence. This converts "rural" and "ethnicity" from proxies into mechanism, and produces a predictive risk surface for unsurveyed villages — of which Solomon Islands has thousands across 990+ islands. Continental-scale mapping of housing change from satellite data demonstrates that the covariates are extractable at exactly this resolution and in exactly these settings.

Added value to my researchTextbook multi-source data fusion for my GeoAI agenda, but with an unusual virtue: a ground-truth health outcome measured by clinical examination, not self-report or claims data. That makes it a strong benchmark for fine-tuning a geospatial foundation model — I can ask whether embeddings from a pretrained EO backbone outperform hand-engineered morphology metrics at predicting a disease driven by physical crowding. If they do, it is one of the cleanest demonstrations I could offer that foundation-model embeddings encode socially meaningful built-environment structure.

⑤ Redesign the monitoring architecture: decision-aware adaptive surveys instead of exhaustive re-enumeration

Extending this paperEach evaluation round cost roughly 10,000 in-person skin examinations across 90 clusters spread over hundreds of dispersed islands — and the decision it ultimately informed was binary: run another MDA round, or stop. Fiji's ministry chose to stop after seeing 3.4%. That is an enormous measurement budget spent to resolve a single threshold crossing, and the paper concedes the simplified IACS criteria "have not been validated in settings with lower prevalence" — precisely the regime where the stop/continue decision is being made. Extending the paper: replace fixed cluster counts with decision-aware adaptive sampling, in which cluster allocation at each round maximizes the expected value of information for the stop/continue decision rather than minimizing variance of a national point estimate, with a Bayesian spatial model carrying prior information forward between rounds. Pair this with lightweight complementary signals — clinic dispensing records, community-health-worker reports, or participatory digital surveillance — to flag re-emergence between expensive survey rounds. Decision-aware ML for health-commodity allocation formalizes the "optimize the decision, not the prediction" objective; digital participatory surveillance demonstrates the cheap complementary signal.

Added value to my researchThis closes the loop between my open-science/FAIR line and my methods line. On the FAIR side, the paper's data are request-only with a 3-month review — a concrete case for arguing that cluster-level aggregate releases with coordinates jittered to protect villages would enable exactly the reanalysis proposed here at negligible disclosure risk. On the methods side, "where should I measure next?" is the same active-learning problem I face in telehealth and mental-health accessibility studies, where survey coverage is sparse and non-random; a scabies MDA program with five completed rounds is a rare setting where the adaptive design can be retrospectively validated against what full enumeration actually found.
