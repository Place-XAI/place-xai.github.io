Global impact and cost-effectiveness of tuberculosis interventions

Katherine C. Horton · Alvaro Schwalb · Martin J. Harker · Lara Goscé · Elena Venero-Garcia · Lily O'Brien · Arkaprabha Gun · Tom Sumner · C. Finn McQuaid · Rebecca A. Clark · Tomos O. Prys-Jones · Roel Bakker · Yiran E. Liu · Mmamapudi Kubjane · Christian Lienhardt · Richard G. White · Rein M. G. J. Houben Published 2026-07-21 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00171-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Under a single standardized comparison across Brazil, India and South Africa, only three of seven TB interventions avert more than 10% of incident TB episodes by 2050: symptom-agnostic community-wide screening (median 31–38%), prevention-of-disease vaccination (15–28%) and annual mass screening in prisons in Brazil (23%). Everything aimed only at clinic-diagnosed patients and their households — improved diagnosis, universal DST, TPT, shorter regimens, nutritional support — averts 0–5%, because the size of the target population, not the efficacy of the tool, sets a hard ceiling on population impact. High impact is not the same as high cost: shorter drug-resistant treatment is cost-saving, prison screening costs US$146 per DALY and nutritional support US$281 per DALY, so impactful interventions can be affordable — but only if programmes reach beyond the clinic door.

★

Why It Matters

  • It makes seven very different interventions directly comparable for the first time in a decade. Prevention, screening/diagnosis and treatment are normally evaluated in separate literatures with separate assumptions. Here start year (2025), scale-up shape (linear 2025–2030), coverage (80%) and evidence standard (published empirical efficacy only) are held identical, so the ranking reflects epidemiology rather than modelling convention.
  • It reframes impact as a population-attributable-fraction problem. The paper's central mechanism is blunt: if an intervention is restricted to clinic-diagnosed individuals or their household contacts, the target population imposes a hard ceiling on population-level benefit no matter how high the within-group burden or how good the product.
  • It puts structural drivers on the same balance sheet as biomedical products. Incarceration (PAF 36.9% in Brazil), undernutrition (PAF 13–45.2% in India) and HIV (PAF 49% in South Africa) are modelled explicitly — and the two interventions that address them (prison screening, RATIONS-style nutritional support) show the lowest positive ICERs of the whole portfolio.
  • It changes what a target product profile should optimize. A shorter DS-TB regimen that is merely non-inferior averts ~0% of episodes yet costs more; the paper argues TPPs must specify desired population-level impact, not just individual benefit.
  • Coverage is non-linear and that matters for austerity budgets. Vaccination, community-wide screening or prison screening delivered at only 20% coverage still avert more episodes than any other intervention at 80% coverage — a directly actionable finding under the current global TB funding contraction.
  • Fully open and reproducible. Data and analysis scripts are on OSF and GitHub (lshtm-tbmg/PACE-TB), making the comparison auditable and re-runnable for other country archetypes.

This is a modelling paper: there is no primary field data collection. The "data" are (a) calibration targets from public surveillance, (b) efficacy estimates from named trials, and (c) unit costs from country cost studies. All figures below are stated in the article; where the article does not give an exact number, that is said explicitly.

Geographic scope
Three country archetypes — Brazil, India, South Africa, chosen to span burden and structural drivers. Estimated 2023 TB incidence across them ranges 49–427 per 100,000. Brazil and South Africa are upper-middle-income; India is lower-middle-income (low-income settings are explicitly not covered).
Interventions
Seven interventions spanning three domains — prevention (prevention-of-disease vaccine; TPT), screening & diagnosis (symptom-agnostic community-wide screening; improved clinic diagnostics; DST for all clinic-diagnosed), treatment (shorter DS-TB regimen; shorter DR-TB regimen) — plus two country-specific structural-driver interventions: annual prison screening (Brazil) and nationwide RATIONS-style nutritional support (India).
Simulation volume
ABC-MCMC identified ≥25,000 parameter sets consistent with all calibration targets; 500 were randomly selected per country and each intervention was run across all 500, giving paired impact estimates. 500 cost sets per country were sampled from gamma distributions (±50% around point estimates) and randomly matched to parameter sets. 95% uncertainty intervals are 2.5th–97.5th percentiles.
Time horizon
Calibration to 2010 and 2023 indicators; models run to 2025 then projected under BAU vs intervention to 2050. Scale-up 2025–2030; indirect effects accumulated 2030–2050. Community-wide screening: 5-year scale-up + five annual campaigns, then cessation (~2035). Vaccination: routine ages 15–19 plus adult catch-up campaigns at scale-up and in 2040.
Model resolution
Age-stratified in 5-year bands to 75+; separate child (<15 y) and adult natural-history parameters; three disease states (sTB, aTB, nTB) aligned to WHO recategorization; drug-susceptible vs rifampicin-resistant strata; treatment-history strata. Structural strata: incarceration (never / current / released within 7 years, Brazil), BMI (<17, 17–18.4, 18.5–24.9, >25 kg m⁻², India), HIV (negative / positive / positive-on-ART, South Africa).
Calibration targets
WHO-reported incidence, notifications and mortality (2010, 2023); prevalence and aTB proportion where available (India, South Africa); plus driver-specific targets — incarceration rate and prison notification rate (Brazil), PAF for BMI <18.5 (India), HIV-stratified incidence/notification/mortality (South Africa).
Efficacy evidence
Published trials only: M72/AS01E vaccine final analysis (NEJM 2019); RATIONS nutritional cluster-RCT (Lancet 2023); TB-PRACTECAL short oral DR-TB regimen (Lancet Respir Med 2024); 4-month rifapentine DS-TB regimens (NEJM 2021); community-wide screening trial (NEJM 2019); Brazilian serial prison mass screening (CID 2024); Xpert Ultra vs Xpert MTB/RIF Cochrane review.
Economics
Health-system perspective; all costs in 2023 USD; 5% annual discount on costs and benefits. Unit costs from WHO-CHOICE, Brazilian prison screening cost study, Costs of TB Services in India, mobile digital X-ray cost study (India), the South Africa National TB Cost Model (HE²RO, Wits) and Stop TB Partnership GDF budgeting prices Q1-2025. Cost-effectiveness thresholds follow Ochalek et al. updated to 2023 GDP: Brazil US$6,968–10,735, India US$413–569, South Africa US$2,444–3,285 per DALY.
Auxiliary inputs
Contact/mixing from Prem et al. projected contact matrices (177 regions); HIV/ART projections from UNAIDS; India BMI-distribution trajectory from a published projection; India nutrition strata informed by NFHS-5 (2019–21); disability weights from GBD 2021; post-TB DALYs from Menzies et al. 2021.
Compute
Cirrus UK National Tier-2 HPC (EPCC) and the EuroHPC "Discoverer" supercomputer (Sofia Tech Park) — a signal of the calibration cost, though total core-hours are not reported.
Availability
Data and analysis scripts: OSF.IO/ZYKSD and github.com/lshtm-tbmg/PACE-TB. No human participants; all parameterization from published/public sources.
Honest unknowns
The article does not report modelled population sizes, number of simulated individuals (it is a deterministic compartmental model, not agent-based), total core-hours, or the number of compartments. Absolute budget-impact values are flagged by the authors as uncertain because some health-system costs may be omitted and patient costs are excluded. Sub-national spatial variation is not modelled at all — each country is a single well-mixed (age/driver-stratified) population.

Core Methods

  • Deterministic dynamic transmission model: an age-stratified compartmental model of TB natural history extending existing LSHTM TB vaccine-modelling infrastructure, with three disease states — symptomatic (sTB), asymptomatic bacteriologically-positive (aTB) and bacteriologically-negative non-infectious (nTB) — plus self-clearance of M. tuberculosis infection, regression and recovery. Both sTB and aTB transmit; nTB does not (Fig. 5).
  • Structural-driver stratification: the model is subdivided by the country's dominant structural determinant — incarceration status in Brazil (with assortative mixing and strongly increased within-prison contact to represent overcrowding), BMI category in India, HIV/ART status in South Africa — so that "who is at risk" and "who an intervention reaches" are represented jointly.
  • Explicit diagnostic pathways: clinic- and community-based screening/diagnosis pathways are modelled including people without TB entering the pathway, with test positivity specified per model state — which is what allows false-positive burden and diagnostic cost to enter the economics honestly.
  • Bayesian calibration: ABC-MCMC with a variable step size, followed by manual fitting, against WHO incidence/notification/mortality (2010, 2023), prevalence and aTB proportion where available, and driver-specific targets; ≥25,000 accepted sets, 500 sampled per country. Post-2025, BAU holds parameters constant except UNAIDS HIV/ART projections (South Africa) and projected BMI shifts (India).
  • Standardized intervention protocol: identical start (2025), linear scale-up to 2030, 80% coverage of the target population, and inclusion only where published empirical efficacy exists — deliberately excluding speculative products (e.g. pan-TB regimens) so the comparison is not driven by optimistic assumptions.
  • Paired costing model: a costing layer linking intervention activities and epidemiological outcomes to costs (including altered demand for existing diagnosis/treatment services), 2023 USD, health-system perspective, 5% discounting, 500 gamma-sampled cost sets (±50%) randomly matched to epidemiological parameter sets, with high- and low-cost diagnostic scenarios for community-wide screening.
  • Outcome metrics: incident sTB episodes averted, prevalence and mortality reduction, DALYs averted (including post-TB DALYs), total budget impact, incremental cost, and ICER vs BAU — with the probability of cost-effectiveness computed as the share of the 500 paired simulations falling below the country threshold.
  • Sensitivity analyses: coverage lowered to 50% and 20% (revealing non-linear impact vs near-linear cost scaling), and a common-natural-history variant that ignores country calibration to isolate how much between-country difference comes from parameterization rather than structure.

① Give the national compartments a geography: spatially explicit PAF and sub-national ICERs

Extending this paperThe model's most consequential simplification is that each country is a single well-mixed population stratified only by age and one structural driver. But the paper's own conclusion — that impact is governed by the population attributable fraction of the group you can reach — is intrinsically a spatial statement: incarceration burden concentrates in specific prison catchments, undernutrition in specific Indian districts, HIV in specific South African municipalities. A direct extension is a metapopulation version of PACE-TB, one patch per district, coupled by commuting/mobility flows, with district-level PAF driving where community-wide screening is deployed. The prediction is sharp and testable: because impact scales sub-linearly with coverage (Fig. 4), geographically targeted 20–30% coverage in high-PAF districts should dominate uniform 80% national coverage on cost per DALY — turning the paper's single national ICER into an ICER surface. Brazil's national community-wide-screening ICER of US$2,509 per DALY is almost certainly an average over districts spanning an order of magnitude.

Added value to my researchThis is my core competence applied to a live global-health question. The district-level "who can we reach" term is exactly a 2SFCA/R2SFCA supply-demand ratio: screening vans, X-ray units and GeneXpert instruments are the supply, high-PAF populations are the demand, and the distance-decay function governs realized coverage. It gives me a route to publish accessibility methods in an epidemiological-impact currency (DALYs averted per dollar) rather than the usual proximity index, and it makes my spatiotemporal KDE and network community-detection tooling directly relevant to defining screening catchments.

② Replace the assumed contact structure with mobility-derived, driver-aware mixing

Extending this paperThe transmission engine rests on Prem et al. synthetic contact matrices plus a hand-tuned assortativity bump for incarcerated individuals to represent overcrowding. That single tuned parameter carries enormous weight: prison screening averts 23% of Brazil's episodes precisely because the model believes prisons are hyper-connected transmission amplifiers. Extending the paper means replacing assumed mixing with empirically derived contact structure — mobility-driven synthetic contact matrices, prison-transfer administrative networks, and release-to-community flows (the model already tracks a "released within 7 years" stratum but not where people are released to). One could then ask a question the current model cannot: how much of the prison-screening benefit is intramural versus spillover into surrounding communities, and does that change the ICER of US$146 per DALY?

It also sharpens a methodological line I care about: quantifying how much epidemiological-conclusion variance is attributable to mixing assumptions, an explainability question about model inputs rather than model outputs, and a natural companion to my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction work.

③ A surrogate emulator so the portfolio comparison runs everywhere, not in three countries

Extending this paperThe comparison cost two national HPC allocations (Cirrus and EuroHPC Discoverer) to cover three countries — which is why the paper reasons by "archetypes" rather than by coverage. The natural extension is to train a neural surrogate / emulator on the existing 500 parameter sets × 500 cost sets × 9 interventions × 3 coverage levels already generated, mapping (calibration targets, driver PAFs, unit costs) → (episodes averted, DALYs averted, ICER). With a validated emulator, the same standardized portfolio comparison could be produced for all 30 WHO high-burden countries in minutes, with prediction intervals, and re-run whenever WHO updates its estimates. The emulator also permits inverse queries the current design cannot answer: what unit cost would a screening algorithm need to hit for community-wide screening to become cost-effective in a 50-per-100,000 setting?

Added value to my researchThis is a textbook case for the surrogate-modelling and foundation-model fine-tuning strand of my work — and crucially, an emulator with a SHAP/explainability layer would surface which structural parameters actually drive the ICER ranking, converting an opaque mechanistic model into an interpretable decision tool. It also gives me a well-posed benchmark problem for physics-informed learning: the emulator must respect known monotonicities (impact non-decreasing in coverage, cost increasing in coverage) as hard constraints.

④ Add an equity axis: the paper optimizes efficiency, not who benefits

Extending this paperICERs are a pure efficiency criterion — a DALY averted counts the same whoever it belongs to. Yet this paper's two cheapest interventions target the most marginalized populations imaginable (incarcerated people, undernourished households), and its most impactful intervention (community-wide screening) is precisely the one whose realized coverage will be most unequal in practice. The paper assumes 80% coverage is uniformly achievable and explicitly sets aside operational constraints. A serious extension replaces the scalar ICER with a joint efficiency–equity frontier: model coverage as a function of access rather than a fixed target, then trace the Pareto surface between DALYs averted and the concentration index of who receives the benefit. Some interventions will move sharply — universal DST looks harmless on efficiency grounds but, if uptake tracks clinic access, may widen the gap it is meant to close.

Added value to my researchThis is the same efficiency-versus-equity trade-off I formalized for EV charging-station siting, transplanted to health interventions with a far higher-stakes objective. It is also the natural bridge to my telehealth/digital-divide work: coverage-as-a-function-of-access is precisely the mechanism by which a technically excellent intervention produces inequitable outcomes, and it can be estimated from the same accessibility surfaces I already build.

⑤ From one-at-a-time comparison to sequenced portfolios under a real budget constraint

Extending this paperThe authors flag this limitation themselves: every intervention is compared to BAU in isolation, with no combinations and no synergies. But their own results imply strong interaction — community-wide screening's benefit decays after campaigns stop in 2035 (Fig. 1), which is exactly the gap vaccination's 2040 catch-up campaign could fill; and shorter DS-TB treatment, worthless alone, becomes valuable when community screening floods the system with newly diagnosed cases. Extending the paper: pose it as a sequential budget-constrained portfolio optimization — given an annual envelope 2025–2050, choose which interventions to start, at what coverage, in what order, maximizing DALYs averted. That converts a static ranking table into a schedule a national programme can actually execute, and would let the model answer the funding-cut question directly: if the envelope shrinks 40%, what should be dropped first?

Added value to my researchSequential resource allocation over a network of interacting facilities and populations is the same formal object as my facility-location and service-area optimization work, with time added. It also invites the agent-based and Monte Carlo tooling I use, and produces a genuinely reusable artifact — an open, FAIR-compliant policy-scheduling layer built on top of the authors' already-open PACE-TB code, which is the kind of reproducible infrastructure contribution I want my group to be known for.
