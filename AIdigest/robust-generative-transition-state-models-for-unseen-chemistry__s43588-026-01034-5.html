Robust generative transition-state models for unseen chemistry

Samir Darouich · Jacob W. Toney · Weiliang Luo · Johannes Kästner · Mathias Niepert · Heather J. Kulik Published 2026-08-12 DOI 10.1038/s43588-026-01034-5 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Generative transition-state (TS) models that reach near-chemical accuracy on small organic reactions do not generalize beyond their training domain. Using two purpose-built benchmarks — Transition1x-2p3p4p (controlled elemental substitutions) and Transition1x-TMC (transition metal complexes) — the authors show that a vanilla React-OT-style model fails on previously unseen elements. Their fix is architecture-agnostic and cheap: self-supervised pretraining on equilibrium conformers turned into "pseudo-reactions", which exposes the generator to new chemical environments before targeted fine-tuning. This lowers the median RMSD of generated TS geometries on Transition1x-TMC and cuts the amount of fine-tuning data required, giving reliable performance even in low-data regimes, with validation carried through to the DFT level.

Why It Matters

  • It audits a field that was grading itself on a single test set. Nearly every recent generative TS model (OA-ReactDiff, React-OT, GoFlow, and relatives) reports numbers on Transition1x, whose chemistry is small organic molecules. This paper asks the question the leaderboard never asked — what happens off-distribution? — and answers it with controlled, reproducible splits rather than anecdote.
  • The failure is not a rounding error; it is structural. Elemental substitution alone (moving into heavier p-block analogues) and, more sharply, transition metal complexes break the vanilla model. That matters because TMCs are exactly where TS search is most expensive and most valuable — homogeneous catalysis, organometallic mechanism design.
  • The remedy needs no new labels. Equilibrium conformers are abundant and cheap (semi-empirical or force-field level), whereas TS structures require saddle-point searches at DFT cost. Converting free conformers into pseudo-reaction supervision is a data-economics argument, not just a modelling trick.
  • Low-data regimes become viable. Reduced fine-tuning requirements mean a group with a few hundred well-computed TMC transition states — rather than tens of thousands — can adapt a pretrained generator to their own chemistry.
  • It ships as reusable infrastructure. Benchmarks, pretrained checkpoints and databases (Zenodo) and an open codebase (RobustTSGen) let others measure their own model's novelty gap immediately.

Core Methods

  • Targeted novelty benchmarks (Fig. 1). Two data-generation workflows separate the axes of generalization: elemental novelty (2p3p4p substitutions, same reaction topology) and chemical + structural novelty (TMCs, new coordination environments). This is the paper's methodological backbone — a controlled ablation of "unseen-ness" rather than a random train/test split.
  • Failure-mode diagnosis of the baseline (Fig. 2). The vanilla React-OT generator is stress-tested on the new benchmarks and its errors are characterized qualitatively (what kind of geometry it produces when it fails), not just aggregated into a mean RMSD.
  • Conformer-based pseudo-reaction pretraining (Fig. 3). The core contribution: equilibrium conformers of molecules containing the novel elements are assembled into synthetic reactant/product/TS-like triplets, giving a self-supervised objective in the same input format the TS generator consumes. The model learns the geometry and equivariance statistics of unseen elements without a single true saddle point.
  • Targeted fine-tuning with data-efficiency scans (Fig. 4). After pretraining, the generator is fine-tuned on limited true TS data, and performance is traced as a function of fine-tuning set size to quantify the low-data advantage.
  • Equivariant generative backbone. Diffusion/optimal-transport generation over 3D molecular graphs with E(3)-equivariant message passing — reactant and product conditions in, TS geometry out — inherited from OA-ReactDiff / React-OT and LEFTNet.
  • DFT-level validation (Fig. 5). Generated TS geometries are carried into real quantum-chemistry workflows (saddle-point refinement with Sella, energy evaluation with ORCA) so that improvements are demonstrated in barrier/geometry terms a computational chemist would accept, not only in ML metrics.
  • Evaluation metric of record: median RMSD of predicted vs. reference TS geometries (reported as the headline improvement on Transition1x-TMC), complemented by barrier-related and success-rate style diagnostics.

① Generalize "pseudo-reactions" into a taxonomy of cheap-proxy pretext tasks

Extending this paperThe pretraining signal here is one specific construction: equilibrium conformers assembled into synthetic reactant–TS–product triplets. That is a single point in a much larger design space, and the paper does not ablate why it works — is the model learning element-specific bond-length priors, equivariant geometry statistics, or simply a better initialization? A direct extension is to define a family of label-free pretext tasks over the same input format (conformer interpolation, deliberately distorted geometries, masked-atom-type reconstruction, cheap GFN2-xTB relaxation trajectories treated as pseudo-paths) and measure which one buys the most transfer per unit of compute. That would turn an effective trick into a stated principle: which cheap physical states carry the information a rare-event generator is missing? Testing whether pretraining representations converge to a shared geometry prior across backbones would also connect this work to the emerging "universal representation" literature on atomistic foundation models.

Added value to my researchThis maps almost one-to-one onto my geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning work. Rare, expensive labels (verified healthcare-facility catchments, validated telehealth-utilization outcomes, ground-truthed mental-health survey points) are the geospatial equivalent of DFT transition states; abundant, cheap "equilibrium states" are the static layers I already hold — POIs, road networks, remote-sensing composites, building footprints. The lesson is that I should design label-free spatial pretext tasks (masked-POI reconstruction, network-distance regression, land-cover-to-morphology prediction) rather than jumping straight to fine-tuning on scarce health outcomes.

② Give the generator a conscience: uncertainty-aware refusal and active benchmark growth

Extending this paperFig. 2 shows the vanilla model fails silently — it emits a confident-looking geometry for chemistry it has never seen. Pretraining reduces the error but does not install a detector. The natural extension is to equip the generative TS model with a calibrated applicability domain: an ensemble or evidential head over the equivariant backbone that scores each generated TS for out-of-domain risk, so the pipeline can route uncertain reactions to a full NEB/dimer search instead of returning a plausible-looking artefact. Once that exists, it closes into an active loop: the uncertainty signal selects which TMC reactions are worth their DFT cost, and each new saddle point is added to Transition1x-TMC — the benchmark grows where the model is weakest rather than where data happened to exist.

Added value to my researchThis is the missing piece in my explainable-ML spatial prediction work. Models trained on dense, well-instrumented cities are routinely applied to rural or low-income regions with no warning flag — the geospatial equivalent of a TMC. I want a spatial applicability-domain layer that reports, per grid cell, whether the prediction is interpolation or extrapolation, and an active-sampling rule that tells a public-health agency where the next survey should go to maximally shrink model uncertainty. That converts uncertainty from a caveat into a survey-design instrument.

③ Treat "novelty axes" as the real contribution and extend them beyond elements

Extending this paperThe quiet innovation here is benchmark design: instead of a random split, novelty is decomposed into controllable axes (element identity in 2p3p4p; element + coordination geometry in TMC). But chemistry has more axes than the paper exercises — reaction mechanism class, charge and spin state, ring strain, solvent/environment, and molecule size beyond the small-organic regime of the base dataset. Extending the paper means building a novelty grid in which each axis is varied independently, then reporting a per-axis generalization profile for each published TS generator. Pairing that with a controlled study of training-set composition would answer the follow-on question the paper raises but leaves open: how much of the failure is missing elements versus missing structural diversity in the training mixture?

Added value to my researchSpatial machine learning has exactly this pathology and mostly ignores it: random k-fold CV over grid cells reports flattering scores because neighbouring cells leak. I want to port this design directly — define explicit spatial novelty axes (unseen city, unseen climate zone, unseen density regime, unseen socio-economic stratum, unseen country) and report a generalization profile per axis for every accessibility or health-outcome model I publish. That is a concrete methodological paper for the GeoAI community, using this article's benchmark logic as the template.

④ Push physics from the data into the sampler: saddle-point conditions as a hard constraint

Extending this paperPretraining here is a statistical remedy — the model sees more chemistry and therefore extrapolates better — but nothing in the generator enforces what a transition state actually is: a first-order saddle point with exactly one imaginary vibrational mode. A physics-informed extension would couple the equivariant generative sampler to a fast surrogate Hessian or a pretrained interatomic potential, and steer sampling toward geometries satisfying the saddle condition (energy-tilted or gradient-guided sampling), so that correctness is structural rather than learned. The payoff should be strongest precisely where data are thinnest — TMCs — because the physical constraint does not care whether the element was in the training set.

Added value to my researchThis is my physics-informed graph-learning agenda restated in a different domain, and it sharpens what I should be enforcing. In accessibility and mobility models the analogous hard constraints are conservation of flow through a network, monotonic distance decay, and capacity limits at facilities — properties I currently hope a GNN learns from data. Building them in as sampler-level constraints is what would let an accessibility model trained on a handful of well-mapped cities behave sensibly in an unmapped one, the same way saddle conditions rescue an unseen metal.

⑤ Turn the data-efficiency curve into a label-budget planning tool

Extending this paperFig. 4 establishes that pretraining reduces fine-tuning data requirements — a qualitative claim from a scan. The extension is to make it prescriptive: fit the accuracy-versus-labels curve per novelty axis, express it in the currency practitioners actually spend (CPU-hours of DFT saddle-point search, not "number of samples"), and publish a calculator that answers "for target median RMSD X on metal M, compute N transition states". Adding which TS to compute (from idea ②) makes it a full experiment-design tool. A parallel worth testing is whether transfer to an unseen metal behaves like transfer to an unseen biological cell type — a well-studied setting where foundation-model fine-tuning curves are already characterized.

Added value to my researchLabel budgets are the binding constraint in exactly the health-geography settings I care about most: fine-tuning a geospatial foundation model for primary-care or mental-health accessibility in a region where validated facility and utilization records number in the hundreds. A budget calculator — "to reach usable accuracy in this province, you need N ground-truthed facilities, and here is where they should be" — is a deliverable that funding agencies and health departments can act on directly, and it aligns with my open-science/FAIR commitment because the curve, not just the model, becomes the shared artefact.
