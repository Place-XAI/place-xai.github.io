Effects of Uber and Lyft on jobs, wages and GDP

Adam Koling · Jeremy Michalek · Daniel Armanios · Connor Forsythe · Akshaya Jha Published 2026-07-24 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00478-0 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Using the staggered entry of UberX and Lyft into 167 US metropolitan/micropolitan service regions (2010–2019) as a natural experiment, the authors estimate causal effects with heterogeneity-robust difference-in-differences (Callaway & Sant'Anna). TNC entry raised regional GDP per capita by ~2.9% (95% CI 0.7–5.0%) and unstable (seasonal, temporary or intermittent) employment per working-age population by ~3.7% (0.6–6.8%). Effects on total employment, total earnings and unstable earnings were statistically indistinguishable from zero. In short: ride-hailing's measurable citywide footprint shows up as growth plus job churn, not as broad-based jobs-and-wages gains — a partial, heavily qualified vindication of the platforms' own claims.

Why It Matters

  • Tests corporate claims against public data. The outcome variables were chosen from an archival inventory of Uber and Lyft executive statements and both firms' 2019 IPO filings ("flexibility is the new stability", ridesharing "has a powerful effect on cities, their economies"). The paper turns marketing rhetoric into falsifiable, pre-registered-style hypotheses.
  • Moves the ride-hailing literature from drivers to whole cities. Prior work measured taxi displacement, transit substitution, car ownership or driver income; this is a citywide, aggregate accounting of jobs, earnings and GDP at the metro-area scale.
  • Reframes the gig-work debate empirically. Higher unstable employment with no detectable change in total employment is consistent with substitution of stable jobs by intermittent ones and/or increased turnover — precisely the contested terrain of worker-classification policy.
  • Methodological hygiene with policy stakes. By abandoning two-way fixed effects for estimators robust to staggered, heterogeneous and dynamic treatment, it shows how much of the earlier "Uber effect" literature may rest on a biased workhorse regression.
  • Fully reproducible on public data. Balanced panel, R replication script and a public GitHub repository — a rarity for platform-economy causal work, where data are usually proprietary.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Treatment units
167 non-overlapping UberX/Lyft coverage areas, each matched one-to-one to a US metropolitan or micropolitan statistical area. Treatment = year of first UberX or Lyft launch (whichever came first), verified against prior literature, archived Uber/Lyft press releases and local news; corroborated with web-archive cached coverage maps and regional Google Trends. Slightly more than half of cities — and nearly three quarters of the represented population — were first treated in 2013 or 2014; 2018 contains only a single launch.
Time period
2010–2019, annual resolution (pre-pandemic by construction). Quarterly QWI values are summed across industries and averaged across quarters to match the annual ACS population denominators; no interpolation or imputation.
Labor data
Quarterly Workforce Indicators (QWI), US Census, built on Longitudinal Employer–Household Dynamics (LEHD). Multiyear panel released Q4 2020, subset to 2010–2019, covering all four-digit NAICS industry groups. Supplies beginning-of-quarter employment, stable employment, and the corresponding average monthly earnings — the stable/total distinction is what makes the paper's "unstable" measures possible.
GDP data
Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA) annual regional GDP at the metropolitan/micropolitan level; converted to real terms with the BEA GDP implicit price deflator.
Population data
US Census / American Community Survey (ACS) — annual estimates extracted from five-year windows, 2005–2019. Working-age (18–65) population normalizes the four labor variables; all-age population normalizes GDP.
Price adjustment
Earnings deflated with CPI from FRED (Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis). All dollar values reported in 2012 US dollars.
Qualitative input
Archival audio/video inventory of Uber and Lyft executive statements plus both firms' 2019 IPO securities filings (full list tabulated in the Supplementary Information); plus ~100 rides driven for a TNC in the Pittsburgh metro area, May–August 2022, under Carnegie Mellon IRB approval — used for interpretive context only, not for estimation.
Exclusions
Metro areas were dropped if they were missing any quarter 2010–2019, had substantially redrawn boundaries, or had discontinuities in ACS population estimates. Regions not served by a TNC by 2019 were excluded as likely categorically different, so the design uses not-yet-treated rather than never-treated controls.
Balance
Headline ATTs use a truncated [−3, +2] event-time window where ≥96% of the 167 metros have data; event studies plot [−4, +6] but gray out estimates outside the balanced window. The paper is explicit that later post-treatment years rest on a non-random subset.
Availability
Fully open. Balanced panel CSV (2025-09-14 TNC Dataset Inflation Adjusted.csv) plus two auxiliary CSVs and a commented R script on GitHub. Raw-file generation script available on request.
Not reported
The article does not state a total number of city-year observations, a driver count per city, or any trip-level ride-hailing volume — no such number is fabricated here. Treatment is a binary launch indicator, not an intensity measure.

Core Methods

  • Staggered natural experiment: TNC entry year varies across the 167 regions, giving cohort structure g (first-treatment year) exploited by modern DiD rather than a single pre/post break.
  • Callaway & Sant'Anna (2021) main estimator: group–time average treatment effects ATT(g,t) computed against base period t = −1 using not-yet-treated metros as controls, then aggregated across cohorts weighted by the share of cities treated in each year. This avoids the "already-treated as illegitimate control" contamination that biases TWFE under heterogeneous, dynamic effects.
  • Outcome construction: five dependent variables — employment, unstable employment, earnings, unstable earnings, GDP. Unstable series are defined as total minus stable (jobs held in the quarter but not in the adjacent quarters), capturing both genuinely intermittent work and turnover between otherwise stable jobs. All are normalized per population and log-transformed (justified by Shapiro–Wilk tests), so ATTs read as approximate percentage changes.
  • Simultaneous rather than pointwise confidence bands: 95% simultaneous bands control the joint distribution of ATT estimates over event time, correcting for multiple hypothesis testing; P values are asymptotic normal (z) approximations from those bands.
  • Superset/subset comparison: a simultaneous bootstrap with 5,000 replications plus a paired t-test tests whether effects on unstable employment/earnings differ significantly from effects on the corresponding totals.
  • Parallel-trends diagnostics: event studies show no visually significant pre-trends, confirmed by a joint Wald test; a Goodman-Bacon-style rationale motivates abandoning TWFE in the first place.
  • Robustness battery: Sun & Abraham (2021), stacked regression (Cengiz et al. 2019) and classic TWFE are all reported in the Supplementary Information, alongside Callaway–Sant'Anna results for the full [−4, +6] window and a varying-base-period variant.
  • Identification threats named explicitly: reverse causality (platforms entering cities already at the start of an expansion), contemporaneous entry of other gig platforms that cannot be dated as precisely, and the inability to attribute the net effect to any single mechanism (driver supply vs. passenger mobility vs. agglomeration).

① Break the metro-area container: sub-metropolitan, spatially explicit treatment effects

Extending this paperThe paper's finest spatial grain is the metropolitan/micropolitan statistical area, and treatment is a binary launch dummy. But Uber/Lyft coverage grew as an expanding polygon inside each region, and the paper itself used web-archive coverage maps to date launches — those same maps encode where service existed, not just when. A direct extension: rebuild the panel at census-tract or ZIP-level using LEHD LODES workplace/residence blocks and QCEW, and replace the binary treatment with a continuous exposure surface (share of tract area or population inside the coverage polygon, distance to the coverage frontier). This converts one ATT per city into a spatially varying dose–response, and lets the authors test whether the +2.9% GDP effect is concentrated in dense cores (agglomeration channel) or in auto-reliant suburbs where drivers actually live (driver-supply channel) — the very mechanism ambiguity the Discussion flags as unresolved.

Added value to my researchThis is the exact bridge between causal inference and my spatial-heterogeneity toolkit: run MGWR on the tract-level ATT surface so that the treatment coefficient itself becomes a mapped, locally varying field, with bandwidth diagnosing the scale at which ride-hailing's economic effect operates. It also gives me a clean template for GWR/MGWR-augmented DiD that I can reuse for telehealth rollout, EV-charging deployment and clinic openings — all staggered, spatially expanding interventions where I currently have no principled way to report where the effect landed.

② Close the mechanism gap with mobility data: decompose the net effect into driver-side and passenger-side channels

Extending this paperThe authors are candid that their design "cannot attribute the estimated net effects to any particular one, or combination, of the mechanisms" — driver income, passenger access to work and consumption, agglomeration, reduced drunk driving, corporate headcount. That is the single largest hole in an otherwise tight paper. Extension: layer mobility-derived intermediates onto the same 167-region panel — POI visitation and trip counts from mobile-phone/GPS panels, transit ridership from NTD, VMT from HPMS — and run a mediation-style causal decomposition (or a sequential-DiD on each intermediate) to ask how much of the GDP effect flows through additional consumption trips versus additional labor supply. Because the paper's GDP effect is delayed in event time (Fig. 3), an intermediate that also builds slowly is the natural suspect.

Added value to my researchThis maps directly onto my multi-source data-fusion line: mobile-phone records, social media and POI check-ins as mediators rather than as outcomes. Operationally it yields a reusable pipeline — spatiotemporal KDE on trip origins/destinations to build the intermediate exposure field, then feed that field into the causal decomposition — which I can transplant wholesale to "does a new transit line improve healthcare utilization, and through which travel channel?"

③ Test the "gateway job" hypothesis on an occupation network instead of an aggregate counter

Extending this paperThe Main section floats a striking mechanism — TNC driving as a gateway experience into wider app-based gig work, language acquisition and local knowledge — but the empirical apparatus can only report that unstable employment rose 3.7% while stable employment did not move. A richer test: build an occupation-transition network from LEHD job-to-job flows or CPS/ACS microdata, and ask whether TNC entry changed the topology of transitions — did it add new edges out of unemployment/inactivity into intermittent work, and are those edges absorbing states or genuine stepping stones toward stable employment 2–4 years later? This turns the paper's ambiguous "substitution vs. turnover" reading (it explicitly cannot distinguish them) into a testable structural question, and it exploits the fact that QWI/LEHD already track individuals across employers.

Added value to my researchDirectly feeds my job–housing network and network-community-detection work: an occupation-transition graph is a bipartite companion to the job–housing graph, and community detection on it identifies labor-market submarkets whose boundaries need not match commuting sheds. Pairing the two lets me ask whether spatial job–housing imbalance and occupational entrapment reinforce each other — a genuinely new axis for my urban-analytics agenda, with a well-defined treatment (TNC entry) to give it causal traction.

④ Predict which cities gain: explainable causal machine learning on the 167-region heterogeneity

Extending this paperThe paper's whole methodological premise is that treatment effects are heterogeneous across cities — yet it reports a single aggregated ATT per outcome and relegates city characteristics (Fig. 4 population, plus SI median household income and 2010 population density) to descriptive plots. Extension: estimate conditional average treatment effects with a causal forest / double machine learning layer over the same balanced panel, using pre-treatment covariates the paper already assembled — density, income, transit supply, industry mix, car ownership, urban form — then apply SHAP-style attribution to the CATE function to say which city attributes buy the GDP gain and which merely buy job churn. With only 167 units the honest framing is exploratory rather than confirmatory, and that limit must be stated up front — but it converts a single number into a transferable prediction rule for cities that had not yet been treated by 2019 and for non-US contexts.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction line pointed at a causal target instead of an outcome level: the object being explained is a treatment effect surface, not a prediction. Methodologically it gives me a defensible answer to the perennial reviewer question about ML in policy work — "your model predicts, but does it tell me what to do?" — and it generalizes immediately to my EV-charging efficiency-vs-equity siting and telehealth-expansion work, where the real question is always which places benefit most per dollar.

⑤ Re-run the design with health as the outcome: ride-hailing entry as a natural experiment on care access

Extending this paperThe paper's identification strategy — 167 staggered launches, not-yet-treated controls, heterogeneity-robust DiD on public panel data — is outcome-agnostic. Its own literature review already touches health-adjacent effects (traffic fatalities, drunk driving, discriminatory refusal of taxi service to low-income and minority riders). The natural sequel swaps QWI/BEA outcomes for health-system outcomes: missed-appointment and no-show rates, emergency-department utilization for ambulatory-care-sensitive conditions, prenatal-care initiation, dialysis adherence — all available at metro or county level. Because non-emergency medical transport is a documented access barrier, TNC entry is a plausible shock to realized accessibility with none of the endogeneity of a clinic opening. The same caveats apply and should be carried over: reverse causality, concurrent platform entry, and the fact that a launch dummy is not an intensity measure.

Added value to my researchThis is the highest-value transplant for my health-geography line. It supplies the missing causal counterpart to my 2SFCA/R2SFCA work: 2SFCA quantifies potential access from supply, demand and travel impedance, but a TNC launch is an exogenous reduction in impedance for a subset of the population — so I can finally validate whether modeled accessibility gains translate into measured utilization gains, and by how much. It also sharpens the digital-divide argument: ride-hailing access is smartphone-, banking- and credit-gated, so the same shock that improves access for connected populations may widen the gap for those excluded — a testable equity prediction rather than an assertion.
