Principled approaches for extending neural architectures to function spaces for operator learning

Julius Berner · Miguel Liu-Schiaffini · Jean Kossaifi · Valentin Duruisseaux · Boris Bonev · Kamyar Azizzadenesheli · Anima Anandkumar Published 2026-07-03 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01267-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Standard neural networks learn maps between finite-dimensional arrays, but most scientific data are discretized samples of continuous fields. The paper distils three defining principles for learning in function space — discretization agnosticism, a fixed parameter count and universal approximation — and gives a concrete recipe for converting popular architectures (MLPs, CNNs, GNNs, transformers, encoder–decoders) into neural operators: replace index-based parameters with coordinate-dependent kernels, replace sums over neighbour indices with quadrature-weighted integrals over coordinate-defined neighbourhoods, and make latent interfaces independent of the observation grid. Empirically on Navier–Stokes, FNO and OFORMER trained only at resolution 128 generalize to unseen coarser and finer resolutions, whereas U-Net and ViT degrade away from the training resolution — and mixed-resolution training helps only at the resolutions actually seen.

Why It Matters

  • Turns "neural operator" from a family of models into a design rule. Instead of proposing yet another architecture, the paper states what any layer must satisfy to be a legitimate function-space map, then shows how to retrofit that property onto architectures people already trust.
  • Explains a failure mode that is easy to mistake for a data problem. A U-Net accepts arrays of any size, so it looks resolution-flexible; in fact its receptive field is tied to grid spacing, so at a new resolution it silently implements a different operation. The paper isolates this as an architectural defect, not a generalization gap to be patched with augmentation.
  • Rehabilitates quadrature weights as a first-class modelling object. Aggregating irregularly sampled values with uniform weights over-counts densely sampled regions and the output has no unique limit under refinement (Fig. 5). Weighting by Voronoi/Delaunay segment measure, grid spacing or Monte Carlo density restores a consistent limit — a point with direct consequences far beyond PDEs.
  • Keeps operator learning coupled to mainstream deep learning progress. Because the conversion is layer-local and "minimal", advances in attention, normalization and multiscale design can be inherited rather than reinvented, which is the paper's explicit strategic argument.
  • Practically actionable. The recipe ships with the open neuraloperator library, an archived dataset and archived code, so the claims are reproducible rather than programmatic.

Core Methods

  • Three defining principles. A function-space model must be (i) discretization agnostic — the same parameters apply at any discretization with outputs consistent up to a vanishing discretization error; (ii) fixed in parameter count, independent of the number of sample points; and (iii) a universal approximator of sufficiently regular operators.
  • The conversion recipe. Identify which continuous operator a finite-dimensional layer discretizes, then re-discretize it respecting coordinates, quadrature weights and sampling structure. Concretely: index-dependent parameters → coordinate-dependent functions; sums over indices → integrals over coordinate-defined neighbourhoods; integrals → quadrature on the available points. Most layers fall under the template g(y) = Ψ(y, f(y), ∫ K(x, y, f(x), f(y)) dx).
  • Layer-by-layer correspondences. Fully connected layer → integral transform with learnable kernel and bias functions; activations, 1×1 convolutions, liftings and projections → pointwise operators (transfer almost unchanged); discrete convolution → coordinate-defined convolution with fixed support radius, or, via the convolution theorem, spectral convolution (the FNO); GNN message passing → graph neural operator with quadrature-weighted neighbourhood integrals; attention → global input-dependent integral operator requiring quadrature weights in both aggregation and normalization (reducing to standard attention on uniform grids); encoder–decoder → inner products with learned basis functions and a latent dimension fixed independently of the sample count.
  • Quadrature as the load-bearing detail. Weights Δi are grid-spacing products on regular grids, Delaunay-triangulation partitions on point clouds, or Monte Carlo weights when samples follow a known density. Without them, refinement changes the answer and no unique limit exists (Fig. 5).
  • Receptive-field diagnosis. Nearest-neighbour neighbourhoods collapse in physical extent as the mesh is refined (Fig. 3); fixing the support radius in domain coordinates makes the receptive field a property of the domain rather than of the grid.
  • Function-space training objectives. Losses are defined through discretization-consistent (quadrature-aware) norms, so that the discrete loss approximates ∫|g − g*|2 dy; Sobolev and physics-informed variants, multiresolution training and autoregressive rollouts are discussed as operator-preserving strategies.
  • Controlled cross-resolution protocol. Train once, evaluate the identical parameters across resolutions, and contrast principled operators against (a) fixed-grid networks, (b) multiresolution-augmented networks and (c) interpolation-wrapped networks — isolating which design choice actually buys resolution generalization.

① Push the quadrature argument out of the toy setting and into biased, non-uniform sampling

Extending this paperFig. 5 makes the paper's sharpest point — uniform aggregation over irregular points has no unique limit under refinement — but demonstrates it on a one-dimensional illustration, while every quantitative experiment (Navier–Stokes, Darcy) runs on regular grids where the weights cancel. The obvious extension is to stress-test the recipe where quadrature actually bites: sample the same field on point clouds whose density is correlated with the signal (dense where gradients are steep, sparse elsewhere), and compare Delaunay/Voronoi weights, Monte Carlo weights under a known density, and naive uniform weights. That would convert the paper's qualitative "use quadrature weights" instruction into an empirical error budget, directly addressing its own stated open problem of quantifying and controlling discretization error in practice.

Added value to my researchThis is the modifiable areal unit problem stated as numerical analysis. Census tracts, grid cells, sensor networks and mobile-phone towers are all non-uniform quadrature schemes, and my accessibility and exposure surfaces are integrals over them. Adopting explicit population- or area-weighted Δi inside a spatial ML model gives me a defensible answer to the perennial reviewer question of why results shift between tract, block-group and hexagon aggregations — and a diagnostic for when they should not shift. It also suggests a scale-consistency check for spatiotemporal kernel density estimation: refine the bandwidth grid and verify convergence rather than tuning it by eye.

② Read 2SFCA as an instance of the paper's Eq. (4) — and learn its kernel

Extending this paperThe paper's central template, g(y) = ∫D(y) K(x, y, f(x), f(y)) dx, is presented as the continuum limit of message passing on PDE meshes. But structurally it is a spatial accessibility operator: f is a supply or demand density, D(y) is a catchment, K is a distance-decay kernel. Extending the paper means demonstrating the recipe on a non-PDE field problem where the ground-truth operator is known analytically but the domain is an irregular network rather than a square — showing that "operator learning" is a statement about data being fields, not about physics. This also tests the paper's own claim that graph neural operators subsume integral-transform and convolutional cases through the choice of neighbourhood D(y).

Added value to my researchDirect and concrete: in 2SFCA/R2SFCA the decay function is assumed (Gaussian, power, piecewise), and the catchment radius is a hand-set constant. Rewriting the estimator as a graph neural operator layer makes K a learnable coordinate-dependent kernel fitted to observed utilization or mobility flows, while the fixed-radius-versus-kNN distinction of Fig. 3 explains precisely why kNN-based catchments behave inconsistently as one moves from rural to dense urban sampling. The prize is an accessibility model whose parameters transfer between study areas with different spatial resolutions instead of being re-tuned per city.

③ Treat cross-city transfer as the geographic analogue of cross-resolution generalization

Extending this paperThe paper's experimental axis is resolution: train at 128, test at 64 and 256, same parameters throughout. It never varies the domain — the underlying operator is fixed and only its sampling changes. A natural and more demanding extension is a two-axis protocol that varies discretization and the distribution of input functions (different forcing statistics, different boundary geometry), separating discretization error from conditional shift. The paper implicitly needs this: it warns that discretization-agnostic designs can underperform specialized networks at a fixed resolution, and only a two-axis study can show whether that cost buys robustness to real distribution change.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly my spatial transferability problem stated cleanly. A model of primary-care access or telehealth uptake trained in one metro is asked to run in another, where both the sampling (tract sizes, survey density) and the process (demographics, provider supply) differ. Borrowing the paper's protocol gives me a principled ablation for GeoAI papers: hold parameters fixed, vary resolution first, then vary region — and report the two error contributions separately instead of a single out-of-sample number. It also gives spatial non-stationarity (my MGWR territory) a crisp companion diagnostic: non-stationarity is conditional shift, not discretization error, and the two demand different fixes.

④ Take the recipe to domains with holes, coastlines and moving boundaries

Extending this paperThe paper names "operator architectures for complex geometries and adaptive discretizations" as an open direction, and its own evidence is confined to a periodic square (with padding as the workaround for Darcy's non-periodic boundaries). Spectral convolution — the workhorse behind its strongest result — assumes a structure that real domains violate. Extending the paper means specifying, layer by layer, what the conversion recipe becomes when the domain has irregular boundaries, interior holes and a boundary that moves: which layers survive unchanged (pointwise ones), which need coordinate-defined local kernels instead of global spectral ones, and how quadrature weights must be redefined near the boundary so the integral remains consistent.

Added value to my researchUrban domains are exactly this: street networks with holes, administrative boundaries that cut catchments, rivers and coastlines that make Euclidean kernels wrong, and — in flood or epidemic settings — a boundary that moves in time. A boundary-aware operator layer would let me model an exposure or accessibility field on a domain that respects barriers rather than smoothing across them, which is the single most common criticism of kernel-based urban surfaces. It also connects naturally to my agent-based and SIR work, where the "free boundary" is the moving edge of an outbreak or an evacuation zone.

⑤ Apply the conversion recipe to pretrained geospatial foundation models — and benchmark it honestly

Extending this paperThe paper shows that a ViT is not a neural operator because its tokenization is tied to the grid, and that interpolation-wrapping any pretrained network yields only a naive operator that discards high-resolution detail. Yet nearly every geospatial and Earth-system foundation model today is a patch-tokenized ViT. The high-value extension is to apply the recipe to a pretrained model rather than a from-scratch one: replace patch tokenization with coordinate-based positional information, insert quadrature weights into attention aggregation and normalization, and fine-tune — then measure how much of the pretrained representation survives, and whether the resulting model achieves genuine zero-shot resolution transfer instead of interpolation-quality transfer. Paired with this, the field needs a benchmark whose evaluation grid is deliberately different from any training grid, so that resolution-overfitting cannot be mistaken for skill.

Added value to my researchFine-tuning geospatial foundation models is an active line for me, and this reframes the central obstacle. Satellite and reanalysis products arrive at heterogeneous resolutions (10 m, 30 m, 250 m, 0.25°), and a patch-tokenized backbone quietly encodes the resolution it was pretrained on — so fine-tuning on one sensor and deploying on another changes the operation, not just the input. An operator-ified backbone would let one model ingest Sentinel, Landsat and reanalysis fields without resampling everything to a lowest common denominator, which is where most multi-source fusion loses information. The benchmark discipline is equally useful to me: for FAIR/open-science reporting, "tested at a resolution never seen in training" is a far more informative claim than a held-out split at the same grid.
