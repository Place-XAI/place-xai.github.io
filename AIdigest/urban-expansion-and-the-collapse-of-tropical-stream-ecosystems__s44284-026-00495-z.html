Urban expansion and the collapse of tropical stream ecosystems

Victor S. Saito · Rodolfo Mei Pelinson · Thiago Leão-Pires · Gabriel Brejão · Lilian Casatti · Erika Shimabukuro · Tadeu Siqueira · Ricardo H. Taniwaki · Luis Schiesari Published 2026-08-14 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00495-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Across an urban-cover gradient in the Metropolitan Region of São Paulo (a megacity of >22 million people), urban expansion does not merely thin out stream biodiversity — it removes species non-randomly with respect to body mass. Very small, moderately large and large taxa are selectively lost, truncating the size spectrum and leaving urban streams dominated by small-bodied organisms, with size ranges compressed by four orders of magnitude relative to rainforest streams. Food-web complexity collapses from networks of >150 trophic links to as few as 3, producing a 1,446-fold reduction in energy flow. Because energy flux underpins nutrient cycling and self-purification, this is a loss of urban ecosystem function, not just of species lists.

Why It Matters

  • Moves urban ecology from "how many species" to "how much function." Species counts fall gradually; energy flux falls by three orders of magnitude. The paper shows that biodiversity indicators badly understate the functional damage urbanization does to streams.
  • Identifies a mechanism, not just a correlation. Loss is size-selective. Body mass is the trait that couples urban infrastructure to metabolism, trophic linkage and flux — giving a mechanistic chain from land cover to ecosystem service.
  • Sets a functional baseline for urban river restoration. Rainforest headwaters in the same basin act as the reference state, so "restored" can be defined by size-spectrum slope and food-web link density rather than by aesthetics or a single water-quality index.
  • Speaks to the Global South megacity, where most future urban growth will occur. Tropical headwater streams in fast-urbanizing basins are the least monitored and most exposed; this quantifies what is being lost while it is still being lost.
  • Direct implication for urban public health. Nutrient cycling, organic-matter processing and self-purification in streams are the free services that failing sanitation infrastructure leans on; their collapse shifts the burden back onto engineered treatment and onto exposed residents.

Core Methods

  • Space-for-time comparative gradient design: headwater streams are sampled across catchments ordered by urban cover, using rainforest catchments as the reference endpoint — urbanization intensity substitutes for a longitudinal series.
  • Individual-level body-mass reconstruction: every sampled organism is converted from body length to dry mass via published length–mass regressions, producing an individual-resolved mass distribution rather than taxon-level averages.
  • Size-spectrum fitting: community size distributions are fitted following the maximum-likelihood recommendations of Edwards et al. (2017), with sensitivity analyses reported in Supplementary Sections — this is what makes the "truncation" claim statistically defensible rather than visual.
  • Allometric food-web construction: trophic links are inferred from predator–prey body-mass ratios plus functional feeding groups from Neotropical literature, then summarized as link counts and network complexity indices.
  • Energy-flux estimation with fluxweb: metabolic rates scaled from body mass (metabolic theory of ecology; Brown et al., 2004) are propagated through the inferred network to estimate energy and carbon flow per stream, yielding the 1,446-fold contrast.
  • Uncertainty handling: Monte Carlo evaluation of network-index robustness (Kones et al., 2009), likelihood-based model fitting in R (Bolker, 2008), and residual diagnostics for hierarchical/mixed models via DHARMa — plus sampling-effort analyses to show diversity loss is not an artifact of detection.
  • Non-randomness test: the central inferential move is showing that species loss is size-biased — a departure from random extinction — which is what converts a biodiversity decline into a food-web structural collapse.

① From a sampled gradient to a wall-to-wall, explainable stream-condition surface

Extending this paperThe paper's inference rests on a handful of intensively sampled headwater catchments — powerful ecologically, but it leaves the rest of the Upper Tietê Basin unmapped. A direct extension: treat the measured size-spectrum slope, link density and energy flux as response variables and train an explainable ML ensemble on MapBiomas 30 m land cover, impervious fraction, riparian-buffer width, slope, sanitation-network coverage and CETESB water-quality stations, then predict every ungauged headwater catchment in the MRSP. SHAP/accumulated-local-effect curves would expose whether the collapse is a threshold (a critical urban-cover fraction) or a smooth decay — a question the paper's gradient design raises but cannot resolve at basin scale. The upshot is a functional stream-condition map that planners can actually act on, with per-catchment attribution of which urban attribute drove the prediction.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction pipeline transplanted into an ecological response, and it gives me something my urban-health work has lacked: a validated environmental-quality covariate surface for the Global South, built from open remote sensing, that can later be fused with health outcomes. It also stress-tests whether MGWR-style spatial non-stationarity or tree-ensemble interactions better captures land-cover-to-function relationships in a tropical megacity.

② A physics-informed graph operator for food-web collapse

Extending this paperThe paper builds food webs from allometric predator–prey rules and pushes metabolic rates through them with fluxweb — a fixed, hand-specified generative model applied stream by stream. Extending it: learn a physics-informed graph neural operator that maps (catchment covariates, species pool, body-mass distribution) → (adjacency of trophic links, per-link energy flux), with metabolic scaling (M3/4) and mass-balance conservation imposed as soft constraints in the loss rather than as rigid assumptions. Trained across the sampled streams and validated against the paper's own fluxweb outputs, such an operator would let the 1,446-fold flux result be extrapolated to catchments where only community composition is known, and would reveal which links are structurally fragile — the ones whose removal cascades the 150→3 collapse.

Added value to my researchEcological food webs are an ideal, well-instrumented testbed for the physics-informed graph learning I apply to urban flow networks: both have conservation laws, allometric scaling and sparse observed edges. Lessons about encoding conservation priors here transfer directly to job–housing networks and service-supply graphs, where I want GNNs that respect flow balance instead of merely fitting adjacency.

③ Close the loop from stream collapse to human exposure and health geography

Extending this paperThe paper's closing claim — that degraded food webs will have "extensive consequences for urban societies" — is asserted rather than measured. The extension is to make it an empirical link: overlay the predicted per-catchment flux loss with sanitation-network coverage, informal-settlement footprints and residential proximity to open channels, then test associations with waterborne-disease notifications, vector-borne incidence and the pharmaceutical contamination the team's own MRSP work has documented. A defensible mediation framing is available: urban cover → loss of stream self-purification capacity → contaminant residence time → human exposure — with the paper's flux estimate as the mediating variable rather than a proxy such as impervious fraction.

④ Replace space-for-time with an actual time axis: legacy, lag and hysteresis

Extending this paperThe design substitutes an urban-cover gradient for a chronology, so it cannot say when each catchment collapsed or whether recovery is possible. MapBiomas provides annual land cover back to 1985, which makes a genuine extension feasible: reconstruct each catchment's urbanization trajectory, date its crossing of the candidate threshold from idea ①, and test whether current size-spectrum truncation depends on time-since-urbanization and rate-of-change rather than on present-day cover alone. Coupled with spatiotemporal kernel density estimation of urban-expansion fronts, this would separate catchments still relaxing toward collapse from those already at their degraded attractor — and detect hysteresis, where reducing urban cover does not restore flux.

Added value to my researchExtraction and dating of spatiotemporal thresholds from long land-cover series is directly reusable in my urban-agglomeration and healthy-cities work, where I repeatedly need to know whether an outcome tracks current form or accumulated history. The lag/hysteresis machinery also sharpens my telehealth and facility-access studies, in which infrastructure legacies persist long after the land use that produced them.

⑤ Turn the collapse curve into a restoration-siting optimizer with an equity constraint

Extending this paperThe paper diagnoses; it does not prescribe. Its dose–response between catchment urban cover and energy flux is, however, exactly the objective function a planner needs. The extension is an agent-based / spatial-optimization layer: candidate interventions (riparian-buffer restoration, sewer interception, stream daylighting, permeable-surface retrofits) are placed to maximize predicted recovery of food-web flux per unit cost, under a distributional constraint so that restoration does not concentrate in high-income sub-basins. Monte Carlo simulation over the flux model's uncertainty would yield robust rather than knife-edge portfolios, and the resulting Pareto front (ecological recovery × cost × equity) is a deliverable the paper's own numbers can support.

Added value to my researchThis is the same efficiency-versus-equity optimization structure I use for EV-charging siting and public-facility allocation, retargeted at green-blue infrastructure — and it lets me test whether the equity penalty behaves similarly when the "service" being allocated is ecological function rather than a facility. It also gives my agent-based modelling line a rare case with a physically grounded objective instead of a purely behavioural one.
