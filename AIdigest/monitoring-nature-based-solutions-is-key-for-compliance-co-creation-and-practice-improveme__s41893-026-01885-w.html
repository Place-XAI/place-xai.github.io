Monitoring nature-based solutions is key for compliance, co-creation and practice improvement

Erich Wolff · Perrine Hamel · Alex M. Lechner · Kim Irvine · Niki Frantzeskaki · Loan Diep · Luisa Tavenisa · Ashley Welch · Pamela Gloria Cajilig · Monalisa Sen · Chloe Pottinger-Glass · Lelani Mannetti · Claudia Tan · Alina Bill-Weilandt · Shaikh Fairul Edros Ahmad Shaikh · Kashif Shaad · Cathy Oke · Dixi Mengote-Quah · Yuliya Dzyuban Published 2026-07-14 Type Perspective DOI 10.1038/s41893-026-01885-w Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) of urban nature-based solutions should not be treated as a downstream reporting chore but as the connective tissue between international commitments and local action. The authors argue that M&E serves three inseparable purposes — compliance with climate and biodiversity commitments, co-creation with the communities who live with the intervention, and practice improvement through reflexive learning — and that today's indicator frameworks are systematically decoupled from what practitioners can actually implement (Fig. 1). Their remedy is to reframe M&E as a collective learning process that credits people's everyday knowledge and leverages community involvement, run as an iterative, reflexive loop rather than a one-off compliance audit (Fig. 2).

Why It Matters

  • Names the failure mode that quietly voids global commitments. Pledges under the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework, NDCs and the UNEA resolution on NbS are only as credible as the evidence that they were delivered. The paper argues the binding constraint is not ambition but the framework-to-practice gap in M&E — indicators that are unmeasurable, unaffordable or meaningless at project scale.
  • Reframes monitoring from audit to learning. Treating M&E as compliance-only produces indicators nobody uses twice. Framing it as a collective learning process makes monitoring generative: it feeds back into design, maintenance and stewardship of the intervention itself.
  • Puts everyday knowledge on the evidence ledger. Residents observe flooding, heat, mosquito nuisance, maintenance failure and use patterns continuously and for free — the paper argues this observational capacity is currently discarded rather than instrumented, and that community involvement is an epistemic asset, not a participation formality.
  • Speaks from where the implementation gap is largest. The 19 authors sit across academia, IUCN, ICLEI, Conservation International, UN-adjacent and World Bank programmes, and consultancy, with a strong Southeast Asian and Pacific centre of gravity — so the argument is grounded in low- and middle-income and informal-settlement contexts where standardized Global-North indicator sets travel badly.
  • Directly actionable for cities. The prescription is procedural — iterate, reflect, involve, revise — which municipalities can adopt without waiting for new satellites, new standards or new budgets.

Core Methods

  • Argumentative synthesis, not statistical analysis. The contribution is conceptual: a structured reading of NbS standards, M&E handbooks and governance scholarship, filtered through the authors' collective implementation experience across Southeast Asia, the Pacific, Europe and the Americas.
  • Three-purpose decomposition of M&E. The organizing device is separating what monitoring is for — compliance (upward accountability to global frameworks and funders), co-creation (downward legitimacy and shared ownership with residents), and practice improvement (internal learning) — and showing that indicator systems built for one purpose fail the other two.
  • Gap diagnosis between frameworks and practice (Fig. 1). Mapping where standardized indicator frameworks lose traction at implementation: capacity, cost, data availability, mismatched temporal horizons, and indicators that are legible to funders but meaningless to the people living beside the intervention.
  • Reflexive-monitoring loop (Fig. 2). Adapting reflexive monitoring from transitions research: monitor → interpret collectively → revise both the intervention and the indicator set → re-monitor, so that indicators are treated as revisable hypotheses rather than fixed contractual metrics.
  • Knowledge-pluralism stance. Elevating everyday, local and Indigenous observation to admissible evidence alongside instrumented measurement — with citizen-science flood monitoring in informal settlements (cited from the lead author's prior work) as the demonstrator that community-generated data can be both cheap and decision-grade.
  • Explicit positionality across science–policy–practice. The method is partly institutional: convening academic, NGO, multilateral and consultancy authors so the prescription is testable against actual programme constraints.

① Turn Fig. 2's reflexive loop into a running GeoAI monitoring observatory

Extending this paperFigure 2 is a schematic loop with no clock and no data layer: it says monitoring should iterate but never specifies what is sensed, at what cadence, or how community observation and instrumented measurement are reconciled. The concrete extension is to instantiate the loop as a spatiotemporal assimilation pipeline for a single city's NbS portfolio — resident reports (photo, geotag, short structured form), low-cost sensors, and satellite/UAV indices flowing into one georeferenced state estimate per site, with disagreement between community signal and sensor signal treated as information (a maintenance flag, an equity flag, or a mis-specified indicator) rather than noise to be averaged away. Citizen-science light mapping in our corpus shows the volunteer-observation half of this is already operationally viable at city scale; the paper's contribution would become testable the moment it is wired to such a stream.

It also gives me a real reflexive-loop testbed for agent-based modelling of who reports, when, and after which event.

② Build the compliance crosswalk the paper argues is missing

Extending this paperThe paper's "compliance" pillar assumes a translation exists between locally meaningful indicators and the international frameworks that actually gate finance (IUCN Global Standard, KMGBF, adaptation reporting). That translation is precisely what does not exist — which is the gap Fig. 1 draws. The extension is to construct an explicit indicator crosswalk matrix: rows = community-defined indicators elicited from real projects, columns = framework indicators, cells = an evidenced mapping strength, plus a measured cost-to-collect per indicator. Then quantify the orphan set — indicators communities care about that no framework counts, and framework indicators no project can afford — turning a rhetorical gap into an auditable ledger. The 3-30-300 benchmarking study in our corpus is the template for how a simple, legible rule can be evaluated city-wide and where such benchmarks distort what gets measured.

Added value to my researchSquarely my open-science/FAIR line: a versioned, machine-readable crosswalk is a reusable research artefact, not a paper appendix. It also generalizes a problem I keep meeting in health geography — reconciling administratively convenient service metrics with what residents experience as access — and gives me a concrete schema-mapping task on which to fine-tune a language model for indicator harmonization.

Cross-ref (corpus): Assessing European cities with the 3-30-300 rule underscores the need for enhanced urban greening efforts — 10.1038/s41467-026-71523-8

③ Add causal attribution so "practice improvement" has something to learn from

Extending this paperThe paper's third pillar — practice improvement — quietly presupposes that monitoring can tell you what worked. But most NbS M&E measures outputs (trees planted, basin volume built), not attributable outcomes, so the reflexive loop has no gradient to descend. The extension is to bolt a counterfactual layer onto the loop: matched control sites, staggered-rollout designs, or synthetic-control construction from unaffected neighbourhoods, so each monitoring cycle returns an effect estimate with uncertainty rather than a status report. Corpus work quantifying cooling and energy savings across NbS types and scales supplies exactly the effect-size priors needed to power such designs — and shows the estimates are strongly type- and scale-dependent, which is itself an argument against one-size indicator sets.

Added value to my researchThis is where my explainable ML for spatial prediction earns its keep: SHAP-style attribution over a site-level outcome model tells a city not just whether an intervention worked but which contextual features (canopy density, upstream imperviousness, baseline deprivation) carried the effect — an interpretable, transferable design rule. Pairing it with MGWR turns spatially varying effectiveness into an explicit map of where the same NbS type will and will not pay off.

④ Make co-creation measurable: monitor the actor network, not just the vegetation

Extending this paper"Co-creation" is the paper's most normatively loaded pillar and its least operational one — there is no proposed way to tell a genuinely co-created project from a consultation performed once at inception. The extension is to monitor the governance network itself as a first-class object: build a temporal bipartite graph of actors (residents' associations, NGOs, city departments, funders) and NbS sites, then apply community detection and centrality over time to detect brokered versus captured configurations, and test whether network structure predicts maintenance survival five years out. Corpus work on how NGOs reshape green-space justice in Beijing demonstrates that intermediary actors materially redistribute who benefits — so their position is a legitimate monitored indicator, and one currently absent from every standard in the paper's reference list.

Added value to my researchDirectly reuses my network community-detection toolkit from job–housing and regional-collaboration studies, transplanted onto environmental governance. It also lets me couple governance structure with a 2SFCA/R2SFCA accessibility layer over NbS assets — treating green sites as supply and population as demand — so "who co-created it" and "who can actually reach it" become two measurable faces of the same equity question.

⑤ Reconcile the paper's local reflexive M&E with a global remote-sensing backbone

Extending this paperThe paper argues from the local outward, but international compliance is increasingly assessed from the satellite downward — creating a scale mismatch it flags but does not resolve. The extension is a two-way calibration study: take cities already covered by global NbS mapping, run the paper's reflexive local M&E in a subset of sites, and quantify where top-down classification and bottom-up community assessment disagree — small, fragmented, informal or maintenance-degraded interventions being the predicted blind spots. Green-roof vegetation-health remote sensing in our corpus shows the resolution frontier where satellite monitoring of small NbS assets starts to break, giving a principled boundary between what should be sensed globally and what must be observed locally. The output is a fitness-for-purpose map of monitoring modalities — the missing operational layer under the paper's argument.

Added value to my researchThis is a well-shaped task for fine-tuning a geospatial foundation model: small, heterogeneous, partially labelled urban-nature assets with sparse community-verified ground truth is exactly the low-label regime where foundation-model transfer beats bespoke classifiers, and where community reports become a validation set rather than an afterthought. It gives my GeoAI work a reproducible benchmark that is simultaneously a policy instrument.
