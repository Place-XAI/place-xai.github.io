A unifying framework from neural superposition to sparse interpretable codes

David Klindt · Charles O'Neill · Patrik Reizinger · Harald Maurer · Nina Miolane Published 2026-07-14 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01259-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Neural networks — biological and artificial — encode more concepts than they have neurons by placing features in superposition, and this is not a bug but a recoverable code. The paper unifies three previously separate literatures into a three-step framework: (1) identifiability theory shows that networks trained for classification recover the true latent features up to linear mixing; (2) compressed sensing supplies the conditions and guarantees under which sparse coding (for example, sparse autoencoders) can un-mix those features; (3) behaviourally grounded interpretability metrics test whether the recovered features actually correspond to human-legible concepts. Taken together, this converts "opening the black box" from an art into a theory-backed, falsifiable pipeline, and pins down where its guarantees currently break.

Why It Matters

  • Gives sparse autoencoders a theory, not just a heuristic. SAEs and dictionary learning have become the default tool for decomposing large models, yet they were justified largely empirically. Recasting them as a compressed-sensing recovery problem tells us when they are provably expected to work — and when their output is an artefact.
  • Explains why superposition exists. Instead of treating polysemantic neurons as an inconvenience, the identifiability step explains superposition as the predictable outcome of training objectives that fix latents only up to a linear transform — so linear mixing, not neuron-level meaning, is the expected state of a trained network.
  • Makes interpretability falsifiable. By insisting the third step be behavioural/quantitative rather than "these feature visualizations look meaningful", the framework directly answers long-standing critiques that interpretability research lacks testable claims.
  • Bridges neuroscience and AI on the same mathematics. Sparse coding in olfactory cortex, population codes in V1 and grid cells, and dictionary features in large language models become instances of one recovery problem — a genuine two-way transfer between theoretical neuroscience and AI transparency.
  • Directly relevant to AI safety and audit. If features can be recovered with guarantees, then feature-level monitoring, steering, and model auditing rest on something firmer than correlation — a prerequisite for interpretability evidence to be used in regulation or high-stakes deployment.

Core Methods

  • Step 1 — Identifiability analysis: apply nonlinear-ICA / identifiable-representation theory to show that a network trained on a discriminative objective (classification) recovers the ground-truth latent features up to a linear mixing. This is the formal statement of "features live in superposition": the information is there, but rotated and entangled across neurons.
  • Step 2 — Compressed-sensing recovery: treat the neuron-space activation vector as a random linear measurement of a high-dimensional sparse latent code, then import ℓ1 / sparse-coding recovery guarantees (Donoho-style compressed sensing, blind CS, dictionary-learning uniqueness) to state the conditions — dictionary size m, neuron count n, sparsity k — under which the un-mixing is exact.
  • Step 3 — Quantitative interpretability evaluation: score the recovered dictionary elements with behaviourally grounded, human- or machine-in-the-loop metrics (psychophysics-style feature-interpretation tasks, automated interpretability scoring) rather than qualitative visual inspection, so that "this feature is interpretable" becomes a measurable claim.
  • Scaling-law derivation: Figure 3 turns the compressed-sensing conditions into scaling relations that predict how dictionary recoverability degrades as feature count grows relative to width and sparsity — a testable phase-transition-style prediction for SAE practitioners.
  • Cross-domain synthesis: systematically map the same three-step pipeline onto biological recordings (V1, olfactory cortex, grid cells) and non-language AI domains (vision models, protein language models, cryo-EM latents) to argue the framework is modality-agnostic.
  • Gap analysis: explicitly enumerate where the guarantees fail in practice — SAEs trained on identical data learning different dictionaries, amortization gaps in SAE inference, "interpretability illusions", and the unresolved question of whether sparse probing buys real downstream utility.

① Turn Fig. 3's scaling law into a falsifiable, cross-modal benchmark

Extending this paperThe paper's most testable artefact is the compressed-sensing scaling law relating neuron count n, dictionary size m and sparsity k — but it is presented as theory-plus-illustration, not as a measured phase diagram. The direct extension is an empirical recovery benchmark: train dictionaries across a grid of (n, m, k) on models with known planted features, sweep across modalities (vision backbones, language models, and compact cortical models where n is genuinely small and superposition pressure is maximal), and chart where actual recovery departs from the predicted transition. A compact model of visual cortex with a few hundred units is the cleanest stress test the paper never runs: it is exactly the small-n, large-m regime where the theory should either shine or break.

Added value to my researchThis gives me a principled answer to a question I currently settle by cross-validation: how many latent urban/health factors can an embedding of dimension n actually carry before they become unrecoverable? When I compress multi-source features (POIs, mobile-phone flows, remote sensing, census) into a shared representation for spatial prediction, the scaling law becomes a design rule for embedding width — and a diagnostic for when my "interpretable" spatial components are just mixing artefacts.

② Close the loop from "interpretable feature" to causal intervention

Extending this paperStep 3 of the framework grades features by whether humans (or automated judges) can describe them — a fundamentally correlational criterion. A feature can score highly as "human-interpretable" while playing no causal role in the model's computation, which is precisely the "interpretability illusion" the paper itself flags. The natural extension is a fourth step: causal validation — ablate, clamp, or steer each recovered dictionary direction and require a predicted, specific behavioural change before certifying it. This would upgrade the framework from "the code is recoverable and legible" to "the code is recoverable, legible, and load-bearing", and would give the community a single number combining recovery guarantee, description quality, and causal effect size.

Added value to my researchMy explainable-ML work on spatial prediction leans on attribution (SHAP, MGWR surfaces) that is equally correlational. Importing this causal-certification discipline means I stop asking "which variable does the model attribute this accessibility gap to?" and start asking "if I intervene on this recovered spatial factor, does predicted access change as the theory says?" — a much stronger evidentiary standard for policy-facing claims about telehealth divides or primary-care shortage areas.

③ Apply the three-step pipeline to biological and sensor time series, not just static activations

Extending this paperThe paper's neuroscience bridge is argued through examples (V1, olfactory cortex, grid cells) but its recovery machinery assumes something close to i.i.d. sampling of static activation vectors. Real neural recordings are temporally autocorrelated, heavily subsampled, non-stationary and noisy — conditions under which compressed-sensing guarantees degrade in ways the review acknowledges but does not quantify. Running the full pipeline end-to-end on a large-scale foundation model of neural activity — identifiability of its latents, sparse un-mixing of its embedding, then behavioural scoring of the resulting dictionary against stimulus-response tasks — would be the first genuine test of whether the framework survives contact with biological data rather than proxy models.

Added value to my researchSwap "neurons over time" for "sensors over space-time" and this is my data: GPS trajectories, mobile-phone records, smart-card taps. A sparse dictionary learned over spatiotemporal activation streams would yield interpretable mobility motifs with recovery conditions attached — telling me when a decomposed "commuting component" or "healthcare-seeking component" is identifiable versus when I am reading structure into mixing. That is directly usable in my spatiotemporal KDE and job-housing network analyses.

④ Extend identifiability to spatially dependent data — an identifiability theory under Tobler's law

Extending this paperEvery guarantee in step 1 rests on assumptions about how the latent factors are distributed and sampled; the classic identifiability results the paper builds on assume independence across samples and, typically, independence across latent factors. Geographic, epidemiological and climate data violate both: neighbouring observations are correlated by construction, and latent factors (density, deprivation, greenness) are themselves spatially coupled. Extending the paper means asking what identifiability survives when samples are drawn from an autocorrelated field — does spatial dependence act as extra structure that helps recovery (like an additional auxiliary variable in nonlinear ICA), or as effective sample-size collapse that destroys it? A version of the paper's framework carrying an explicit dependence term would be a genuinely new theoretical contribution, and would tell Earth-system and geospatial foundation-model builders whether their embedding features are identifiable at all.

Added value to my researchThis is the single most valuable extension for me. It supplies the missing theory for a project I want to run: fit sparse autoencoders on the embeddings of a geospatial/Earth-system foundation model and ask whether the recovered directions are real urban concepts (industrial land, informal settlement, healthcare desert) or rotation artefacts. With a dependence-aware identifiability condition, that becomes a defensible method rather than a fishing expedition — and it slots straight into my fine-tuning-geospatial-foundation-models line and my open-science/FAIR reproducibility standards.

⑤ Test the framework's implicit premise: is post-hoc un-mixing ever preferable to an interpretable-by-design bottleneck?

Extending this paperThe whole three-step pipeline is post-hoc: let the network superpose, then recover. But if compressed sensing tells us the recoverability conditions, it equally tells us how to train into them — impose a sparse, overcomplete bottleneck at the capacity the theory prescribes and get identifiable features by construction, with no recovery step and no illusion risk. The paper never runs this comparison, yet its own theory supplies the design constants. A rigorous head-to-head — accuracy cost, feature stability across seeds (the review notes SAEs on identical data learn different dictionaries), and causal load-bearingness — would resolve whether superposition is an unavoidable efficiency of scale or an artefact of unconstrained training that we tolerate out of habit.

Added value to my researchThis maps directly onto the choice I face in every health-policy model: a black-box ensemble plus attribution, versus a constrained model whose components are auditable up front. In high-stakes settings — spatial accessibility of mental-health care, EV-charging equity allocation — an interpretable-by-design sparse spatial factor model that a planning agency can inspect is worth a small accuracy loss, and this paper's theory tells me how wide that bottleneck must be to not lose information.
