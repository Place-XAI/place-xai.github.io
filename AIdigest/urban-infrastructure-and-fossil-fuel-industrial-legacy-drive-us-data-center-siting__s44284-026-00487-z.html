Urban infrastructure and fossil fuel industrial legacy drive US data center siting

Camilla Ancona · Ofek Lauber Bonomo · Anton Rozhkov · Maurizio Porfiri Published 2026-07-31 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00487-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

US data centers are an overwhelmingly urban phenomenon — 97.5% of facilities sit inside metropolitan or micropolitan statistical areas, refuting the "rural cloud expansion" narrative. Their location logic is dominated by the nameplate capacity of nearby power plants, and secondarily by broadband quality, retired coal plants, IT employment and natural-hazard risk. Because siting piggybacks on local generation, it inherits each state's energy mix, sharpening geographic contrasts in how sustainable the boom actually is. Population-driven growth projections indicate the digital transition will reinforce existing urban hierarchies rather than decentralize — a path-dependent evolution welded to fossil-fuel industrial legacy, with real consequences for urban livability.

Why It Matters

  • Kills a policy-shaping myth with a number. "AI is moving to the countryside" underpins rural-development pitches, tax-incentive packages and grid-planning assumptions. The 97.5% metro/micropolitan share reframes data centers as an urban land-use and urban-livability problem, not a rural one.
  • Names the dominant driver: electricity, not land or labor. The strongest predictor is local power-plant nameplate capacity — siting follows the grid's existing hardware, which means the digital economy is being built on the generation stock a region already owns.
  • Documents a fossil-fuel lock-in mechanism. Retired coal plants — with their interconnection rights, transmission, water and industrial land already permitted — attract data centers, so the "cleanest" digital era is partly being poured into the footprint of the dirtiest previous one.
  • Turns siting into an energy-justice question. Because facilities draw on state-level generation mixes, identical racks in two states carry very different carbon and local-pollution burdens — and the load lands on communities already hosting legacy energy infrastructure.
  • Forecasts concentration, not diffusion. Population-based projections say the build-out will thicken the existing urban hierarchy, so grid, water, noise and zoning stress will compound where it already exists rather than spreading out.

Core Methods

  • Urban/rural classification of every facility: data-center points are intersected with Census metro/micropolitan statistical-area geometries (TIGER/Line, county–MSA–CSA crosswalk) to produce the headline 97.5% urban share — a deliberately simple, auditable geoprocessing step (ArcGIS/Esri).
  • Count regression of siting drivers: the number of data centers per spatial unit is modeled with negative binomial regression (Hilbe; statsmodels/SciPy), the standard treatment for overdispersed counts, ranking nameplate capacity, broadband quality, retired coal plants, IT employment and hazard risk.
  • Spatial dependence diagnostics: Moran's I via PySAL to test whether the residual siting pattern is spatially clustered rather than independent — the check that separates "drivers" from "neighborhood spillover."
  • Energy-mix coupling: facility footprints are joined to plant-level capacity and eGRID generation/emission characteristics, translating siting geography into state-level sustainability contrasts (Fig. 3).
  • Energy-community overlay: facilities are positioned relative to IRA EC designations and retired coal plants (Fig. 4), operationalizing "fossil-fuel industrial legacy" as a measurable spatial layer rather than a metaphor.
  • Scenario projection: population-based growth models propagate SSP-controlled county population trajectories into projected data-center counts across scenarios (Fig. 5), yielding a spatial spread rather than a single forecast.
  • Causal framing & robustness: interpretation is disciplined by an explicit causal-inference stance (Pearl) and by 12 supplementary discussions with 17 figures and 6 tables of sensitivity analyses.

① Break the global coefficient: spatially non-stationary, locally explainable siting

Extending this paperThe driver ranking rests on a global negative-binomial model — one coefficient per covariate for the entire country. But "nameplate capacity dominates" almost certainly means different things in ERCOT, PJM and the Pacific Northwest, where capacity, price, water and permitting bind differently. A direct extension: refit the same covariate set with MGWR (covariate-specific bandwidths) and with a gradient-boosted counterpart read through SHAP, producing a map of elasticities instead of a table of them. Wherever the local coefficient on retired-coal proximity is large, you have identified the specific metros where fossil legacy — not electricity abundance in general — is doing the work, which is exactly the geography a state legislature can act on. The paper's own Moran's I diagnostic is the entry point: significant residual autocorrelation is the statistical invitation to go local.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean, high-visibility testbed for my explainable ML for spatial prediction line: a count outcome, a policy-relevant covariate set, open processed data, and a published global baseline to beat. The MGWR-vs-SHAP comparison lets me push a methodological point I care about — that bandwidth heterogeneity and feature-attribution heterogeneity are two views of the same spatial non-stationarity — on a dataset where the audience already cares about the answer.

② Add the missing dependent variable: who breathes, hears and pays for the racks

Extending this paperThe paper explains where facilities go but stops short of who ends up next to them. Its own machinery makes the next step almost free: it already holds ACS population, eGRID emission intensity, the EVI energy-vulnerability layer and facility coordinates. Extending it means building a population-weighted exposure surface — distance-decayed residential exposure to data-center clusters, weighted by the marginal generation actually dispatched to serve them (gas peakers, revived coal, diesel backup) — and then testing whether exposure is disproportionately borne by low-income, minority or already energy-burdened tracts. That converts an interesting siting result into an environmental-justice finding with a health endpoint, and it directly tests the "urban livability" claim the abstract makes but does not quantify.

Added value to my researchStructurally this is a 2SFCA in reverse: instead of supply of care flowing to demand, a disamenity flows outward with distance decay and competition among nearby populations. My R2SFCA catchment machinery ports over directly, giving me a "reverse-accessibility" / exposure-burden formulation I can reuse for other locally unwanted land uses — and it links the digital build-out to the environmental determinants of health that anchor my public-health geography work.

③ Invert the broadband arrow: do data centers actually deliver digital access?

Extending this paperBroadband quality enters this paper as a predictor of siting. The far more consequential question is the reverse causal direction: once a hyperscale campus lands, does local digital service quality improve for residents, or does the community absorb the grid, water and land costs while the bandwidth serves customers three time zones away? Extending the paper means assembling a panel of broadband quality and latency before and after facility openings and running a difference-in-differences / staggered-adoption design against comparable non-hosting counties. The paper's causal framing (Pearl) and its EC-designation layer supply both the identification vocabulary and a plausible instrument-like discontinuity in incentive eligibility.

Added value to my researchThis is the missing supply-side layer of my telehealth and digital-divide work. If telehealth accessibility depends on latency and reliability rather than on nominal subscription rates, then a bandwidth-decay 2SFCA — where the impedance is round-trip latency to the serving facility rather than road distance — becomes a defensible metric of digital care access, and this paper's facility geography is the supply layer it needs.

④ Replace population-proportional projection with a co-evolving grid–demography simulator

Extending this paperFig. 5 projects growth by scaling with SSP-controlled county population — elegant, but it assumes the grid is a passive backdrop and that the very driver the paper identified (nameplate capacity) stays fixed. Yet capacity is being added because of data centers, interconnection queues are the actual binding constraint, and depopulating metros will not shed their generation stock. A stronger extension: an agent-based / physics-informed graph model where developers choose sites, utilities respond by adding or retiring capacity, and the transmission network enforces power-flow feasibility as a hard constraint — with Monte Carlo ensembles over SSP demography producing a distribution of futures rather than one spread. That would let the paper say when the urban-hierarchy reinforcement breaks down, not just that it holds.

Added value to my researchSquarely my physics-informed graph learning + agent-based modeling agenda: a real network with conservation laws (power flow) as the physics prior, siting decisions as the agent layer, and demography as the exogenous forcing. It also generalizes my EV charging-station efficiency-vs-equity optimization — same grid, same siting logic, same trade-off — into a single framework covering both loads that are reshaping urban electricity demand.

⑤ Turn "path dependence" from an adjective into a measured spatiotemporal process

Extending this paper"Path-dependent evolution" is the paper's headline interpretation, but the evidence is a cross-sectional snapshot — a single 2025 inventory. Path dependence is a claim about history and can only be tested longitudinally. The extension: reconstruct a facility-level opening-date panel (permits, utility interconnection filings, press releases) and model siting as a spatiotemporal point process — spatiotemporal KDE for the diffusion front, a survival/hazard model for how long after coal-plant retirement the first data center arrives, and network community detection on the capacity–facility bipartite graph to see whether clusters are inherited from the 20th-century industrial map or newly formed. Add spatial entropy over time and you can state quantitatively whether the build-out is concentrating or dispersing, replacing the projection-based inference with an observed trajectory.

Added value to my researchEvery method here is already in my toolkit — spatiotemporal KDE, spatial entropy, network community detection, Monte Carlo — applied to a subject with unusual policy traction. The coal-retirement-to-data-center hazard model is a transferable template for other legacy-conversion questions I study (hospital closures and care-desert formation, industrial land turning residential), where the interesting quantity is always the lag between an infrastructure's death and its reuse.
