Why cities cannot learn

Zaheer Allam Published 2026-07-09 DOI 10.1126/science.aeh8677 Source science.org ↗
Core Conclusion

Cities historically improved through a four-step learning loop: disruption produced evidence, evidence generated public and political pressure, and pressure forced governing agencies to modify rules. The Great Fire of London (1666) yielded building regulations within two years; recurrent Rhine and Danube flooding produced the zoning rules and hazard mapping now standard in European planning. The author argues this loop is now degrading on every inhabited continent — hazards arrive faster than institutions can convert them into codified learning — and that what replaces it is a "volatile urbanism" in which change is constant but cumulative adaptation, and therefore resilience, is lost.

★

Why It Matters

  • Reframes resilience as a learning problem, not an engineering problem. Most urban resilience work asks how strong the levee, the grid, or the building is. This piece relocates the failure one level up: the binding constraint is the institutional capacity to convert a disaster into a durable rule. A city with modern infrastructure but a broken learning loop is still fragile.
  • Names a mechanism with testable steps. "Disruption → evidence → pressure → modification" is a chain that can be instrumented empirically. Each link can be measured, and each can be shown to fail independently — turning a rhetorical worry into a research agenda.
  • Introduces "volatile urbanism" as a diagnostic concept. The claim is not that cities have stopped changing but that they change without accumulating. That distinction matters for policy: rapid construction, rapid retrofit, and rapid tech deployment can all coexist with declining resilience.
  • Global scope with historical depth. By anchoring on 1666 London and late-20th-century Rhine/Danube governance and then asserting continent-wide degradation, the argument spans centuries and continents, positioning urban learning as a long-run civilizational capacity rather than a local administrative detail.
  • Directly relevant to compounding climate hazards. If hazard frequency rises while learning latency stays flat or lengthens, the two curves cross — and cities enter a regime where each event arrives before the previous one has been institutionalized. That is a quantifiable tipping condition.

Core Methods

  • Conceptual model building. The central method is the articulation of a causal chain: disruption → evidence → pressure → modification by governing agencies. This is a mechanism sketch, offered as the general pathway by which urban systems historically improved.
  • Historical case reasoning. Two well-documented episodes (1666 London fire; late-20th-century Rhine/Danube flooding) are used as existence proofs that the loop once closed quickly and durably, establishing a baseline against which present-day performance is judged.
  • Comparative-historical contrast. The argument works by contrasting the historical baseline with the present, asserting a degradation of the pathway rather than a change in hazard exposure alone — locating the failure in institutional throughput.
  • Conceptual coinage and diagnosis. "Volatile urbanism" is introduced as the named replacement regime: high rates of change without cumulative adaptation. The concept functions as a diagnostic label for a state the author claims is now widespread.
  • What the methods are not. No statistical model, no systematic case selection protocol, no quantitative indicator, no replication material. The contribution is framing and hypothesis generation, and it should be read and cited as such.

① Turn "cities cannot learn" into a measurable quantity: an urban learning-latency survival model

Extending this paperThe paper's strongest asset is also its weakest: the 2-year London fire-to-regulation lag is one number in an argument that claims continental degradation. The direct extension is to make latency the dependent variable. Build a global event–policy panel by pairing hazard chronologies (flood, heat, fire, subsidence) with the dates of subsequent municipal regulatory amendments, mined at scale from ordinance archives and local news corpora with an LLM extraction pipeline. Then estimate a survival / time-to-event model for "time from disruption to codified rule change," with the event decade as the covariate of interest. If the author is right, the hazard ratio for policy adoption should decline monotonically across recent decades after conditioning on hazard severity and city capacity. This converts a rhetorical claim into a falsifiable curve — and would either vindicate the piece or bound its generality. Our corpus already contains the closest empirical precedent, a 549-city test of whether disasters actually induce adaptation actions, which supplies both a sampling frame and a benchmark the extension must beat.

Added value to my research"Institutional response latency" becomes a new spatiotemporal covariate for my health-geography work. The COVID-era telehealth policy rollout is exactly this structure: a shock, evidence of access collapse, pressure, then state-by-state reimbursement rule changes with wildly different lags. I can regress that lag surface on socio-spatial predictors with MGWR and test whether policy-response speed is itself spatially non-stationary — i.e., whether the digital divide is partly a governance-latency divide, not only an infrastructure divide. That would give my telehealth and primary-care accessibility work a temporal governance dimension it currently lacks.

② Find which link in the chain actually breaks — mediation analysis plus explainable ML on the four-step loop

Extending this paperThe paper asserts the whole pathway is degrading but never asks where the chain snaps. That is the most consequential unanswered question in the piece, because the policy fix differs completely by link: a broken disruption→evidence step needs monitoring infrastructure; a broken evidence→pressure step needs media and civic mobilization; a broken pressure→modification step needs regulatory throughput and accountability reform. Extend the paper by instrumenting each link separately — hazard occurrence, official damage assessment/declaration, media and petition volume, and enacted ordinance — then running causal mediation to allocate the total degradation across links, with SHAP-style attribution on a gradient-boosted model to identify which city attributes gate each transition. The corpus offers a sharp illustration of a break at the very first link: repeated, localized flooding often fails to trigger a formal disaster declaration, so the damage generates no official evidence, no federal funds, and therefore no learning — the loop dies before pressure can ever form.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct application of my explainable machine learning for spatial prediction line, but pointed at governance rather than at physical outcomes. The same architecture transfers to my mental-health and primary-care access work: model why documented shortage areas do not become funded shortage areas, and use SHAP maps to expose which spatial and demographic attributes suppress the evidence-to-action transition. That is a genuinely novel target for XAI in health geography — explaining institutional inaction rather than explaining a prediction.

③ Cities do not learn alone — model learning as diffusion on an intercity network

Extending this paperThe paper treats learning as an intra-city property: this city experiences a fire, this city writes a code. But the Rhine/Danube example the author himself cites is inherently trans-municipal — the hazard mapping became "standard across Europe," meaning cities learned from each other's disasters, not only their own. Extending the paper: recast learning capacity as a property of position in an intercity governance network (administrative twinning, personnel rotation, professional associations, economic coupling), and model rule adoption as diffusion over that network with community detection to identify "learning basins" — clusters that converge on shared codes — and structural holes where diffusion stalls. Two corpus results make this concrete and tractable: evidence that moving people between cities outperforms transplanting policy text as a mechanism of governance improvement, which tells you the correct edge weight is personnel flow rather than document similarity; and evidence that economic ties propagate disaster impacts across city systems, which means the same network carries both the shock and the potential lesson. The sharpest testable claim: volatility rises where a city is strongly coupled for shock transmission but weakly coupled for knowledge transmission.

Added value to my researchThis maps cleanly onto my urban agglomeration and regional collaboration line and reuses machinery I already run — network community detection and spatial entropy, previously applied to job–housing networks. It also supplies a policy-relevant target for the healthy-cities agenda: identify which cities in an agglomeration are net importers versus net exporters of health-governance innovation, and quantify the coupling asymmetry between economic integration and public-health knowledge integration.

④ Operationalize "volatile urbanism" as a multi-source spatiotemporal volatility index

Extending this paper"Volatile urbanism" is the piece's central coinage and it is currently a metaphor with no measurement. Extending the paper means giving it an index. Volatility here has a precise meaning — high rates of change with low persistence — so it should be measured as the ratio of change rate to retention of change, computed from data that already exist at global scale: built-form churn from multi-temporal satellite morphology, POI turnover and business survival from commercial datasets, mobility-pattern instability from mobile-phone and GPS trajectory entropy, and land-use transition frequency. A city that rebuilds constantly but whose spatial configuration never stabilizes scores high; a city that changes slowly but retains each change scores low. The critical test the paper implies but does not perform: does this volatility index predict the learning-latency measure from idea ①, and does it predict worse outcomes under a hazard stress test? Corpus work that stress-tests cascading flood impacts across hundreds of cities provides both the outcome variable and the shock-propagation framework this index would need to be validated against.

Added value to my researchSpatial entropy and spatiotemporal kernel density estimation are already core tools in my methods stack, and this is a new and prominent application for them — volatility as an entropy-rate over a spatiotemporal field rather than a static dispersion measure. It also creates a specific instrument for health geography: a service-stability index measuring whether the healthcare and mental-health facilities serving a neighborhood persist over time. Accessibility computed on a single snapshot silently assumes stability; a 2SFCA score is much less meaningful in a high-churn service landscape. Adding volatility turns my accessibility surfaces from static to dynamically reliable.

⑤ Build the counterfactual: an agent-based learning-loop simulator on a geospatial foundation model

Extending this paperThe paper's most provocative implication is a counterfactual it cannot evaluate: if the evidence step were automated and instantaneous, would cities learn again? Answering that requires a simulator. Extend the paper by building an agent-based model of the loop itself — hazard process, residents and civic groups generating pressure, agencies with finite regulatory throughput and re-election horizons, developers responding to codes — coupled to a fine-tuned geospatial foundation model that supplies the physical hazard-exposure surface the agents act on. Then run policy experiments: shorten the evidence lag, raise agency throughput, remove electoral discounting, and observe which intervention restores cumulative adaptation. Two corpus results discipline this design. A systematic map of machine learning for urban climate mitigation shows where ML has and has not been usefully applied in this domain, preventing the simulator from becoming yet another unvalidated tool. More importantly, work on the uneven geographies of urban knowledge production raises the sharpest critique of the whole enterprise: if the evidence layer is trained on data and literature concentrated in a handful of wealthy regions, then automating the evidence step would deepen the very asymmetry the paper laments — cities with the least representation in the training corpus would learn the least. The simulator must therefore treat evidence coverage as a spatially varying, auditable input, not a uniform given.

Added value to my researchThis unites three of my lines in one artifact: agent-based modeling, fine-tuning geospatial foundation models, and open science / FAIR reproducibility. Released as an open, versioned urban policy-learning digital twin with documented data provenance, it becomes reusable infrastructure rather than a one-off study. The health-policy version is immediate and valuable: simulate how fast a metropolitan region converts an observed mental-health service gap into a funded facility, and test which governance interventions actually shorten that path — the accessibility analogue of the fire-to-code lag, and a natural next step for my 2SFCA/R2SFCA and facility-siting work.
