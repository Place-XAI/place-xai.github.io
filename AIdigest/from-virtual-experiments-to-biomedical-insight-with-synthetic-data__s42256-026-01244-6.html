From virtual experiments to biomedical insight with synthetic data

Margarida Victoriano · Milena Pavlović · Geir Kjetil Sandve · Hélder P. Oliveira · Artur Rocha · Victor Greiff Published 2026-06-11 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01244-6 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Synthetic biomedical data — when generated with transparent, fully specified parameters — act as rule-based systems with access to ground truth at unconstrained scale, making them indispensable for developing and benchmarking ML in immunomics, genomics and proteomics where real data are scarce. The blocking problem is the "simulation to reality" (sim2real) gap: it is not established that performance on synthetic data predicts performance on experimental data, and no standardized sim2real benchmarks exist. The authors argue for multilayered validation frameworks — statistical fidelity, biological realism, domain adaptation and hybrid (synthetic + experimental) validation — as the precondition for trustworthy synthetic data and for predictive digital twins used in diagnosis and therapy design.

  • Reframes synthetic data as ground truth, not as a data substitute. The value proposition is not "more rows"; it is that the generative rule is known. Core Methods

  • Conceptual synthesis, not experiment. The method is a structured literature argument: survey synthetic-data practice across immunomics, genomics and proteomics, extract the recurring failure mode, and propose a corrective framework. No new algorithm is trained or benchmarked.
  • The realism–control taxonomy (Fig. 2). Data-generation approaches are ordered on a single trade-off axis: mechanistic rule-based simulators give full parameter transparency and exact ground truth but limited biological realism; deep generative models (GANs, VAEs, diffusion, sequence LMs) trained on experimental data give realism but obscure the generative rule, which destroys the ground-truth guarantee.
  • Ground-truth-driven benchmarking. Because the implanted signal (a binding motif, a regulatory edge, a variant effect) is known by construction, a model's recovery of that signal is directly measurable — the paper's argument for why synthetic data are epistemically different from augmented data.
  • sim2real diagnosis. The gap is decomposed into divergent statistical properties (marginal and dependence structure, sparsity, sharing/overlap patterns) and divergent biological properties (cross-reactivity, germline variation, batch and technical artefacts, population structure), each of which can silently break generalization.
  • Multilevel staged validation (Fig. 4). A ladder rather than a single score: (i) statistical fidelity against real distributions; (ii) biological realism/plausibility checks; (iii) downstream utility — does a model trained on synthetic data hold up on experimental data; (iv) privacy and governance clearance.
  • Domain adaptation and hybrid validation as the bridge. Techniques imported from robotics sim2real — domain randomization, bi-directional domain adaptation, sim-to-real augmentation search — combined with hybrid designs that mix synthetic training with limited real evaluation.
  • Metric portability critique. Generative metrics borrowed from images and molecules (FID, precision/recall, density/coverage, FCD) are examined for whether they mean anything on receptor sequences, omics matrices or spectra — a warning against uncritical metric transfer.
  • Interpretability as a validation instrument. Sparse autoencoders and feature-attribution on protein/single-cell foundation models are cited as ways to test whether a model learned the implanted rule or a shortcut — interpretability used as evidence, not decoration.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

① Turn "the sim2real gap" from a diagnosis into a measured coefficient

Extending this paperThe Perspective's strongest claim is also its softest: sim2real is asserted as an uncertainty but never quantified, because no standardized benchmark exists. The concrete extension is to define a sim2real transfer coefficient — for a family of methods, the rank correlation between the leaderboard obtained on a synthetic benchmark and the leaderboard obtained on the matched experimental benchmark — and to report it per task (receptor–antigen binding, GRN inference, MS peptide identification) and per generator. That converts Fig. 4's qualitative ladder into a single auditable number: a simulator is "validated" for a task when its coefficient is high enough that synthetic ranking substitutes for real ranking. It also exposes the interesting failure mode the paper does not separate — a simulator can be statistically faithful yet rank-inverting, or crude yet rank-preserving, and only the latter property is what benchmarking actually needs.

Added value to my researchThis is directly portable to geospatial health modelling, where I routinely validate on simulated landscapes: I can compute the same coefficient between models ranked on synthetic demand/supply surfaces and models ranked on real service-utilization data, giving an honest answer to "does my simulation study predict which accessibility model wins in practice?" It also disciplines agent-based and SIR work — an ABM calibrated to reproduce case curves may still mis-rank the interventions, which is the only ranking a policy client cares about.

② Add a dependence-structure axis to the realism–control spectrum

Extending this paperFig. 2 collapses generator quality onto one axis (realism vs control), but the sim2real failures the paper lists — receptor sharing patterns, population structure, batch effects, cross-reactivity — are all failures of dependence structure, not of marginal realism. A generator can match every univariate distribution and still destroy the correlation geometry that carries the biological signal. The extension is a second explicit axis for structural fidelity, with matched diagnostics: sharing/overlap spectra for repertoires, co-expression and GRN topology for omics, and hierarchical/nested structure for cohorts. A generator would then be certified as a point in a 2-D plane (control × structural fidelity), not on a line — which also explains why FID-style metrics imported from vision, as the paper notes, tell you so little here: they were designed for perceptual marginals, not for dependence.

Added value to my researchThe geospatial analogue of "biological realism" is spatial dependence, and it is exactly the property synthetic geodata most often lose. This gives me a principled validation checklist for synthetic populations and synthetic mobility: does the surrogate preserve Moran's I and the semivariogram, the distance-decay curve, the spatial heterogeneity that MGWR is meant to detect, and the entropy of trip-length distributions? A synthetic city that matches every marginal but flattens autocorrelation will make any spatial model look good for the wrong reason.

③ Close the loop: make hybrid validation an active, budgeted calibration protocol

Extending this paperThe paper recommends domain adaptation and hybrid validation but treats real data as a passive test set applied at the end. Extending it: reformulate hybrid validation as an active sim2real loop with an explicit experimental budget — the simulator proposes the regions of parameter space where synthetic and real behaviour are predicted to diverge most, a small number of real assays are spent there, and the generator's parameters are updated. This turns the paper's static ladder into a closed control system and gives a quantity the field currently lacks: the cost of closing the gap, measured in experiments per unit of transfer-coefficient gain. It also connects the argument to the lab-in-the-loop antibody-design literature the paper already cites, but as a validation instrument rather than a design instrument.

Added value to my researchHealth-geography simulation faces the same budget problem in a sharper form: ground truth costs surveys, and the real utilization data that could falsify a 2SFCA parameterization are expensive or restricted. An active loop tells me which counties or which telehealth cohorts to sample next to maximally reduce the divergence between my simulated accessibility surface and observed care-seeking — turning limited fieldwork or data-purchase budgets into an optimization problem rather than a convenience sample.

④ Use known-rule synthetic data as the only fair testbed for explainable AI

Extending this paperThe Perspective observes that synthetic data enable "interpretable model testing" and cites sparse autoencoders on protein and single-cell foundation models — but stops short of the obvious inversion: because the generative rule is known by construction, synthetic data are the only setting where an explanation method can be scored against a ground-truth explanation. The extension is an XAI-fidelity benchmark: implant a specified motif or regulatory edge, then measure whether attribution methods recover it, at what signal strength they fail, and how gracefully they degrade as nuisance structure is added. That reframes synthetic data as infrastructure for validating explanations, not just predictions — a contribution more durable than any single simulator, and one that gives the paper's "reliability" claim actual teeth.

Added value to my researchThis is the missing rigour in explainable ML for spatial prediction. I can generate landscapes with an implanted spatial process — a known non-stationary coefficient field, a known threshold interaction between density and accessibility — and then ask whether SHAP, PDPs or MGWR surfaces actually recover it, or whether they are reporting artefacts of spatial autocorrelation. It gives me a defensible answer to the reviewer question I get most often: how do you know your explanation is right, and not just plausible?

⑤ Ship a FAIR "simulation provenance card" so synthetic datasets become citable, governed objects

Extending this paperThe paper's entire case rests on generators with "transparent and fully specified parameters" — yet it offers no mechanism to make that transparency machine-checkable, which is why the field's synthetic datasets remain unreproducible in practice. The extension is a lightweight simulation provenance card: generator name and version, full parameter set, random seed, implanted signals and their strengths, the validation ladder results from Fig. 4, the measured sim2real coefficient, and the privacy/governance status under frameworks such as the EHDS. Registered alongside the dataset with a DOI, it makes a synthetic dataset a first-class FAIR research object that a reviewer can verify and a regulator can audit — the missing institutional layer between the paper's argument and its adoption.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly the artefact my open-science and privacy work needs. Mobile-phone traces, patient addresses and claims data cannot be shared, and synthetic surrogates are the standard workaround — but they are currently released without any statement of what spatial structure was preserved or what disclosure risk remains. A provenance card lets me publish reproducible synthetic mobility and synthetic-population datasets for accessibility research with an explicit, auditable claim about which properties survived, which is the precondition for anyone else building on them.
