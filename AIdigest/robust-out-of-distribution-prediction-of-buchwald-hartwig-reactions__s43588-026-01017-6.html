Robust out-of-distribution prediction of Buchwald–Hartwig reactions

Paulo Neves · Bo Hao · Santeri Aikonen · Justin B. Diccianni · Jörg K. Wegner · Philippe Schwaller · Iulia I. Strambeanu Published 2026-08-10 DOI 10.1038/s43588-026-01017-6 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

For Buchwald–Hartwig C–N cross-coupling, data diversity — not dataset size or model sophistication — is the decisive driver of out-of-distribution (OOD) generalization. By standardizing eight published HTE datasets into one unique-structure-per-entity corpus (~27.5k reactions) and adding 11.3k newly generated, actively-learned HTE reactions, a plain random forest reaches >90% ROC AUC on high-confidence OOD predictions. The authors' new Compound-Reaction Diversity Score (CRDS) correlates with OOD ROC AUC at r = 0.79, versus r = 0.60 for dataset size alone. Wet-lab validation confirmed it: 10 of 11 historically failed substrate pairs were rescued with only 4 predicted conditions each, and high-confidence picks from a ~2.46 × 1011-reaction virtual space gave a 1.64× enrichment over a diversity-selected baseline.

Why It Matters

  • It reframes the scaling debate as a diversity problem. The field's default reflex is "more data, bigger model." Here, models trained on smaller-but-more-diverse sources beat larger, narrower ones on strict OOD clusters — and the effect is quantified rather than asserted, via CRDS (Pearson r = 0.79 vs 0.60 for size).
  • It supplies a prospective planning metric, not just a post-hoc diagnosis. CRDS can be computed before a data-production campaign, so a lab can ask "will this plate design buy me generalization?" instead of discovering the answer after spending the reagents.
  • Calibrated uncertainty converts a classifier into an operational screening tool. Confidence-binned performance means the model can say "I do not know" — the property that makes in-silico reagent recommendation deployable rather than decorative.
  • Predictions were tested in the lab, prospectively. 44 recommended reactions rescued 10/11 dead substrate pairs (27 successes at >10% yield); an entirely novel chemical domain gave 33% success under high confidence versus ~19% baseline. Claims are falsifiable and were falsification-tested.
  • The curated corpus and code are open. The unified BH HTE dataset (GitHub + Zenodo) becomes shared infrastructure for the reaction-prediction community — the reusable asset outlives the specific model.
  • Simple models win when the representation is right. Feature representation mattered more than model family; a random forest on DRFP + reagent descriptors sufficed. That is a real, transferable lesson about where effort should go.

Core Methods

  • Two-phase data production. Phase 1 builds a baseline model from a designed set governed by four factors: informer set, structure-diversity criteria, space-density criteria, and undesired-substructure filtering. Phase 2 switches to model-guided design.
  • Ranked Batch-Mode Active Learning. Iterations 2–3 select reactions from the 64M virtual space by combining model uncertainty with Tanimoto distance to previous selections, plus diversity and density terms — explicitly trading exploitation against coverage. Balanced accuracy improves monotonically across iterations (Fig. 2).
  • Featurization. DRFP (differential reaction fingerprint) over the full reaction, a vector of physicochemical reagent descriptors, and a split representation (substrate–product DRFP + reagent physicochemical descriptors); plus a SMILES-based model using BERT Enriched Embedding, an extension of a transformer yield predictor.
  • Triple debiasing protocol. Model bias → 6 classical ML models × 2 feature types + 1 SMILES transformer; data bias → OOD by DRFP + k-means clustering; metric bias → 8 metrics screened to 4 (AUPRC, balanced accuracy, F1, ROC AUC).
  • Strict OOD evaluation. k = 12 k-means clusters over DRFPs (reagents and substrates); per-source tests use only clusters where that source is unrepresented, and any test reaction whose aryl halide or amine appears in training is deleted. Combined-dataset tests use 10 iterations × 4 cycles of 3-held-out/9-training clusters until every cluster is tested once.
  • CRDS (Compound-Reaction Diversity Score). A data-centric score combining unique reactant pairs, unique reagent combinations and dataset size, with adjustable thresholds inducing diminishing returns and tunable weights. Exponents were empirically fitted to BH chemistry — the authors state recalibration would be needed for other transformations.
  • Uncertainty calibration. Predictions binned into five confidence bins, confidence derived inversely from uncertainty and normalized to [0.5, 1]; calibration curves assessed over 20 independent cluster initializations against the perfect-calibration diagonal.
  • Reagent recommender. Binary reactivity classifier + reagent dictionary + uncertainty ranking = an HTE plate designer that proposes both substrates and reagents; shipped as a self-contained notebook.
  • Hyperparameter study. RF, histogram gradient boosting (XGB) and MLP tuned across the two representations; conclusion — feature representation dominates model family for OOD performance.
  • Statistics. Ten random seeds (random_state 0–9) for benchmarking, random_state 42 for training; mean ± s.d. reported; no randomization or blinding of experiments; no data excluded.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

The bridge is structural: this is a paper about generalizing to unsampled regions of a heterogeneous space, which is the geographer's problem stated in chemical coordinates.

① Turn the k-means OOD protocol into a severity-graded shift curve

Extending this paperThe paper's OOD test is binary in spirit: fix k = 12, hold out clusters, delete training substrates, report one number per metric. But "out of distribution" is not a state, it is a distance. A direct extension: parameterize the split by a measurable shift magnitude — mean Tanimoto distance from held-out cluster centroid to the training manifold, or CRDS gap between train and test — and report performance as a continuous function of shift severity rather than a single hexagon. That would tell a synthetic chemist not just "the model generalizes" but how far it generalizes before collapsing, and would expose whether the JnJ25 gain is a uniform lift or a rescue only of near-boundary clusters. The anomalous-diffusion work in our corpus does exactly this kind of graded reliability analysis for a physical inference model and supplies the template.

Added value to my researchThis is the single most portable idea here for my work. Random k-fold CV on spatial data is structurally the same mistake as random splits on reaction data — neighboring tracts leak into each other and inflate accuracy. Adopting the paper's protocol wholesale gives me geo-cluster holdout with a spatial-shift severity axis: cluster census tracts or cities by feature-space fingerprint, hold out whole clusters, and plot telehealth-access or mental-health-accessibility model skill against geographic/covariate shift distance. Every accessibility model I publish could carry an "extrapolation range" statement instead of a single R². For fine-tuning geospatial foundation models this becomes the acceptance test: does the fine-tuned model still work in a city climate/morphology cluster it never saw?

② Learn the CRDS weights instead of fitting them — and build a spatial cousin

Extending this paperCRDS is the paper's most reusable invention and also its most fragile: the authors admit the weighting exponents were empirically fitted to BH chemistry and would need recalibration elsewhere. The extension is to stop hand-fitting. Treat CRDS as a small parametric function whose arguments are (unique reactant pairs, unique reagent combinations, size, and — newly — coverage entropy over the DRFP manifold), then learn its parameters by meta-regression across reaction classes, using each published HTE dataset as one training instance with its measured OOD ROC AUC as the target. That converts a bespoke score into a calibrated, cross-chemistry predictor of generalization, and would let a lab estimate the marginal OOD return of the next 1,000 wells before committing them. It also directly tests the paper's boldest claim — that diversity dominates size — outside the single reaction class where it was observed.

Added value to my researchI want the geographic analogue: a Spatial-Contextual Diversity Score that scores a candidate sampling design (which cities, which tracts, which time windows) by predicted transferability rather than by n. My accessibility and telehealth studies constantly face the question "is this sample enough to generalize to rural Appalachia / to a different state's Medicaid regime?" — currently answered by intuition. Formalizing coverage over a joint (built-environment, socio-demographic, provider-supply) fingerprint space and validating it against measured out-of-region error would give grant reviewers and health departments a defensible sampling rationale. It also reframes multi-source fusion: fusing mobile-phone + POI + remote-sensing data is worth it precisely when it raises coverage entropy, not when it raises row count.

③ Replace five confidence bins with conformal prediction and a cost-aware decision layer

Extending this paperFig. 4's calibration analysis is what makes the model usable, but binning into five confidence buckets gives no finite-sample guarantee — it is empirical calibration measured on the same cluster resamples. Extending it with split or Mondrian conformal prediction, calibrated per OOD cluster, would attach a distribution-free coverage guarantee to each recommendation ("this reagent set succeeds with ≥80% probability at 90% coverage"), which is materially stronger for a pharma go/no-go decision. Layer on the economics the paper leaves implicit: each well has a reagent and instrument-time cost, each rescued substrate has a program value, so the recommender should maximize expected value per plate rather than rank by confidence alone. The paper already reports the ingredients (1.64× enrichment, 19% baseline, 1,092 candidate conditions); the missing step is the decision-theoretic wrapper. Uncertainty-driven active learning for interatomic potentials shows how uncertainty can steer the sampling dynamics and not merely score them.

Added value to my researchSpatial prediction in health geography is a high-stakes decision problem dressed up as a regression. A conformal + cost-aware layer maps directly onto where to site the next clinic, charging station, or telehealth hub: instead of a point-predicted accessibility surface, produce prediction sets with guaranteed coverage, then optimize expected equity gain per dollar. This is the rigorous version of my efficiency-versus-equity EV-charging optimization, and it gives my R2SFCA outputs an uncertainty band — something 2SFCA-family methods have historically lacked entirely, which is a genuine gap I could own.

④ Take the "representation > model" finding seriously and go fully interpretable

Extending this paperThe paper's quiet bombshell is that a random forest on a well-chosen split representation beat everything else, and that feature representation mattered more than model family. The authors explain it mechanistically — tree splits on binary fingerprint bits are literally "substructure present/absent" decisions. The extension writes itself: if the winning model is already a decision ensemble over interpretable indicators, extract the rules. Mine the forest for the substructure–reagent interactions that drive predicted failure, cross-check them against known catalytic poisons and steric/electronic effects, and publish a human-readable failure atlas alongside the model. That converts a black-box screening tool into transferable chemical knowledge — and it would test whether the model has learned chemistry or has memorized dataset provenance, which the CRDS analysis hints at but cannot separate. Rudin's argument that high-stakes domains should use inherently interpretable models rather than post-hoc explanations is the strongest available justification for this direction.

Added value to my researchThis validates and sharpens my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction line. The lesson I take: before reaching for a deeper architecture, fix the representation — separate structural identity from contextual attributes, exactly as the paper separates substrate DRFP from reagent descriptors. The spatial analogue is separating place identity (network topology, morphology fingerprint) from place attributes (demographics, supply), and letting a tree ensemble split on the former while regressing on the latter. That is a cleaner, more defensible design than the SHAP-on-a-black-box pattern that dominates GeoAI papers, and it dovetails with MGWR, where the whole point is that local coefficient surfaces are themselves the interpretable output.

⑤ Close the loop causally: from uncertainty sampling to intervention design

Extending this paperRanked Batch-Mode Active Learning picks the reactions the model is most unsure about, weighted by distance from prior picks. That is an epistemic criterion, not a causal one: it maximizes information about the predictive function, not about which reaction components cause failure. Recasting phase 2 as optimal intervention design over a causal model of the reaction system — where catalyst, base and solvent are manipulable nodes — would let the campaign choose experiments that identify mechanism-level effects transferable to reagent classes never synthesized, rather than merely patching the model's blind spots. This is the honest route past the paper's own stated limitation that predictions for novel reagent classes "should be interpreted accordingly," and it is what would let the framework survive the jump to other transformations that the accompanying News & Views frames as the real prize.

Added value to my researchMy agent-based models and SIR-style simulations are causal generators; my ML accessibility models are correlational predictors; they rarely talk to each other. This paper's loop — model proposes, experiment disposes, corpus grows — is the template for coupling them, with ABM playing the role of the HTE plate and active learning choosing which policy scenario to simulate next. Concretely: for a telehealth-expansion or clinic-siting study, use intervention-design active learning to pick the small set of counterfactual configurations worth simulating expensively, then let the surrogate cover the rest. It also settles a reproducibility principle I care about: the paper's real deliverable is the curated, FAIR, unique-entity-per-structure corpus, not the model — a standard my own multi-source fusion pipelines should be held to.
