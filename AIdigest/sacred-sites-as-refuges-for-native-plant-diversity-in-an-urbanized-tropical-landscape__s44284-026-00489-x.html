Sacred sites as refuges for native plant diversity in an urbanized tropical landscape

Luc Schmid · Adrienne Grêt-Regamey · Hoby Nambinintsoa Nomenjanahary · Aro Vonjy Ramarosandratana · Harison Rabarison · Sariaka Ramamonjisoa · Tokiniaina Ramaroson · Johnson Gabriel Andrianantenaina · Michiel van Breugel Published 2026-07-24 DOI 10.1038/s44284-026-00489-x Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

In Antananarivo, Madagascar, a field inventory of 25,261 naturally regenerating woody individuals (206 species, 39% native) across 329 vegetation patches shows that native species richness declines steeply along gradients of building density/complexity and socio-economic development, while exotic richness shows no consistent urban signal. The strongest positive predictor is cultural, not biophysical: expected native richness is about 2× higher in 32 'sacred' patches than in 297 non-sacred ones (β̂ = 0.70 ± 0.15 SE, P < 0.001, after spatial correction), and seven of the ten most species-rich patches sit on pre-colonial sacred royal hills. Socio-cultural institutions — ancestral rules and fady taboos — thus function as de-facto conservation infrastructure, and are simultaneously an opportunity and a barrier for tropical urban restoration.

★

Why It Matters

  • Fills the largest blind spot in urban ecology. Nearly all urban-biodiversity evidence comes from affluent temperate cities; this is one of the first comprehensive field assessments of spontaneous woody vegetation in an Afrotropical, lower-income city — precisely where urban growth will be fastest.
  • Culture outranks greenness as a predictor. The paper shows a socio-cultural variable (sacred status) carrying more explanatory weight for native diversity than most urban-form metrics — an argument that biodiversity models missing institutional/heritage variables are misspecified, not merely incomplete.
  • Overturns imported planning heuristics. No 'luxury effect', no peri-urban diversity peak, no native-diversity benefit from private gardens — three regularities from Global-North cities that fail here, warning against transplanting 3–30–300-style rules unmodified into tropical cities.
  • Regeneration, not standing canopy, is the resilience signal. By restricting analysis to unassisted recruits (DBH ≤ 5 cm), the study measures whether the system can renew itself — a far stricter test of ecological resilience than tree-cover percentages.
  • Directly actionable conservation targets. A handful of named hills (Ambohimanga, Antsahadinta, Ambohijoky, Alasora, Ilafy, Ambohidratrimo, Analamanga) concentrate the region's remaining native regeneration — a small, mappable, already socially protected estate that restoration policy can build on.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Study area
Antananarivo, Madagascar (18.9185° S, 47.5211° E) plus peri-urban and rural surroundings — a ~40 × 40 km² window at ~1,200–1,600 m a.s.l., containing 856 Fokontany (smallest administrative unit) classified into four urban–rural classes by Jenks natural breaks on % impervious surface. City population ~3 million, growing ~5%/yr.
Field inventory
29,341 woody individuals recorded; analyses restricted to the 25,261 with DBH ≤ 5 cm (natural-regeneration proxy; >95% estimated unassisted recruits). 206 species across 55 families — 81 native (39%), 107 exotic (52%), 18 undetermined (9%); 99% of individuals identified/validated. 329 vegetation patches (32 sacred, 297 non-sacred), 604 transects of 40 m² (10 × 4 m, split into ten 2 × 2 m squares), 1–6 transects per patch, mean 42.5 individuals per transect.
Time period
Field sampling June 2022 – January 2024; census predictors from Madagascar's 2018 RGPH-3; satellite imagery for delineation/predictors from 2024–2025 Google layers.
Predictors
30 candidate variables → 12 final predictors in three thematic blocks: urban form & configuration (UFC, 12 → PC1–PC3 explaining >91% of variance), socio-economic & cultural (SEC, 13 → socio-economic index PC1 at 71.5% variance, plus population density, neighborhood age, cultural–spiritual significance) and environment & landscape (EL, 5: patch size, canopy openness, surrounding tree cover, topography, vegetation type). All VIF < 1.78; 100 m buffer chosen after testing 25/50/100 m radii (Pearson r > 0.85 across scales).
Urban morphometrics
Derived from an open building-footprint map (continental-scale building detection from high-resolution satellite imagery): building-level area, shape index, compactness, elongation and orientation (IQR + median), plus building density, nearest-neighbor index (NNI), area-weighted compactness index (AWCI) and aggregation index (AI, computed on 1-m rasterized footprints).
Sacred-site labels
Not from any dataset — classified by triangulating local landowners/inhabitants, Fokontany chiefs, traditional site guardians, Malagasy co-author expertise and historical/UNESCO literature. Seven named pre-colonial hill sites appear in Fig. 1 (Analamanga, Ambohijoky, Antsahadinta, Ambohimanga, Ilafy, Ambohidratrimo, Alasora).
Availability
Spatial, predictor and analysis datasets plus code are open on figshare (10.6084/m9.figshare.29125403). Honest caveat: the full vegetation inventory, complete species list and herbarium scans are deliberately withheld pending a standalone botanical publication led by the Malagasy co-authors (available from the corresponding author on request).
Stated unknowns
The paper does not report a total count of sacred sites in the region (only that 32 of 329 sampled patches are sacred), nor patch-area totals in the main text; sampling coverage per urban class is uneven by design (Class 1: 42/105 Fokontany; Class 2: 112/133; Class 3: 86/181; Class 4: 89/437) and the authors flag accessibility/safety constraints in the densest core. No figure is given for how much sacred land is legally protected.

Core Methods

  • Stratified-random field design: 856 Fokontany classified into four urban–rural classes (Jenks breaks on % impervious), random draws within class, 1–7 patches per selected Fokontany; patches delineated as structurally and management-homogeneous units not split by major roads or buildings.
  • Regeneration filter: only individuals > 5 cm tall with DBH ≤ 5 cm retained, with planted stock excluded via field observation of growth form/size heterogeneity plus landowner accounts of land-use history.
  • Dimensionality reduction: PCA on z-scored UFC variables (PC1–PC3, >91% variance: building-level metrics → PC1, AWCI/AI → PC2, density/NNI → PC3) and on 10 census housing variables (PC1 = socio-economic index, 71.5% variance).
  • Local-scale model: additive negative-binomial GLM on 12 predictors with a log offset for transects per patch, full-subset dredging (MuMIn), AICc ranking and model averaging over ΔAICc ≤ 2, reported as incidence rate ratios with Akaike-weight importance; McFadden's pseudo-R² = 0.15 (native) vs 0.03 (exotic).
  • Robustness battery: zero-inflation tested against ZINB by AICc + parametric bootstrap LR (200 sims) and zero simulation (n = 1,000); two-way-interaction global model re-run; quadratic/cubic terms tested and rejected; max VIF ≈ 3 in top models.
  • Spatial-confounding control: Moran's I (k = 8 nearest neighbors) on Pearson residuals, NB-GLMMs with Fokontany/commune random intercepts for the coarsely-resolved predictors, and a mgcv GAM with a bivariate smooth of projected x/y — the sacred effect survives (natives P < 0.001; exotics remain non-significant).
  • Confounding audit of "sacred": drop-one model comparisons show removing vegetation type raises the sacred coefficient from 0.49 ± 0.14 to 0.72 ± 0.15 (LR = 5.73, ΔAIC = 3.7, P = 0.02), demonstrating a partly independent cultural signal.
  • Landscape-scale estimation: coverage-based rarefaction/extrapolation (iNEXT::estimateD, q = 0, Chao1-type) standardized at 95% sample coverage with 50-bootstrap CIs, across tertile-binned or categorical versions of 12 selected variables.
  • Group comparison: two-sided Brunner–Munzel rank-order tests with Holm adjustment plus Cliff's δ (native δ = 0.61, 95% CI [0.43, 0.75]) — chosen for robustness to the extreme 32-vs-297 group-size imbalance.
  • Stack: QGIS 3.40.3 + Google Earth Engine for preparation; Python (GeoPandas, Shapely, Rasterio, SciPy) for morphometrics; R (MASS, MuMIn, AICcmodavg, emmeans, mgcv, spdep, vegan, iNEXT, ggplot2) for modeling and diversity estimation.

① Replace AICc dredging with explainable ML that maps where each driver bites

Extending this paperThe paper's inference engine is a globally-stationary NB-GLM: one IRR per predictor for the whole 40 × 40 km window, with non-linearity explicitly tested and discarded and spatial structure handled as a nuisance to be smoothed away by a GAM. Yet its own McFadden R² gap (0.15 native vs 0.03 exotic) and the drop-one instability of the sacred coefficient (0.49 → 0.72) are symptoms of interaction and non-stationarity, not noise. A direct extension: refit the same 12 predictors with a gradient-boosted ensemble + SHAP and with MGWR, so that "building density suppresses native richness" becomes a surface — steep on the western plain, flat on the hills — and so that the sacred × vegetation-type entanglement is read off an interaction value instead of a model-deletion experiment. SHAP dependence plots would also test whether the socio-economic gradient is truly monotone or has the threshold shape the tertile analysis hints at.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-spatial-prediction workflow applied to an ecological response with an unusually clean cultural treatment variable — a rare natural experiment for validating that SHAP-derived local importances actually recover known place-based institutions. If MGWR bandwidths align with the sacred-hill geography, I gain an empirical argument that spatial non-stationarity is often institutional rather than statistical, which is exactly the framing I want for my health-accessibility non-stationarity work.

② Scale the Antananarivo signal to the Afrotropics with building-footprint morphometrics + a geospatial foundation model

Extending this paperEvery one of the paper's urban-form predictors — density, NNI, AWCI, aggregation index, four building-shape IQRs — is computed from a continental-scale open building-footprint layer that exists for all of sub-Saharan Africa, and its socio-economic index comes from a census schema replicated across many African states. The 329 field patches are therefore a label set, not a ceiling. The obvious extension: train a model mapping (footprint morphometrics + census PCA + terrain + canopy) → native/exotic regeneration richness on Antananarivo, then transfer it to a dozen Afrotropical cities, using fine-tuned geospatial foundation-model embeddings of the same imagery to carry texture information the hand-crafted metrics miss. The critical honesty check is built in: the "sacred" predictor cannot be remotely sensed, so the transfer error map itself becomes a detector of unmapped culturally protected refugia.

Added value to my researchThis is a textbook case for my multi-source fusion + foundation-model fine-tuning line, with a twist I care about: the residual is the product. A model that predicts biodiversity well from morphometrics everywhere except where an unobserved institution operates gives me a general "institutional residual" detector — reusable for finding informal clinics, unregistered care networks, or community facilities that my accessibility models systematically miss.

③ Turn sacred refugia into a supply layer: 2SFCA accessibility to native biodiversity

Extending this paperThe paper establishes an extreme concentration of value — seven of the ten richest patches in a handful of peripheral hills — but stops at the ecological description and never asks the distributional question: who can reach these refugia? Extending it: treat each patch as a supply point weighted by coverage-standardized native richness (the paper's own iNEXT estimates), take Fokontany-level population as demand, and run an R2SFCA over the walking/road network to produce a native-biodiversity exposure index for Antananarivo. Overlay with the socio-economic PC1 and the finding sharpens from "urbanization reduces native diversity" to "the poorest and densest quarters are also the furthest from the only remaining native gene pools" — or, possibly, the opposite, since the socio-economic gradient here runs against Global-North intuition.

Added value to my researchDirectly extends my 2SFCA/R2SFCA family into a non-facility, non-substitutable supply type: unlike clinics, a sacred grove cannot be duplicated, so the catchment-competition term must be replaced by a congestion/trampling penalty that itself degrades the resource. That is a genuinely new functional form for my accessibility toolkit, and it pairs naturally with my green-space-and-mental-health interests, where quality of exposure has always been the weak link.

④ Model fady as a decaying institution: agent-based simulation of custodianship under urban encroachment

Extending this paperThe paper's causal story is explicitly speculative on the mechanism side: it names ancestral rules and fady taboos against cutting endemic plants near tombs, but concedes these "operate over historical and socio-cultural dimensions not captured by our spatial and compositional data", and that sacred status is partly confounded with peripherality and low building density. The extension that would resolve this is not another regression but a coupled agent-based model: custodians, in-migrant households and land developers acting on a landscape whose patches carry both a regeneration process and a taboo-compliance state variable that erodes as neighborhood turnover rises. Calibrating compliance decay against the paper's neighborhood-age classes (pre-colonial / −1975 / −2022) and running it forward under 5%-per-year growth would convert a static contrast into a forecast of which sacred hills lose their protection first.

Added value to my researchMy agent-based and Monte Carlo work has mostly encoded physical constraints; here the state variable is normative. Building a compliance-decay module gives me a transferable component for policy-adherence problems I actually face — vaccination and screening uptake, informal-care norms, EV-charging etiquette — and a way to represent institutions as spatial fields rather than dummy variables.

⑤ From isolated patches to a dispersal graph: physics-informed graph learning of source–sink regeneration

Extending this paperThe paper treats its 329 patches as independent observations — spatial autocorrelation (Moran's I = 0.20 for natives) is corrected away as a statistical nuisance, even though the paper's own narrative is a dispersal story: Asteraceae dominate natives because of wind dispersal and persistent seed banks, and high-diversity non-sacred patches are described as "typically adjacent to sacred sites". That adjacency is the signal. Reformulating the landscape as a graph — nodes = patches, edges weighted by wind/bird/human dispersal kernels attenuated by building density and hard barriers — and fitting a physics-informed GNN with a mass-conservation-style propagule budget would let the model estimate which hills are net sources and quantify how much native richness each sacred site exports to its neighbors. The autocorrelation the paper removes becomes the quantity of interest.

Added value to my researchThis is my physics-informed graph-learning agenda in its cleanest form: a conserved quantity (propagules) diffusing over a built-environment-modulated network, with ground-truth composition at 329 nodes. The same operator — sources, barrier-attenuated edges, conserved flow — is what I need for spatiotemporal disease seeding and for job-housing/service network diffusion, and the ecological setting offers something my mobility datasets rarely do: an independently observed, non-behavioral outcome to validate the learned kernel against.
