Generative design of bacteriophages with genome language models

Samuel H. King · Claudia L. Driscoll · David B. Li · Daniel Guo · Aditi T. Merchant · Garyk Brixi · Max E. Wilkinson · Brian L. Hie Published 2026-08-06 DOI 10.1126/science.aec2657 Source science.org ↗
Core Conclusion

A genome language model can write an entire viable genome, not just a gene. Using the small ssDNA phage ΦX174 as a design template, the authors generated complete bacteriophage genomes de novo; synthesis and experimental testing yielded 16 viable phages with diverse fitness profiles and the intended host tropism. Cryo-EM showed one generated phage assembles a capsid using an evolutionarily distant DNA-packaging protein — a genuinely novel solution, not a memorized copy. A cocktail of generated phages rapidly cleared ΦX174-resistant E. coli, sketching a concrete path to AI-generated phage therapies against fast-evolving bacterial pathogens.

★

Why It Matters

  • The unit of generative design moves from molecule to genome. Prior generative biology designed proteins, binders, or single genes. This is the first report of complete, functional genomes written by a model — a system whose viability depends on interactions among all its parts, not on any one sequence being "good."
  • Function was validated in the wet lab, not only in silico. The claim rests on plaque-forming, host-killing phages and a cryo-EM structure — an end-to-end design → synthesis → assay loop, which is the standard generative biology usually fails to meet.
  • Novelty is structurally demonstrable. Recruiting an evolutionarily distant packaging protein into a working capsid shows the model recombines across evolutionary space rather than interpolating near the template — the crux of the "is it generative or is it retrieval?" debate.
  • A therapeutic route around resistance. Bacteria evolve away from any single phage; a design engine that emits diverse phages on demand converts phage therapy from a "find one in a sewer" search problem into a generation problem, directly addressing the antimicrobial-resistance crisis.
  • It forces the biosecurity conversation into the open. The reference list explicitly engages nucleic-acid-synthesis screening, dual-use data governance, and built-in safeguards for generative biology tools — the capability and its guardrails arrive together.

Core Methods

  • Genome-scale language modeling. A genome language model (Evo-lineage, per the citations) is used to generate complete phage genomes autoregressively, so that gene content, gene order, overlapping reading frames, and regulatory context are produced jointly rather than gene-by-gene.
  • Template-anchored conditioning. ΦX174 serves as the design template that fixes the architectural class (small ssDNA microvirus) and the target host tropism, letting generation explore sequence space while staying inside a synthesizable, assayable envelope.
  • Model adaptation and retrieval. LoRA low-rank adaptation and retrieval-augmented generation are cited as the mechanisms for steering a general genome LM toward the phage design distribution.
  • Computational triage before synthesis. Generated genomes are screened with standard phage genomics tooling — gene calling (PHANOTATE/Prodigal/orfipy), annotation (Bakta, pLannotate), homology search (BLAST+, PHROG), and structure prediction (AlphaFold 3) — to filter candidates worth building.
  • Synthesis, rebooting, and phenotyping. Candidate genomes are chemically synthesized and booted in E. coli; viability is read out as plaque formation, and the surviving designs are profiled for diverse fitness under laboratory conditions — yielding the 16 reported phages.
  • Structural verification by cryo-EM. A generated phage's capsid is solved by cryo-electron microscopy (cryoSPARC pipeline), establishing that it incorporates an evolutionarily distant DNA-packaging protein — direct structural evidence of novel, functional recombination.
  • Cocktail challenge against resistance. Multiple generated phages are combined and challenged against ΦX174-resistant E. coli, testing whether generative diversity can outpace bacterial escape — the therapeutic proof-of-concept.
  • Biosecurity framing as method, not afterword. The cited governance corpus (synthesis screening frameworks, dual-use data-of-concern, built-in safeguards for generative AI, watermarking of generative biology outputs) indicates deliberate constraint of the design space and screened ordering.

① Condition the generator on where the pathogen is, not just what it is

Extending this paperThe paper conditions generation on a design template (ΦX174) and a target host tropism defined in the laboratory. But the bacteria that phage therapy must actually beat are geographically structured: resistance genes, plasmid backbones, and receptor variants differ between a hospital in Delhi and one in Boston. A direct extension: make the conditioning vector place-specific — fine-tune the genome LM (LoRA, as already cited) on locally sampled host metagenomes from wastewater and clinical isolates, so the model emits a phage panel matched to a city's resident resistome rather than to a reference strain. That turns the paper's single template into a family of geographically indexed design targets, and makes "host tropism" an epidemiological coordinate instead of a bench parameter.

Added value to my researchThis gives my spatiotemporal surveillance work a downstream action layer. Wastewater-based resistome mapping is exactly the kind of multi-source, gridded, spatiotemporal signal I already model with kernel density estimation and hotspot detection — but until now it terminated in a map. Coupling it to a generative design engine defines a new deliverable: a "design-target surface", where each catchment gets a ranked list of bacterial targets weighted by prevalence, growth rate, and population exposure. Methodologically it is a clean fit for my multi-source fusion pipeline (sewershed boundaries as service areas, population disaggregation as denominators, mobility as the connectivity term).

② Put the phage cocktail inside a spatial coevolution model, not just a flask

Extending this paperThe headline therapeutic result — a cocktail of generated phages rapidly overcomes ΦX174-resistant E. coli — is measured in a well-mixed laboratory system. Real deployment happens in structured environments: wards, gut compartments, sewer networks, patient-contact graphs. The natural extension is to embed the paper's cocktail into an agent-based / networked SIR-type coevolutionary model, where bacteria mutate toward escape, phages are re-generated on demand, and transmission runs over an empirical contact or mobility network. The question the paper cannot yet answer is the one that matters clinically: how fast must the design loop turn, relative to bacterial escape and between-patient spread, for a generative cocktail to stay ahead? That converts a static win into a rate-competition result.

The novelty for my line is treating a therapeutic pipeline as a control policy on a spatial network: where to deploy which cocktail, at what refresh interval, given ward-level connectivity. ③ Learn a viability surrogate for the 16 survivors — and demand it be explainable

Extending this paperSixteen viable phages emerged with diverse fitness profiles, but the paper reports the survivors, not a model of survival. The obvious extension is a fitness/viability surrogate: regress measured phenotype on genome-level features (LM likelihood, gene-order perturbation, overlapping-frame integrity, distance to the nearest natural homolog) and then run active learning so that the next synthesis round is spent on informative genomes rather than on likely-dead ones. Critically, the surrogate should be attributable — the scientific payoff is not a better hit rate but knowing which genomic constraints are load-bearing, which is precisely how the distant packaging protein became interesting. Scaling analyses of LM likelihood-versus-fitness show this relationship is not automatic and must be measured.

Added value to my researchStructurally this is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction problem in a different substrate: few positives, expensive labels, correlated features, and a demand for attribution rather than raw accuracy. The transferable asset is the protocol — surrogate model plus SHAP-style attribution plus active sampling — which maps directly onto my fieldwork and sensor-siting decisions (where to spend the next expensive observation). It also sharpens my geospatial-foundation-model fine-tuning work: this paper is a clean demonstration that a general-domain foundation model plus lightweight adaptation plus a hard external validity test is a viable research template, and I should be running the same three-part structure on geospatial FMs.

④ Close the loop: from one-shot generation to an autonomous design–build–test–learn cycle

Extending this paperThe paper demonstrates one impressive pass through generation → synthesis → assay. The version that changes the field is the closed loop: experimental fitness readouts fed back as preference signal (the paper already cites direct preference optimization for aligning protein generative models with experimental fitness), with the model re-steered every round. Extending this paper means specifying the loop's economics — batch size, assay throughput, cost per genome, and the number of rounds needed before the generator's yield saturates — and reporting a learning curve over rounds rather than a single endpoint. Scalable, ML-guided variant engineering campaigns in adjacent domains show what that curve looks like when the loop is properly instrumented.

⑤ Ask who actually receives an AI-generated phage — and where it ends up

Extending this paperThe paper ends at "a path toward AI-generated phage therapies," which leaves two spatial questions untouched. First, access: personalized phage therapy needs isolate sequencing, a design run, GMP synthesis, and a clinician willing to prescribe it — a capability chain concentrated in a handful of academic centers, so the therapy's real catchment is far narrower than its biological host range. Second, environmental fate: engineered phages released through patients enter wastewater and urban water bodies, where phage-mediated gene transfer among resistomes is already documented. Extending this paper means pairing the design engine with an accessibility model of who can plausibly be treated and a release-and-persistence model of where the construct goes — both of which the biosecurity references gesture at but do not operationalize.

Added value to my researchThis is my home ground stated in a new therapeutic domain. The access question is a textbook 2SFCA/R2SFCA problem with an unusually lumpy supply side — a few dozen national capability nodes, catchments defined by referral and shipping logistics rather than driving time, and a strong telehealth component for the consultation step, which folds directly into my digital-divide work. The environmental question extends my urban-health mapping to a novel exposure surface. Together they let me argue a broader thesis I care about: each new AI-enabled biomedical capability creates a new geography of advantage, and mapping that geography at the moment of invention — rather than a decade later — is the useful role for health geography.
