Towards general auditory intelligence for machine listening and speaking

Siyin Wang · Zengrui Jin · Changli Tang · Qiujia Li · Bo Li · Chen Chen · Yuchen Hu · Wenyi Yu · Yixuan Li · Jimin Zhuang · Yudong Yang · Mingqiu Wang · Michael Han · Yifan Ding · Junwen Bai · Tom Ouyang · Shuo-yiin Chang · Xianzhao Chen · Xiaohai Tian · Jun Zhang · Lu Lu · Guangzhi Sun · Zhehuai Chen · Ji Wu · Bowen Zhou · Yuxuan Wang · Tara Sainath · Yonghui Wu · Chao Zhang Published 2026-08-14 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01281-1 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Computer audition has stopped being a stack of separate task pipelines (ASR, TTS, sound tagging, music retrieval) and is being absorbed into large language models. This Review organizes that shift into four pillars — audio comprehension, audio generation, speech-based interaction and audio–visual understanding — and argues that the decisive design choice is the audio representation itself: continuous encoder features versus discrete neural-codec tokens (Fig. 2), which then determines whether a system can be trained, streamed and made full-duplex end to end. The authors conclude that "general auditory intelligence" is not achieved by better recognition accuracy but by latency, turn-taking and paralinguistic competence — hearing how something is said, and speaking back while still listening — and that current evaluation, dominated by static English-centric benchmarks, does not yet measure any of that well.

★

Why It Matters

  • Audio is the interface that needs no literacy. Speech is the lowest-barrier human–machine channel — for older adults, low-literacy populations, people with visual impairment, and anyone whose hands and eyes are busy. Making it work end to end changes who can reach an AI system at all, which is an access question before it is an engineering one.
  • It names the representation bottleneck explicitly. The continuous-feature vs discrete-token dichotomy (Fig. 2) is the single fork that decides latency, streamability, and whether generation and understanding can share one autoregressive backbone. Most downstream disagreements in the field trace back to this fork.
  • Full-duplex is a genuinely different problem, not faster turn-taking. Figs. 6–7 separate streaming speech processing from full-duplex dialogue: a model that listens while speaking must decide when to yield, interrupt and back-channel — behaviours no word-error-rate metric captures.
  • Paralinguistics carries the content that text loses. Emotion, prosody, hesitation, speaker identity and ambient context are the cues that make audio a richer channel than its transcript — and precisely what a cascade of ASR → LLM → TTS destroys.
  • It maps a field that is moving faster than peer review. Of the 278 references, 241 (≈87%) are from 2023 or later and 64 are arXiv preprints — the Review functions as the field's first consolidated, citable map.

Core Methods

  • A four-pillar taxonomy as the organizing method. The Review's analytical instrument is its decomposition into audio comprehension → audio generation → speech-based interaction → audio–visual understanding, with one figure family per pillar (Figs. 3, 4, 5–7, 8). This turns a scattered literature into a comparable architecture space.
  • Representation-first analysis (Fig. 2). Two paradigms are contrasted: continuous representations from self-supervised or supervised encoders (HuBERT, wav2vec 2.0, BEATs, Whisper, USM) fed as soft prompts into an LLM, versus discrete acoustic/semantic tokens from neural codecs (SoundStream, EnCodec, RVQGAN, NanoCodec; FSQ as a quantization alternative) that let audio enter the LLM's own vocabulary.
  • Contrastive audio–language alignment as the comprehension substrate. The CLIP → AudioCLIP → CLAP line is used to explain how audio and text land in a shared semantic space, which is what makes open-vocabulary audio reasoning possible at all.
  • Generation modelling families. Autoregressive codec language modelling, non-autoregressive and diffusion/flow-matching synthesis (E2 TTS, DiTAR, flow matching), and preference alignment for speech (RLHF-style human feedback, SpeechAlign) are compared as alternative routes to expressive, controllable audio.
  • Interaction architectures for full duplex (Figs. 5–7). The Review separates cascaded from end-to-end designs, and streaming from truly duplex ones — parallel text/speech generation, interleaved token generation, listening-while-speaking, and dual-pathway "thinking while speaking" schemes — treating latency and turn-taking as architectural properties, not post-hoc tuning.
  • Audio–visual fusion (Fig. 8). Analysis of how synchronized visual context is injected alongside audio for cross-modal reasoning, temporal grounding and captioning, including audio-visual speech recognition and reasoning-enhanced AV models.
  • Evaluation as an object of study. Rather than reporting a leaderboard, the Review inventories benchmark families and metric types (intrusive/non-intrusive quality, MOS prediction, distributional distance, LLM-as-judge) and diagnoses where they fail to measure interaction quality and trustworthiness.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this Review: first how the Review itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, health accessibility & 2SFCA, explainable and physics-informed spatial learning, multi-source data fusion, open/FAIR science).

① Give the comprehension pillar a coordinate: geo-referenced soundscape understanding

Extending this paperThe Review's audio-comprehension pillar (Fig. 3) treats environmental sound as placeless clips — AudioCaps and Clotho items carry no location, no time-of-day, no built-environment context, and MMAU/AIR-Bench inherit that blindness. A natural extension of the Review's own taxonomy is a fifth axis, situated audition: audio paired with coordinates, timestamp and land-use context, where the model must answer not "what is this sound" but "is this sound normal here, at this hour, in this kind of street." That reframes the Review's proposed "situational awareness" (currently argued only through audio–visual fusion in Fig. 8) as a geographic property, and would demand a benchmark the field does not have: spatially stratified, seasonally balanced, and reported per neighbourhood type rather than per class label.

Added value to my researchThis is a new exposure layer for urban health. An audio LLM that can label continuous ambient recordings into semantically rich categories (traffic, construction, human activity, birdsong, silence) converts cheap microphones into a high-temporal-resolution environmental sensing network — the acoustic counterpart of remote-sensing greenness. I would fuse it with POI, street-network and mobile-phone layers, and run it through MGWR and explainable ML to ask where the acoustic environment mediates the greenness–activity–mental-health pathway, and whether spatiotemporal KDE of "restorative sound" predicts wellbeing better than land-cover proxies alone.

② Turn full-duplex latency (Figs. 6–7) into an accessibility metric, not just an engineering one

Extending this paperThe Review is sharp that latency and turn-taking are architectural, and it inventories Full-Duplex-Bench, Talking Turns and VoiceBench. But every one of those measures the model, never the speaker–context pair. The extension the Review's own future-directions section invites: an interaction-accessibility evaluation in which duplex behaviour is scored across accent and dialect, age (child and older-adult speech), disordered or post-stroke speech, code-switching, background noise typical of a clinic waiting room or a street market, and degraded low-bandwidth channels. A model whose barge-in works at 200 ms for a standard American accent and fails at 900 ms for an accented older speaker is not "fast" — it is selectively fast, and no current benchmark in the Review's 29 would reveal that.

Added value to my researchThis connects audio LLMs directly to my telehealth and digital-divide line. Voice removes the literacy and typing barriers that gate text chatbots, so a working duplex assistant could genuinely flatten access gradients — but only if its failure surface is not itself spatially patterned. I would build a voice-adjusted accessibility measure: an R2SFCA in which the "supply" of a virtual care agent is discounted by a locality's expected interaction-success rate (dialect prevalence, ambient noise, broadband quality), producing maps of where voice AI actually extends primary-care and mental-health reach and where it silently does not.

Cross-ref (corpus): A community-codesigned LLM-powered chatbot for primary care: a randomized controlled trial — 10.1038/s44360-025-00021-w

③ Make Fig. 2's dichotomy quantitative — a rate–distortion–utility law for modality tokenizers

Extending this paperFig. 2 poses continuous features versus discrete codec tokens as a qualitative fork, and the Review lists codecs (SoundStream, EnCodec, RVQGAN, NanoCodec, FSQ) without a common yardstick. The strongest extension is to convert the dichotomy into a measured surface: for a fixed backbone, sweep bitrate and codebook design and trace bits per second → reconstruction distortion → downstream task utility → time-to-first-token, separately for semantic tasks (what was said), paralinguistic tasks (how it was said) and generation quality. That would tell the field where discretization starts destroying prosody and emotion — the very cues the Review argues are audio's reason for existing — and would replace an architectural preference with a design law.

Added value to my researchThe identical unsolved question sits at the centre of geospatial foundation models: should continuous space be fed as continuous patch embeddings, or discretized into tokens (H3 cells, quadkeys, vector-quantized street-view or trajectory codes)? Audio has already run this experiment at scale, and its answer — that discretization is cheap for semantics and expensive for the fine-grained continuous cues — predicts that hard spatial tokenization will preserve land-use semantics while destroying gradient information such as density falloff and distance decay. I would port the rate–distortion–utility protocol directly to trajectory and street-view tokenizers, and use it to choose the token granularity for fine-tuning geospatial foundation models.

Cross-ref (corpus): Multimodal learning with next-token prediction for large multimodal models — 10.1038/s41586-025-10041-x

④ Replace the Review's static benchmark inventory with adversarial, stratified, living evaluation

Extending this paperThe Review's most useful diagnosis is that evaluation is the field's weak joint: ~29 cited benchmarks, largely static, English-centric, and single-turn, alongside three separate audio jailbreak/trustworthiness suites that exist precisely because static tests miss adversarial behaviour. The extension: apply dynamic red-teaming to audio — adversaries that perturb the acoustic channel (accent shift, reverberation, overlapping speakers, whispered or shouted prompts, injected background speech carrying instructions) and are regenerated as models improve, so the benchmark cannot saturate. Audio makes the threat model worse than text, because a prompt injection can be hidden in ambient sound that a human listener never consciously parses — a risk the Review names but no cited benchmark continuously stress-tests.

Added value to my researchThis is my open-science and reproducibility agenda in a different modality. GeoAI has the same disease: models validated on one static held-out region, reported as a single global accuracy that hides spatial non-stationarity. I would adopt the living-benchmark protocol for spatial prediction — adversarial region holdouts, deliberate distribution shifts across density and income gradients, and stratified error reporting by place as a publication requirement — so that "the model achieves R² = 0.87" is never again allowed to conceal systematic failure in low-density or under-resourced areas.

Cross-ref (corpus): Addressing benchmarking gaps in large language models for health and medicine with dynamic red-teaming — 10.1038/s44360-026-00152-8

⑤ Ground audio–visual fusion (Fig. 8) in physics: sound propagation as a structural prior

Extending this paperFig. 8's audio–visual models fuse modalities through learned attention alone — nothing in the architecture knows that sound obeys inverse-square attenuation, reflects off façades, diffracts around corners and arrives at two ears with a measurable delay. Extending the Review's cross-modal-reasoning section: impose an explicit acoustic-propagation prior, so the model reasons over a scene graph in which nodes are surfaces and sources and edges carry physically plausible attenuation and delay. This converts "where is that sound coming from" from an attention artefact into a constrained inference, and would give the audio–visual pillar the kind of falsifiable structure the Review's other pillars already have — plus far better sample efficiency, since geometry no longer has to be learned from scratch.

Added value to my researchThis is the same object as my physics-informed graph learning work, with a new physics attached. Urban noise propagation over a building-footprint graph is a diffusion process with known governing equations; a GNN that carries those as soft constraints could interpolate a citywide noise-exposure surface from sparse microphone deployments far more credibly than kriging, and would extrapolate to unmeasured street canyons. Paired with the audio LLM of idea ①, it yields a full pipeline — semantic labelling of what the sound is, physics-informed propagation of where it goes, and explainable attribution of which morphological features drive exposure inequality.
