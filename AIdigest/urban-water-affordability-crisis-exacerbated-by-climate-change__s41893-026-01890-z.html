Urban water affordability crisis exacerbated by climate change

Jennifer Skerker · Christian Klassert · Baptiste Francois · Aniket Verma · Casey Brown · Sarah Fletcher Published 2026-07-08 DOI 10.1038/s41893-026-01890-z Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Climate adaptation that keeps water reliable can make it unaffordable. Coupling a hydrology–supply system model, a risk-of-failure infrastructure trigger, an infrastructure financing / rate-design model, and a household-level econometric demand model, the authors show that in Santa Cruz, California climate change alone could nearly double median water bills by mid-century (50th-percentile monthly bill US$64 → US$120 under a dry, hot climate), pushing an additional 7–16% of households above the EPA 2.5%-of-income affordability threshold (19% today → 26% moderate → 35% dry). Low-income median burdens rise from 3.9% to 7.3%, and 90th-percentile low-income burdens from 16% to 30% of income. Under prevailing US rate-funded financing, reliability and affordability are in direct tension, and the magnitude is local — demanding city-scale assessment rather than national trend extrapolation.

Why It Matters

  • Closes a structural gap between two literatures. Climate-adaptation planning has been evaluated on utility-scale cost and reliability; affordability research has been cross-sectional and backward-looking. This is, to the authors' knowledge, the first framework to quantify climate → infrastructure → financing → rates → household demand → affordability as one closed loop with feedbacks.
  • Reframes adaptation as a distributional problem. Utility cost increases do not map linearly onto household hardship — the mapping runs through rate structure, cost-of-service allocation, price elasticity, and the income distribution. The paper shows that identical infrastructure spending produces radically different equity outcomes depending on those institutional choices.
  • Sets a policy threshold, not just a number. If only a few percent of households are burdened, utility-funded assistance suffices; at 26–35% of households, local assistance becomes financially infeasible and state/federal financing or regulatory reform is required. That is an actionable decision rule for policymakers.
  • Names the mechanism behind "affordability hotspots." Supply vulnerability + exhausted demand-side options (demand hardening) + costly supply alternatives (desalination, potable reuse, transfers) + rate-design constraints (California's Proposition 218) + income inequality. Table 1 turns that into transferable screening criteria.
  • Quantifies the reliability–affordability Pareto frontier explicitly. Risk-averse Strategy B buys 98.8% average annual reliability at ≥US$9.4M yr⁻¹ and a 5.2% 80th-percentile burden; risk-tolerant Strategy C costs US$1.4M yr⁻¹ with a 2.7% burden but collapses to 61% average minimum annual reliability — an operationally unacceptable failure mode.
  • Honest about who is invisible. Multifamily housing is not individually metered and therefore absent from billing data, biasing the modelled income distribution upward — meaning the reported unaffordability shares are likely underestimates.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

Study system
Santa Cruz, California (single city). Santa Cruz Water Department (SCWD) serves ~96,000 residents; roughly 95% of supply is locally sourced surface water, anchored by the Loch Lomond Reservoir (designed to store about one year of supply), with limited over-year storage — the structural reason the city is climate-exposed.
Household billing
SCWD monthly water billing records for every account active January 2009 – December 2021, n = 2,836,297 bills (filtered for length and quality). Proprietary under a data-use agreement and not publicly shareable; use approved by Stanford IRB, eProtocol no. 63914. Demands are simulated for 21,370 single-family residential accounts, resampled so total single-family use matches 2020 trends.
Property & income
County of Santa Cruz Assessor's Office Database (2021 property and tax records; home tax value used as an income proxy in the demand model), American Community Survey 2019 five-year estimates (block-group income distributions), and ACS PUMS 2015–2019. Household income assigned via multivariate regression plus quantile mapping onto the 16 ACS income bins at block-group scale. Low income defined as < US$39,900 yr⁻¹ (California Poverty Measure, 2023). City median household income ≈ US$91,900 (2020 dollars); ~20% below the federal poverty level, ~20% above US$200,000.
Utility finance & rates
SCWD Long-Range Financial Plans (2016 and 2021, incl. water rate schedule FY2023–FY2027) for operating costs, financing and historical rate structure; 2020 Urban Water Management Plan for historical water use, non-residential/multifamily demands and water losses (constant 201 MG yr⁻¹); infrastructure capital/operating costs from SCWD design studies (Kennedy Jenks, 2024); rate design follows the AWWA M1 manual (7th ed., 2017).
Climate & hydrology
CMIP6 ensemble anomalies drive a stochastic weather generator producing 50-year stochastic simulations; forcing/observational inputs include Maurer et al. (2002) gridded meteorology, USGS gauge San Lorenzo River at Big Trees, Cal-Adapt, NLDAS and GLDAS. A lumped hydrological model of the San Lorenzo River plus regression relationships generates daily flows at all system locations.
Simulation volume
Infrastructure planning strategies optimized over 20 climate simulations; strategy comparison across 500 simulations of plausible climate impacts (Fig. 5b,c). Household-scale output counts are enormous — e.g. Fig. 3 pools n ≈ 1.04 × 10⁹ (low-income) / 3.42 × 10⁹ (all other) monthly household demand/bill/affordability values across the "all climate simulations" scenario. The paper does not report a single headline count of total stochastic simulations run.
Infrastructure options
Five options, 0.5–4 MGD capacity, deployable individually or in combination: two water transfers, aquifer storage and recovery, direct potable reuse, and a 4-MGD desalination plant — all currently under SCWD long-term consideration.
Time window
Billing 2009–2021; property/tax 2021; ACS 2015–2019; baseline calibration to 2020 water use; projections focus on mid-century (illustrated 2020–2050). Climate simulations are treated as stationary, which the authors note probably understates impacts under progressively intensifying change.
Availability
Non-proprietary stochastic flow and weather data on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.18752509); all non-proprietary code on Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.18752481). SCWD billing data are not shareable.

Core Methods

  • Coupled city-scale simulation framework (Fig. 1a). Five linked stages: (1) climate scenario generation → (2) water supply system simulation → (3) rule-based utility adaptation decisions → (4) infrastructure financing and rate design → (5) household demand and affordability accounting, with an explicit feedback loop (new infrastructure → higher rates → lower demand → different volumetric revenue → revised rates).
  • Stochastic climate scenario generation. A stochastic weather generator reproduces historical variability; CMIP6 anomalies then shift mean precipitation (×0.6–×1.2 of historical average), interannual precipitation variability (×1.0–×1.2 of historical CV) and temperature (+0.0 to +5.0 °C). Scenarios are stationary over each 50-year simulation, enabling controlled factor attribution.
  • Water supply system model. The Santa Cruz Water System Model built on Pywr (Python water-resources simulation library) solves daily operations by linear programming — river flows at the reservoir and diversions, allocations to treatment plants, and current plus candidate future infrastructure.
  • Risk-of-failure (ROF) adaptation triggers. Utilities act when the estimated probability that storage falls below a critical (e.g. deadpool) level within a 2-year horizon exceeds a threshold. ROF look-up tables are built by partitioning stochastic simulations into 2-year segments over three dimensions — current reservoir storage, annual water demand, and planned/implemented infrastructure — and construction lead time is explicitly represented.
  • Multi-objective infrastructure planning. Planning strategies (ROF threshold + deployment order over five options, 0.5–4 MGD) are optimized with the Borg multi-objective evolutionary algorithm against total capital + operating cost and average annual unmet demand, averaged over 20 climate simulations; Pareto-approximate strategies A/B/C are then stress-tested on 500 simulations.
  • Infrastructure financing & rate-design model. Three steps: revenue requirement via a cashflow model splitting pay-as-you-go capital, debt-financed capital and annual O&M; cost-of-service allocation per AWWA M1 (single-family assigned 38% of costs recovered volumetrically, 78% via fixed fees); then redesign of Santa Cruz's increasing block tariff (0–5, 6–9, 10+ ccf) with an infrastructure reinvestment fee plus a volumetric consumption charge, holding historical inter-tier cost ratios.
  • Household water demand — discrete/continuous choice model. Econometric specification for increasing block tariffs: w = exp(Zδ + α·ln p + γ·ln y) with a per-household bias-correction term, where p is marginal price, y is home tax value (income proxy), and Z holds housing characteristics and monthly weather. Performance: r² = 0.36 at household scale, r² = 0.78 at income-group scale.
  • Affordability metric. Affordability ratio = simulated monthly bill ÷ household income, evaluated during the highest-rate periods, benchmarked against the EPA 2.5% threshold, and reported as full distributions (median, 80th, 90th percentiles) rather than a single mean.
  • Sensitivity analysis. Fractional factorial designs over high/low parameter values, run separately for (i) financing and rate-design parameters and (ii) demand and demographic parameters (price elasticity, single-family demand levels, population size and composition), plus one uncertain parameter per framework component in the optimization.

① Take the framework from one city to a screened national portfolio — a transferable affordability-risk operator

Extending this paperThe authors argue the three-component framework is general but must be "parameterized with local data" — and then run it exactly once, for Santa Cruz. Table 1 already names the transferable predictors (surface-water dependence, over-year storage capacity, remaining conservation headroom, cost of the cheapest supply expansion, income inequality, rate-design flexibility). The natural extension is a two-tier design: a cheap national screening layer that scores every US community water system on those Table-1 dimensions from public data (SDWIS service-area boundaries, ACS income distributions, USGS/gauge and reservoir-storage attributes, state rate-survey data), followed by full integrated simulation for the top-ranked hotspots. Crucially, that screening layer should be calibrated against the Santa Cruz simulation rather than asserted — train a surrogate on the paper's own hundreds of stochastic simulations so the screening score is an emulator of the coupled model, not a heuristic index. This converts a case study into the first climate-water-affordability hotspot map, and it directly tests the paper's central hypothesis that hotspots exist as a definable class.

② Replace the rule-based ROF trigger with a learned, uncertainty-aware adaptive planning policy

Extending this paperThe paper's utility behaviour is a static rule: an annual ROF threshold read off a look-up table indexed by storage, demand and prior investments, with strategy order fixed by a Borg optimization over just 20 climate simulations. The authors flag both limits — the rules omit political, permitting and financing frictions, and the climate is treated as stationary so multi-investment "tipping point" cascades are excluded. The extension is to recast infrastructure deployment as a sequential decision problem under deep uncertainty and learn a policy over the joint state (storage trajectory, demand, rate level, outstanding debt service, and the current affordability distribution). Two payoffs follow. First, affordability enters the objective rather than being a downstream diagnostic — the policy can learn to stage a 0.5-MGD transfer plus reuse instead of a lumpy 4-MGD desalination plant precisely to smooth the rate shock, an option class the fixed deployment orders cannot express. Second, non-stationary CMIP6 trajectories become tractable, letting the model surface the compounding-investment tipping points the authors explicitly say they cannot represent. Adding a stochastic permitting-delay and public-opposition layer would make the timing of rate shocks realistic rather than instantaneous.

Added value to my researchThis is the same mathematical object as my EV charging-station efficiency-versus-equity optimization — staged, irreversible, capital-lumpy siting under demand uncertainty with an equity constraint — but with a feature my formulations lack: the financing channel, where the cost of the facility is recovered from the very population whose access it is meant to improve. Importing that closed loop would make my facility-siting work considerably more honest, and the same logic transfers to health-facility siting, where capital costs are recovered through prices or taxes that fall on the served population. Methodologically it pushes me toward multi-objective reinforcement learning over a spatial state, which pairs naturally with my physics-informed graph-learning agenda.

③ Make the invisible households visible: multifamily, renters, and the sub-block-group affordability surface

Extending this paperThe paper's most consequential limitation is stated plainly — multifamily homes are not individually metered, so they are absent from the billing data, which biases the modelled income distribution upward and means the 26–35% unaffordability shares are probably too low. Meanwhile household income is imputed by regression plus quantile mapping onto 16 ACS bins at block-group scale, so the entire distributional result rests on a spatial unit far coarser than the household. Two complementary fixes, both squarely GeoAI: (a) build a synthetic-population / spatial-microsimulation layer that reconstructs the full renter and multifamily household stock via iterative proportional fitting against ACS PUMS and assessor parcel records, then allocates master-metered building consumption to units using building-level attributes — recovering exactly the population the billing data drops; (b) replace bin-level quantile mapping with a dasymetric, parcel-level income surface so affordability ratios vary within block groups rather than being constant across them. Together these would let the authors report affordability by tenure — the pass-through of water costs into rent is an entirely different, and largely opaque, transmission mechanism.

④ Close the loop to health: from affordability ratio to a modelled health-burden outcome

Extending this paperThe paper motivates its work with health — unaffordable water limits drinking and hygiene, forces trade-offs with healthcare, and carries negative health impacts — but stops at the affordability ratio, a purely financial statistic benchmarked against an EPA rule of thumb whose empirical link to harm is itself contested. The extension is to append a health-response layer: couple the simulated household bill trajectories to (i) shutoff/arrears risk models, (ii) hygiene-related and heat-related morbidity, and (iii) the documented substitution away from healthcare spending, producing an outcome in DALYs or avoidable-utilization units rather than percent-of-income. This matters practically: a burden that is 2.6 percentage points higher at the distribution's upper bound (5.5 → 8.1) has very different meaning for a household with chronic medical costs than for one without, and Fig. 4's finding that impacts concentrate in the upper tail is precisely where health consequences become nonlinear. Coupling water burden with the co-occurring heat burden — since the same dry, hot climate simultaneously raises cooling costs and water prices — would expose a compound utility-burden shock that neither literature currently models jointly.

⑤ Endogenize behaviour and demography: from price-elastic households to adaptive, mobile, network-embedded ones

Extending this paperHousehold behaviour in the model is a single elasticity coefficient — demand responds to marginal price through α·ln p, and that is the whole behavioural repertoire. The authors list what this omits: long-term demographic change, migration, and endogenous behavioural adaptation, plus the observation that multifamily demand is likely more inelastic. Yet the paper's own results hinge on behaviour: demand hardening is why Santa Cruz has no cheap adaptation left, and it is the reason supply-side factors dominate. An agent-based extension would let hardening emerge rather than be assumed — households adopt efficiency irreversibly (ratcheting elasticity downward), respond to conservation campaigns and to neighbours through social contagion on a spatial network, and, at the extreme, relocate when cumulative housing-plus-utility burden becomes unsustainable. That last channel is the sharpest: displacement would mechanically improve the measured affordability distribution while representing a worse social outcome, meaning the paper's headline metric can move the right way for the wrong reason. Explicitly modelling it would let the authors report affordability net of selection.

Added value to my researchThis is my agent-based modelling plus network community detection stack applied to a utility-rate system, and it addresses a bias I care about generally: metrics computed on the population that remains systematically understate harm when the harmed population leaves — the same selection artefact that flatters healthcare-access statistics in gentrifying neighbourhoods. Modelling contagion in conservation behaviour also connects to my social-media and mobile-phone data work, where peer effects in spatially structured populations are estimable from observed behaviour rather than assumed. And a demand model that ratchets rather than staying elastic is a genuinely better econometric object for any resource-pricing study, including the road-pricing and EV-charging problems I work on.
