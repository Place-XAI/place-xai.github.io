Large language models can predict the results of social science experiments

Ashwini Ashokkumar · Luke Hewitt · Isaias Ghezae · Robb Willer Published 2026-07-08 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10742-x Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Prompted to simulate demographically diverse American respondents, GPT-4 predicted the treatment effects of 70 preregistered, nationally representative US survey experiments (469 effects, 119,330 participants) with correlations comparable to pooled human forecasts — and the correlation held for studies published after the model's training cutoff, and for prominent open-weight models. But the predictions are systematically inflated: effect sizes are overestimated, so the signal is in the ranking, not the magnitude. On a harder secondary archive of 15 megastudies (606 effects), accuracy fell but remained on par with pooled expert forecasters. A survey of 460 social scientists plus applied tests frame the realistic uses (pilot testing, intervention selection, replication triage) and the risks (bias, misuse).

Why It Matters

  • Moves LLM social simulation from "can it mimic a survey answer?" to "can it predict a causal estimate?" Prior work asked whether models reproduce marginal survey distributions; this asks whether the difference between conditions — the quantity experiments exist to estimate — is recoverable. That is a far stricter and far more useful target.
  • It confronts the contamination objection head-on. Because a large share of the archive was unpublished and not publicly posted at GPT-4's training cutoff, the correlation cannot be explained away as memorization. This is the design choice that makes the claim credible rather than suggestive.
  • It gives a calibrated verdict, not hype. High correlation with systematic overestimation of effect sizes is exactly the kind of nuance that determines correct use: rank-order screening yes, effect-size substitution no. Removing human participants is not licensed by these results.
  • It changes the economics of experimental design. Pilot testing, intervention selection from a large candidate pool, and triaging which published effects most need replication are all searches over a wide design space, where a cheap noisy prior with the right rank ordering has real value.
  • It couples the technical result to community norms. Surveying 460 social scientists about probable uses and perceived risks makes this a methods-governance paper as much as a benchmark paper — and the risk analysis (bias, misuse, degraded diversity of represented voices) is done in the same manuscript rather than deferred.

Core Methods

  • Uniform re-estimation of ground truth. Rather than copying published effect sizes, the authors re-analysed raw data from all 70 studies and estimated treatment effects in a consistent manner — removing analytic heterogeneity from the benchmark itself (Extended Data Fig. 1).
  • Persona-conditioned response simulation. The LLM is given the original study materials plus demographically diverse participant profiles, and asked to produce the response a person with that profile would give — condition by condition.
  • Treatment effects by simulated contrast. The predicted effect is inferred, not asked for: average the simulated responses within each condition, then take the between-condition difference. This mirrors the estimand of the real experiment instead of eliciting a forecast in words.
  • Prompt ensembling as variance reduction. Predictions are averaged across a pool of 120 prompts; Extended Data Fig. 2 shows accuracy increasing with the number of prompts averaged (32 runs over random prompt subsets, 95% CIs) — the prompt set is treated as a sample, not a single lucky wording.
  • Contamination control by training-cutoff split. Accuracy is recomputed on the subset of studies neither published nor publicly posted before GPT-4's training-data cutoff, testing prediction rather than recall.
  • Model-family robustness. The analysis is repeated with prominent open-weight models, so the result is a property of capable LLMs rather than of one proprietary system.
  • Human-forecast benchmarking. LLM accuracy is placed on the same scale as pooled layperson forecasts (survey archive) and pooled expert forecasts (megastudies) — the honest comparator, since experiments are already often triaged by human intuition.
  • Bias/subgroup audit. Extended Data Fig. 3 breaks accuracy down by US demographic subgroup with 95% CIs, testing whether the simulation serves all represented groups equally.
  • Application stress tests. The archives are re-used to evaluate three concrete workflows — pilot testing, intervention selection, and identifying effects that most need replication — alongside risk analyses (bias, misuse), with community norms sampled from 460 social scientists.
  • Calibration diagnosis. Alongside correlation, the paper reports a systematic bias: predicted effect sizes are inflated relative to observed ones — separating rank-order skill from magnitude skill.

① Give the simulated respondents a place: geo-conditioned personas and spatial heterogeneity of treatment effects

Extending this paperThe archive is nationally representative of the United States as a whole, and the personas fed to the model are demographic — age, gender, race, education, partisanship — with no geographic context. Yet the effects being predicted (vaccine messaging, climate advocacy, political persuasion) are exactly the ones known to vary by place: urbanicity, broadband availability, local media ecology, deprivation. A direct extension is to re-run the pipeline with geo-conditioned personas — profiles carrying county- or tract-level covariates — and test the paper's own estimand at a finer grain: does the LLM reproduce the observed spatial heterogeneity of treatment effects, or does it collapse every region toward a national mean? The archive already supports this test wherever respondent geography was recorded, and the answer decides whether LLM pre-screening is usable for geographically targeted campaigns at all.

Added value to my researchThis is the bridge from LLM social simulation into health geography. If geo-conditioned personas hold up, I can pre-screen telehealth-uptake and mental-health-service messages across digital-divide strata before fielding anything — generating synthetic response propensities keyed to census tracts and feeding them straight into 2SFCA demand weights instead of assuming uniform willingness-to-use across a catchment. It also gives my spatial-accessibility work a behavioural layer it has never had: access is currently supply geometry, and this makes the demand side estimable ex ante.

② Fix the inflation: a hierarchical recalibration layer that turns rank skill into usable effect sizes

Extending this paperThe paper's most actionable limitation is stated plainly in its own abstract — predictions are strongly correlated with truth yet systematically overestimate effect sizes. That is a well-behaved failure, and well-behaved failures are correctable. The extension is a hierarchical shrinkage / calibration model fitted on the 469-effect archive (the paper already cites metafor), mapping predicted effect → posterior distribution over true effect, with variance components by domain, outcome type and study design, and validated out-of-sample on the 606 megastudy effects. The deliverable would be a calibrated interval rather than a point prediction — precisely what a power analysis needs, and what treatmenteffect.app could serve directly.

Added value to my researchThe same machinery is what my spatial ML predictions need. Ensemble models for accessibility and health-service demand also produce well-ordered but miscalibrated magnitudes, and policy consumers of those numbers need intervals, not point estimates. Porting a hierarchical recalibration layer onto my prediction pipelines — with the spatial unit as the grouping factor, so calibration can differ between dense urban and sparse rural regimes — makes explainable ML outputs decision-grade rather than merely rank-correct.

③ Couple the LLM prior to a spatial simulator: individual response propensity × mobility network

Extending this paperAccuracy drops on the megastudy archive, and the reason is structural: megastudies are interventions delivered into a real population with real logistics, where an individual-level response propensity is only the first link in the causal chain. The extension is a two-stage hybrid — let the LLM supply the individual-level propensity it demonstrably predicts well, then propagate it through an agent-based / network model that carries the parts the LLM cannot see (who is reachable, who interacts with whom, saturation and spillover). Predicting a megastudy arm then becomes a composition of a behavioural prior and a spatial diffusion process, and one can measure how much of the megastudy accuracy gap is closed by adding structure rather than by improving the model.

Added value to my researchThis is directly implementable in my agent-based and SIR work: the perennial weak point is the behavioural response parameter, hand-tuned or borrowed from a literature that never studied the population at hand. An LLM prior conditioned on the actual message and the actual demographic profile replaces a guessed compliance coefficient with something benchmarked against 469 real effects. For campaign design — vaccination outreach, telehealth enrolment — I would get a full pipeline from message wording to spatially resolved uptake.

④ Audit whose effects are predicted well: subgroup fidelity as an equity constraint, not a robustness check

Extending this paperExtended Data Fig. 3 already reports accuracy by US demographic subgroup — the paper takes the audit seriously. But subgroup accuracy is reported as a diagnostic, when in the intervention-selection use case it is a fairness constraint: if the model predicts effects well for majority groups and poorly for minority ones, then using it to select which intervention to field systematically optimizes for the well-predicted. The extension is to make this explicit — measure whether the rank ordering of interventions is preserved within each subgroup (not just whether correlation is positive), extend the audit to intersectional and geographic strata, and define an admissibility rule: an LLM-derived shortlist may only be used when subgroup rank fidelity exceeds a stated threshold.

Added value to my researchThis is the guardrail I need before any LLM-derived prior touches a health-access decision. It maps cleanly onto the efficiency-versus-equity framing I use in facility-siting and EV-charging optimization: an LLM screening layer is another allocation mechanism, and it deserves the same equity accounting as a siting algorithm. Concretely, it gives me a pre-registered fairness test to attach to any GeoAI pipeline that uses synthetic respondents to stand in for underserved populations — the exact populations most likely to be thinly represented in training data.

⑤ Treat the 120-prompt ensemble as a designed sample — and open the black box of what drives the predicted effect

Extending this paperExtended Data Fig. 2 shows accuracy rising as more of the 120 prompts are averaged: the prompt ensemble is behaving like a variance-reduction estimator, and its curve has a shape. Two extensions follow. First, fit that curve — derive the marginal accuracy return per additional prompt and an explicit stopping rule, converting an arbitrary "we used 120" into a compute-budget decision, with active selection of maximally informative prompts rather than random subsets. Second, attribute the prediction: apply explainable-ML machinery over the simulated respondent pool to identify which stimulus features and which persona attributes actually move the predicted effect, and check whether those drivers match the moderators found in the real data. A model that gets the right answer for the wrong reason will fail on the next stimulus, and correlation alone cannot detect that.

Added value to my researchBoth halves transfer straight into my methods stack. The ensemble-size scaling law is the same question I face when budgeting compute for fine-tuning geospatial foundation models and for ML ensembles in spatial prediction — how many members before returns vanish. The attribution half is my explainable-ML agenda applied to a new object: instead of asking which built-environment covariates drive a predicted accessibility surface, ask which prompt and persona features drive a predicted behavioural response, using the same SHAP-style decomposition and the same insistence that a spatial model's reasons be inspectable.
