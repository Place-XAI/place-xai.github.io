Learning millisecond protein dynamics from what is missing in NMR spectra

Hannah K. Wayment-Steele · Gina El Nesr · Ramith Hettiarachchi · Adedolapo M. Ojoawo · Hasindu Kariyawasam · Sergey Ovchinnikov · Dorothee Kern Published 2026-08-10 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10989-4 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

The absence of data is itself the measurement. Residues that are missing from chemical-shift assignments in the ~10,000 protein entries of the BMRB are treated as exchange-broadened by µs–ms motion, turning a decades-old nuisance into a large-scale, free training label. Deep models trained only to predict which residues go missing also predict conformational exchange independently measured by NMR relaxation (>100 curated datasets). The best model, Dyna-1, reads an intermediate layer of the multimodal language model ESM-3, and is most accurate exactly where dynamics matter biologically — enzyme catalysis and ligand binding — mirroring the paper's finding that µs–ms-exchanging residues are more evolutionarily conserved.

Why It Matters

  • It solves a data-scarcity problem by inverting it. Relaxation-dispersion experiments are the gold standard for µs–ms dynamics but exist for only ~102 proteins. By reading missingness in shift assignments, the authors harvest a supervision signal spanning ~10,000 BMRB proteins — two orders of magnitude more coverage, at zero new experimental cost.
  • A negative artifact becomes a positive observable. Exchange broadening has always been the reason a peak "isn't there". Formalizing that absence as a label is a conceptual move that generalizes far beyond NMR — any measurement whose failure mode is systematic carries information.
  • Dynamics is predicted where function lives. The model performs best on residues tied to catalysis and ligand binding, so predictions are not generic flexibility scores but candidate functional hotspots — directly usable for enzyme engineering, allosteric-site discovery and drug design.
  • Evolution corroborates the signal. Exchanging residues are more conserved, giving an orthogonal, sequence-only line of evidence that the learned "missingness" really tracks functionally constrained motion rather than experimental sloppiness.
  • Reuse instead of retraining. Dyna-1 taps an intermediate ESM-3 layer rather than training a bespoke dynamics model — evidence that µs–ms motion is already latent inside general protein foundation models and merely needs to be probed.
  • Ships as a resource. The curated relaxation compendium plus the released models give the field a shared benchmark where previously each lab had bespoke, non-comparable measurements.

Core Methods

  • Proxy-label construction (the central move): for every BMRB protein, mark each residue as assigned or unassigned and adopt the "bold assumption" (the authors' own phrase) that unassigned residues are exchange-broadened by µs–ms motion. Dynamics prediction is thereby recast as a per-residue binary classification of missingness over ~10,000 proteins.
  • Reference-standard curation: assemble and standardize >100 NMR relaxation datasets into a comparable compendium of measured conformational exchange — the field's first large, harmonized ground truth for µs–ms motion.
  • Model bake-off: train various deep-learning architectures on the missing-assignment task and compare them, rather than committing to one design a priori.
  • Dyna-1 — probing a foundation model's middle: the winning model consumes an intermediate layer of ESM-3. The choice matters conceptually: dynamics information is richer in mid-stack representations than in the model's task-specific output, so the method is a probe of an existing multimodal language model rather than a from-scratch dynamics predictor.
  • Cross-observable transfer test: evaluate models trained purely on missingness against the independently measured relaxation data. Agreement is the paper's key evidence that the proxy label carries genuine dynamical content and is not merely learning spectral-crowding artifacts.
  • Function stratification: break performance down by biological role — catalytic residues, ligand-binding sites — showing accuracy concentrates on functionally coupled motions.
  • Evolutionary analysis: test whether µs–ms-exchanging residues are more conserved, supplying a mechanistic rationale independent of both NMR observables.
  • Prospective wet-lab validation: express and measure new recombinant proteins (YjbJ, BcChi-A) to check predictions on systems outside the training corpus.

① Model the missingness of the missingness: a causal, positive-unlabeled treatment of the proxy label

Extending this paperThe paper's engine rests on one admitted leap — that an unassigned residue is an exchange-broadened residue. But assignments also go missing for reasons that have nothing to do with µs–ms motion: peak overlap in crowded regions, prolines, fast amide solvent exchange at loops and termini, low sensitivity, paramagnetic effects, truncated constructs, or simply an assignment campaign that stopped when it had enough. That makes the training set a textbook positive-unlabeled / missing-not-at-random problem, not a clean binary one. The direct extension: model the competing causes explicitly — a mixture model in which each unassigned residue carries a posterior probability of "exchange-broadened" versus "technically unassignable", with covariates such as local sequence composition, predicted disorder, solvent accessibility, spectrometer field and the completeness of the deposition itself. Calibrating against the >100 relaxation datasets would then estimate the label-noise rate directly, and Dyna-1's reported accuracy could be decomposed into "genuine dynamics" versus "learned spectroscopy artifact" — turning the paper's bold assumption from a caveat into a quantified parameter.

Added value to my researchThis is exactly the pathology that haunts my health-geography work and I have never framed it this cleanly. Absence in health data is never random: a census tract with no recorded telehealth visits may lack demand, lack broadband, or lack a reporting channel; a mental-health facility absent from a POI layer may not exist or may simply be unmapped in low-income neighbourhoods, where OSM completeness is systematically worse. Adopting this paper's inversion — treat the gap as an observable, then model why the gap occurred — gives me a principled PU-learning layer for 2SFCA/R2SFCA supply inputs: instead of assuming a missing facility is a zero, carry a posterior over "truly absent" versus "unobserved", and propagate that uncertainty into the accessibility score. It converts a known but usually hand-waved bias in digital-divide and service-desert mapping into an estimable quantity.

② From a binary flag to calibrated rates — and an active-learning loop that decides which protein to measure next

Extending this paperDyna-1 answers "does this residue exchange?" but the physics people care about is quantitative: the exchange rate kex, the excited-state population pB, the chemical-shift difference Δω. The paper's own curated relaxation compendium already contains those numbers, so the natural extension is a heteroscedastic regression head with calibrated predictive uncertainty — trained on the >100 relaxation datasets, pretrained on the ~10,000-protein missingness corpus. Uncertainty then becomes the steering signal for a closed loop: because relaxation-dispersion experiments are expensive and the compendium is only ~102 proteins deep, active learning should choose which protein and which field strength maximally reduce model entropy. That turns a static resource into a self-expanding one and gives the community a rational answer to "what should we measure next?" instead of measuring whatever is convenient.

Added value to my researchStructurally identical to my sampling problems. Mobile-phone, GPS-trajectory and survey data are all expensive and unevenly distributed, and I routinely have a dense-but-noisy proxy (social media, POI counts) plus a sparse-but-trustworthy reference (census, clinical records) — precisely this paper's ~10,000 versus >100 asymmetry. Pretrain on the cheap proxy, fine-tune on the trusted reference, and let calibrated uncertainty decide where to field the next survey wave or where to buy the next mobility panel. Applied to spatial accessibility, this yields uncertainty-aware 2SFCA maps in which the confidence interval, not just the point estimate, drives where new primary-care or EV-charging capacity should be sited.

③ Close the loop: use predicted exchange as a constraint that steers conformational ensemble generation

Extending this paperDyna-1 says where a protein moves on the millisecond timescale but not what the excited conformation looks like. Meanwhile, ensemble generators produce vast candidate conformation sets with no experimental signal to prune them. The obvious fusion: use Dyna-1's per-residue exchange predictions as a soft physical constraint inside the sampler — bias generation toward ensembles in which predicted-exchanging residues actually change environment between states, and penalize ensembles where they stay rigid. That converts an unsupervised sampling problem into an observable-constrained one, and makes the paper's output falsifiable structurally rather than only statistically. A validation target is already at hand: the authors' own YjbJ and BcChi-A measurements.

Added value to my researchThis is my physics-informed graph-learning agenda transposed. The lesson is to inject a learned observable as a differentiable constraint rather than post-hoc validation: predicted "where the system is likely to switch regimes" becomes a penalty term in the generator. For urban agglomeration and 15-minute-neighbourhood scenarios, that means simulated futures are constrained to reproduce the places where real systems demonstrably flip, not merely the average state.

④ Ask why the middle layer wins: a systematic depth-and-scale study of where dynamics lives inside a foundation model

Extending this paperThe most quietly radical detail is that Dyna-1 reads an intermediate ESM-3 layer, not the final one — implying µs–ms dynamics is already encoded in a model never trained on dynamics, and that the output head actively discards it. The paper reports this choice; it does not explain it. Extending it: sweep every layer and every model scale, plot exchange-prediction skill as a function of depth and parameter count, and test whether the optimum drifts with scale as it does for other biophysical probes. Pair that with a sparse-probing analysis to ask whether specific directions in the residual stream encode exchange, and whether they coincide with conservation directions — which would give a mechanistic account of the paper's own conservation finding. The practical payoff is a rule for practitioners: which layer of which model do I tap for which biophysical property?

Added value to my researchDirectly actionable for my geospatial foundation-model fine-tuning. I default to last-layer embeddings from remote-sensing and street-view backbones, and this paper is evidence that the last layer is the wrong default — task-specific heads compress away exactly the intermediate structure that transfers. Concretely: run a layer sweep for spatial downstream tasks (land-use, accessibility surfaces, urban-health outcome prediction) and treat layer index as a first-class hyperparameter, not an afterthought. Combined with explainable-ML attribution over layer-wise features, this would let me say which depth of a geospatial backbone carries morphology versus function — a genuinely new axis of interpretability for GeoAI.

⑤ Span the timescale gap: couple millisecond labels with femtosecond simulators into a multi-scale dynamics model

Extending this paperThe paper occupies a peculiar position on the time axis: molecular dynamics resolves femtoseconds to microseconds, this work labels micro- to milliseconds, and nothing bridges them. A multi-scale extension would train a single model on both regimes — trajectory-forecasting surrogates supplying fast-motion supervision, missingness plus relaxation data supplying slow-motion supervision — and ask whether fast local fluctuation statistics predict which residues will exchange slowly, i.e. whether µs–ms exchange is a coarse-grained consequence of picosecond behaviour. Testing that on the same protein families would either reveal a genuine bridging law or establish that millisecond exchange is emergent and irreducible to short-trajectory statistics. Either result is a substantive advance over the paper's single-timescale framing, and it also suggests a class-conditional test: do immune receptors, enzymes and disordered regions each obey a different fast-to-slow mapping?

Added value to my researchMulti-scale coupling is the recurring obstacle in my spatiotemporal work: mobile-phone traces resolve minutes, health outcomes emerge over years, and my models usually pick one scale and hope. The transferable design is the paper's own trick applied across scales — use the fast, abundant, cheap signal as pretraining and the slow, sparse, expensive outcome as the fine-tuning target, then examine whether fast-scale variability is a leading indicator of slow-scale regime change. For urban health this is concrete: do short-horizon mobility fluctuation statistics predict which neighbourhoods will undergo multi-year shifts in healthcare access or job–housing balance? That reframes my spatiotemporal KDE and network-community work as an explicit fast-to-slow transfer problem.
