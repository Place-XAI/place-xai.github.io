Smoking, education level and excess premature deaths in the USA

Xuyang Tang · Eo Rin Cho · Patrick Brown · Kenneth E. Warner · Prabhat Jha Published 2026-07-10 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00153-7 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Smoking — not opioids — is the dominant engine of America's education-based survival divide. Across 1992–2019, the absolute gap in the 50-year risk of dying between ages 30 and 79 between low- and high-education non-Hispanic white adults more than doubled in men (21 → 44 percentage points) and tripled in women (13 → 42 points). Of an estimated 3.2 million excess premature deaths in the low- and middle-education groups, smoking-attributable disease accounted for 63%, while opioid poisoning contributed ~3% overall (rising to 8% of excess deaths at ages 30–64 during 2010–2019), cirrhosis ~1% and other external causes ~5%. The "deaths of despair" narrative, the authors argue, has systematically under-weighted tobacco.

★

Why It Matters

  • Re-attributes a famous public-health narrative. Case & Deaton's "deaths of despair" framing put opioids, suicide and alcohol at the centre of stagnating US white survival. This paper decomposes the same 30-year divergence and finds that roughly two-thirds of the excess is smoking-attributable — a very different policy target.
  • Quantifies inequality in absolute, intuitive units. At 2019 death rates, ~79% of low-education 30-year-old white men and ~67% of women are expected to die before age 80 — a level of premature mortality the authors compare with the 1930s Great Depression and parts of sub-Saharan Africa today.
  • Separates period effects from long-latency exposure. By using observed lung-cancer mortality as an index of cumulative smoking exposure (the Peto indirect method), it captures 20–30 year lags in tobacco harm that prevalence surveys alone cannot predict — the education gradient we see in deaths today encodes smoking behaviour of the 1980s–90s.
  • Gives a concrete, actionable lever. If the excess is mostly tobacco, then cessation support targeted at low-education adults aged 30–79 — not only youth prevention or opioid response — is the highest-yield intervention, and its benefits are realizable within years rather than decades.
  • Directly relevant to health-equity monitoring. Education is one of the few socio-economic variables recorded on US death certificates, making this a reproducible, nationally complete inequality metric that any state or county can replicate.

Population & period
Entire US non-Hispanic white population, 1992–2024; the main decomposition is restricted to 1992–2019 to avoid the COVID-19 mortality spike (1989–1991 excluded because education reporting was incomplete). Ages 30–79, split into 30–64 and 65–79.
Death records
NCHS Mortality Multiple Cause-of-Death files (National Vital Statistics System). 33 million deaths at ages 30–79 with known education, 1992–2024 (19 M men, 14 M women; 13 M at ages 30–64, 20 M at ages 65–79). Population denominators
Current Population Survey — Annual Social and Economic Supplement (CPS ASEC), US Census Bureau: population by age, sex and education level.
Education strata
Three levels from the death certificate and CPS: ≤11 years (low), 12 years (middle), ≥13 years (high); sensitivity analysis using ≥16 years (college completion) as the reference.
Cause coding
ICD-9 through 1998 and ICD-10 from 1999 onward (the discontinuity is marked by double bars in Figs. 2–4). Opioid deaths follow the CDC classification via ICD-10 T-codes / ICD-9 E-codes; unspecified-drug deaths (T50.9) are excluded from the main analysis and tested in sensitivity.
Reference rates (Peto method)
Smoker and never-smoker lung-cancer reference death rates from the appendix of Peto et al., Lancet 1992, derived from the ACS Cancer Prevention Study II — a 1980s prospective study of 1.2 million Americans.
Relative risks
Age- and sex-specific smoker–never-smoker RRs from Table 12.3 of the 2014 US Surgeon General's Report, with an arbitrary 50% reduction applied to the excess risk for all conditions except lung cancer to absorb confounding.
Risk-factor prevalence
US National Health Interview Survey (NHIS): current/never smoking and obesity (BMI ≥ 30) 1990–2024; binge drinking 1997–2018; NHIS sampling weights applied.
Bias-check cohorts
Three pooled prospective studies used for education-stratified Cox models: NHIS linked to the National Death Index (1997–2004), UK Biobank (2006–2010), Canadian Community Health Survey (2000–2014). Per-cohort analytic sample sizes are not stated in the main text (reported in Supplementary Table 1).
Standardization
All rates age-standardized to the 2000 US standard population; 50-year risk of death computed as 1 − exp(−50·Rp/100,000) over ten 5-year age groups.
Software & code
Stata 17 and SAS 9.4; replication code deposited on Code Ocean (10.24433/CO.5179147.v1).
Honest caveats
No sub-national (state/county) resolution is reported in the main analysis beyond a sensitivity test excluding states with poor education reporting; education on death certificates is proxy-reported by family/funeral directors while denominators are self-reported in CPS — a known numerator–denominator mismatch the authors argue biases toward underestimating the low-education increase.

Core Methods

  • Education-stratified life-table risk. Age-specific death rates across ten 5-year bands (30–34 … 75–79) are converted to a 50-year risk of death via 1 − exp(−50·Rp/100,000), making the low/middle/high-education gap expressible as percentage points rather than rate ratios.
  • Counterfactual excess-death estimation. For each sex, age group and education stratum the average annual rate of reduction (AARR) is obtained by regressing log(mortality) on elapsed time from base years 1992 and 1993 separately. The high-education group's mean AARR is then applied to each lower-education group's own 1992–93 baseline: m̂t = m̄base·(1 − AARR̄)τ. The observed-minus-counterfactual difference, cumulated, gives the 3.2 million excess deaths. Note this equalizes the rate of decline, not the absolute level.
  • Peto indirect method — lung cancer as an exposure dosimeter. A Smoking Impact Ratio SIR = (C − N)/(S − N) compares the observed lung-cancer death rate C against reference smoker (S) and never-smoker (N) rates from ACS CPS-II, bounded to [0, 1]. Because lung-cancer death reflects cumulative exposure lagged 20–30 years, the SIR absorbs age, period and cohort effects (initiation age, duration, tar content, cessation) without explicit APC modelling.
  • Cause-wise attribution. Excess risk = SIR·(RR − 1)/2 (the ÷2 being the 50% confounding correction, waived for lung cancer); PSAD = excess risk / (1 + excess risk); smoking-attributable death rate = age-specific rate × PSAD. Applied separately to lung cancer, other cancers, cardiovascular disease, COPD and other respiratory disease — cirrhosis and non-medical causes are never attributed to smoking.
  • Adversarial bias auditing. (i) Cox proportional-hazards models in three pooled cohorts (NHIS-NDI, UK Biobank, CCHS) test whether smoker–never-smoker HRs differ by education — they do not materially; (ii) log-HR shrinkage after adjusting for obesity and heavy drinking is ≤10%, arguing against differential residual confounding; (iii) never-smoker lung-cancer rates are shown to be stable across education and time; (iv) the Peto method is benchmarked against two alternative indirect methods (Preston et al.; Ezzati & Lopez) and is the most conservative.
  • Separate accounting for "despair" causes. Opioid poisoning, cirrhosis, suicide, motor-vehicle and other external causes are tabulated independently; residual "other" deaths are obtained by subtraction from all-cause mortality within each age × sex × education stratum.
  • Sensitivity analyses. ≥16-years-of-education reference group; exclusion of states with poor education-reporting completeness; inclusion of unspecified-drug (T50.9) deaths as opioid deaths; correction factor varied 0–60%.

① Give the education–mortality gap a geography: a county-level Smoking Impact Ratio surface

Extending this paperThe analysis is entirely aspatial — one national curve per education × sex × age cell, with geography entering only as a sensitivity test that drops states with poor education reporting. Yet every input (NVSS microdata, CPS ASEC) carries a place code. A direct extension: compute the SIR and PSAD at county or PUMA level, using small-area estimation to stabilize sparse low-education strata, and then map where the 63% smoking share is 40% and where it is 80%. The paper's own hint that low- and middle-education mortality diverged only after ~2005 at ages 65–79 almost certainly hides regional timing differences (Appalachia and the rural South versus coastal metros) that a national average erases. Adding MGWR on top would test whether the education gradient itself is spatially non-stationary — i.e. whether "12 years of schooling" carries a different mortality penalty in different labour markets.

Added value to my researchThis is a ready-made substrate for my health-geography line: a nationally complete, cause-decomposed, education-stratified mortality surface is exactly the dependent variable my MGWR/spatial-entropy toolkit needs, and it lets me test whether built-environment and accessibility covariates explain the residual after tobacco is netted out — separating "place effects" from "exposure history effects" in a way cross-sectional health-geography studies usually cannot.

② Replace the AARR extrapolation counterfactual with an explainable, policy-aware causal model

Extending this paperThe 3.2-million figure rests on a deliberately simple counterfactual: project each lower-education group's 1992–93 baseline forward at the high-education group's annual rate of decline. That is transparent but assumes the reference group's trajectory is itself policy-neutral and that no interaction exists between education and the drivers of decline. Extending it: build a panel of state-year tobacco-policy and structural covariates (excise taxes, smoke-free-air laws, Medicaid cessation coverage, quitline funding, deindustrialization and wage indices) and estimate excess deaths with a synthetic-control or double-machine-learning design, then use SHAP-style attribution to say which levers actually bent the low-education curve. This converts a descriptive counterfactual into an identified one and turns the paper's closing call for "expanded cessation efforts" into a ranked intervention list.

③ Close the loop the paper leaves open: an accessibility layer for cessation and screening services

Extending this paperThe paper diagnoses the problem and prescribes "expanded smoking cessation efforts" — but never asks whether cessation capacity exists where the smoking-attributable burden is concentrated. That is a one-step extension with immediate policy value: overlay the county-level SIR surface from Idea ① with a 2SFCA/R2SFCA supply model of cessation resources — quitline coverage, pharmacotherapy dispensing (NRT, varenicline, bupropion), tobacco-treatment-certified clinicians, and low-dose CT lung-cancer screening sites — and compute an unmet-cessation-need index. Because the Peto method already yields a burden estimate in deaths, the mismatch can be expressed directly as avertable deaths per unserved population, giving a facility-siting objective rather than a descriptive map. The paper's own finding that never-smoking prevalence fell among low-education women makes the demand side non-stationary too, so catchments should be education-weighted.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct new application of my R2SFCA/service-area-delineation work, with an unusually strong outcome anchor: instead of validating accessibility against utilization proxies, I can validate it against cause-specific attributable mortality. It also connects to the telehealth/digital-divide line — quitlines and digital cessation apps are the classic case where virtual supply could substitute for absent physical supply, and where the digital divide may reproduce the education gradient the paper documents.

④ Generalize "lung cancer as a dosimeter" into a family of indirect exposure indices for GeoAI

Extending this paperThe paper's real methodological asset is conceptual: a long-latency sentinel outcome used as an integrated dose measure, sidestepping the impossibility of reconstructing decades of individual exposure. Extending it: ask which other exposures admit a sentinel — mesothelioma for asbestos, cause-specific respiratory mortality for chronic PM2.5, alcohol-specific liver disease for cumulative drinking — and build a shared estimator that fuses a sentinel-derived index with satellite/administrative exposure fields. Concretely for this paper: cross-validate the SIR against independent exposure reconstructions (NHIS pack-year histories, state tobacco sales) at sub-national level, and quantify how much of the education gradient survives once co-exposures like air pollution and occupational hazard, which also track education and place, are entered jointly rather than assumed away by the flat 50% confounding haircut.

Added value to my researchThe sentinel-index idea maps neatly onto my multi-source-fusion and foundation-model work: a latent cumulative-exposure field is exactly the kind of intermediate representation a fine-tuned geospatial foundation model could learn from remote sensing plus administrative data, with the sentinel outcome as weak supervision — an exposure "prior" that is physically interpretable rather than a black-box embedding.

⑤ Turn a retrospective decomposition into a forward-looking, spatially explicit agent-based forecast

Extending this paperEverything here is backward-looking: the 20–30 year lag that makes the Peto method work also means the paper is describing smoking behaviour of the 1980s–90s. The natural extension is to run it forwards. Build an agent-based / cohort-microsimulation model in which education-stratified agents initiate, persist and quit under peer, price and place effects, feed the resulting exposure histories through the same SIR→PSAD machinery, and Monte-Carlo the education gap in the 50-year risk of death out to 2050 under scenarios — flat trend, aggressive adult cessation, menthol/nicotine-cap regulation, and the substitution question the paper does not touch (e-cigarettes, which now sit between low-education initiation and cessation). Crucially, adding spatial peer structure would test whether the paper's national divergence is generated by clustered social diffusion rather than by education per se — a mechanism its aggregate design cannot see.

It also gives me a testbed for coupling network community detection (who influences whom) with spatial diffusion — the same coupling I need for mental-health service uptake models.
