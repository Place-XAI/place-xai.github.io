Computational approaches and the future of urban crime research

Gian Maria Campedelli · Zubin Jelveh · Aaron Chalfin · Daniel Semenza · Eric Piza · Ariadna Albors Zumel · Bruno Lepri · Patrick Sharkey Published 2026-07-08 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10622-4 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Urban crime research has been reshaped by novel data (mobility traces, social media, imagery, administrative micro-records) and flexible computational methods, but the field's payoff is being left on the table: prediction has advanced far faster than explanation. This Review argues that the real promise of machine learning lies beyond forecasting — in strengthening causal inference, measurement and theory testing — and sets out three conditions for that to happen: deeper cross-disciplinary integration, much stronger open-science standards, and a research agenda that escapes its Western, data-rich bias. Without those, more data and bigger models will mainly reproduce existing blind spots and enforcement biases.

Why It Matters

  • It re-points a whole field away from prediction. Predictive policing has absorbed most of the field's computational energy and most of its controversy. The Review reframes machine learning as an instrument for measurement and causal identification (heterogeneous treatment effects, debiased estimation, record linkage) rather than a risk-scoring engine — a change of purpose, not just of algorithm.
  • It names the measurement problem honestly. Police-recorded crime is a filtered signal: the "dark figure" of unreported crime, differential recording, and enforcement intensity all contaminate the outcome variable. Any model — predictive or causal — inherits that contamination unless it is modelled explicitly.
  • It documents, not just asserts, the field's imbalance. Fig. 2 uses a systematic Scopus search (queries released in the Supplementary Information) to trace how publications using unorthodox data and computational methods have grown from 1990 to 2025 — turning a disciplinary intuition into a bibliometric fact.
  • It ties an urban-science agenda to policy stakes. Safety is a precondition for mobility, health-service use, school attendance and neighbourhood investment; getting the causal claims wrong misallocates policing, lighting, greening and social-service budgets in exactly the neighbourhoods with least slack.
  • It puts open science on the critical path. Preregistration, specification-curve and multiverse analysis, code/data sharing and replication are presented as necessary infrastructure — the same standards that this Review argues have lagged in criminology relative to psychology and economics.

Core Methods

  • Structured narrative review with a theory spine: Fig. 1 organizes the field's major explanatory theories — social disorganization and collective efficacy, routine activity, rational choice, crime pattern theory, the law of crime concentration — ordered by their principal focus, giving the computational agenda something to be accountable to.
  • Bibliometric mapping: systematic Scopus queries (documented in the Supplementary Information) quantify the growth and composition of computationally-oriented urban crime research, 1990–2025 (Fig. 2).
  • Taxonomy of computational tasks beyond prediction (Fig. 3): machine learning repositioned for measurement (extracting variables from imagery and text), heterogeneous effect estimation, variable selection, record linkage, and simulation — not just forecasting.
  • Causal machine learning toolkit: double/debiased ML, causal forests and heterogeneous treatment-effect estimation, double-lasso variable selection, and explicit cautions on synthetic control for public-safety interventions; difference-in-differences with spatial spillovers is flagged as an open problem for crime's inherently diffusing treatments.
  • Spatial, network and simulation methods: micro-place concentration and spatial-scale analysis; co-offending and gang network analysis; spatio-temporal (Hawkes/point-process, graph and hypergraph neural) crime models; agent-based models — including the emerging use of LLM-driven agents — for generative theory testing.
  • Interpretability and robustness: SHAP-style attribution for model explanation, specification-curve and multiverse analysis, preregistration and ML-augmented pre-analysis plans as defences against researcher-degrees-of-freedom.
  • Data-linkage methodology: machine-learned administrative record linkage (including active learning without ground truth) treated as a measurement problem whose errors silently destroy statistical power in experiments.

① Turn the Scopus map (Fig. 2) into a geo-referenced atlas of evidence coverage — and fill its holes with open proxies

Extending this paperFig. 2 counts publications; it does not say where on Earth the evidence sits. A direct extension: geocode the study areas of every paper in the same Scopus corpus and render a global evidence-density surface against urban population, so the Review's third priority — moving beyond Western contexts — becomes a measurable gap map rather than an exhortation. Then attack the biggest holes with data that exist everywhere: OSM built form, WorldPop, nightlights, and conflict/violence event feeds, testing whether the theories in Fig. 1 (concentration, routine activity, collective efficacy) hold where police micro-data do not exist. The corpus already shows this is tractable: violence in African cities has been analysed at continental scale using city size and geographic isolation, without US-style incident data.

Added value to my researchThis is the same "evidence-desert" logic I use in health geography — mapping where accessibility studies exist versus where need is highest. Concretely, it gives me a reusable pipeline for fine-tuning geospatial foundation models on data-poor regions, transferring a model trained on data-rich cities to cities with only open proxies, and reporting transfer error honestly by density regime. It also produces a publishable methodological artifact: a FAIR, versioned "coverage atlas" schema I can reuse for telehealth and primary-care accessibility literature.

② Replace residential denominators with mobility-derived ambient populations across the whole theory stack

Extending this paperThe Review surveys ambient-population work (mobile-phone, GPS, social-media traces, activity-adjusted rates) but treats it as one data class among many. The stronger move is to make it the denominator reform of the field: systematically re-estimate the classic quantities in Fig. 1 — crime concentration at micro-places, routine-activity convergence, collective-efficacy effects — with per-hour ambient populations instead of nighttime residential counts, and report how much of each canonical finding survives. Because mobility networks are themselves segregated, the correction is not a uniform rescaling: it will shift risk toward high-throughput, low-residence places (transit nodes, entertainment districts) and away from poor residential neighbourhoods, with direct consequences for where "hot spots" are declared.

Added value to my researchThis is the exact denominator problem in my 2SFCA/R2SFCA work: supply-to-demand ratios use residential population, yet demand for clinics, pharmacies and mental-health services is realized at daytime locations. Porting the crime literature's ambient-population machinery into accessibility gives a time-resolved R2SFCA whose catchments breathe with the diurnal cycle, and it pairs naturally with my spatiotemporal KDE for producing continuous risk/access surfaces from the same trajectory data.

Cross-ref (corpus): Human mobility networks reveal increased segregation in large cities — 10.1038/s41586-023-06757-3

③ Model enforcement and reporting bias as an explicit latent layer, not as noise

Extending this paperThe Review is clear that the "dark figure" and differential recording corrupt the outcome variable, but the field's usual response is a caveat paragraph. Extend it by building the bias into the estimator: a two-stage latent-incidence model in which observed counts = true incidence × reporting propensity × enforcement intensity, with the second and third terms identified from victimization surveys, calls-for-service versus arrest discrepancies, and patrol-allocation records. Prediction then targets latent incidence, and any residual spatial structure in the enforcement term becomes a measurable audit statistic rather than an accusation — precisely the kind of signature that event-level crime prediction has already been shown to expose in US cities.

Added value to my researchHealth data have the identical pathology: recorded utilization is access × need × reporting, so unadjusted models mistake under-served areas for low-need areas. A latent-need layer with the same identification strategy would sharpen my telehealth and digital-divide work, and it slots into my explainable-ML agenda — SHAP/attribution computed on the latent-need stage rather than on the contaminated observed stage, which is where most spatial-prediction explanations currently go wrong.

Cross-ref (corpus): Event-level prediction of urban crime reveals a signature of enforcement bias in US cities — 10.1038/s41562-022-01372-0

④ Build the "beyond prediction" agenda (Fig. 3) into a spillover-aware, preregistered causal-ML stack

Extending this paperFig. 3 lists causal tasks; nobody has shipped the pipeline. The extension is an opinionated, open reference implementation: double/debiased ML and causal forests for heterogeneous effects, difference-in-differences corrected for spatial spillovers (crime displaces and diffuses — SUTVA fails by construction), the Review's own cautions on synthetic control encoded as automated diagnostics, plus specification-curve and multiverse analysis run by default so every published effect arrives with its robustness surface attached. Pair it with stability-oriented learning so estimates transfer across cities rather than overfitting one department's data-generating process — and validate it on interventions where the corpus already shows large computational studies overturning conventional wisdom about policing effects.

Added value to my researchSpillover-aware DiD is exactly what my facility-siting evaluations need: opening a clinic or an EV charger changes outcomes at neighbouring sites, so naive treatment-effect estimates are biased in the same direction as crime displacement. Adopting this stack lets me report heterogeneous, spatially-spilled treatment effects for accessibility interventions, and the multiverse/preregistration layer is a direct contribution to my open-science and FAIR-reproducibility line.

⑤ Close the loop from safety to health: crime as a spatial determinant of service access

Extending this paperThe Review's stated goal is "safer and more sustainable cities", but its outcome variable stops at crime itself. Extend it by treating crime as an impedance term in urban accessibility: estimate a perceived-risk field from incident data, street-view visual disorder and mobility avoidance behaviour, then measure how much it suppresses walking to clinics, pharmacies, transit and green space — the mechanism by which violence becomes a health exposure. That converts crime research into a coupled space–human–health system rather than a self-contained field, and it gives the computational toolkit a second, health-side validation target beyond crime counts. Urban scaling work on firearm violence and accessibility in the US shows the two sides are already measurable in the same framework.

Added value to my researchThis is the most direct import into my core agenda: a risk-weighted travel-cost surface that makes 2SFCA/R2SFCA catchments reflect not just distance but perceived safety, producing "effective accessibility" for primary care and mental-health services. It also feeds the healthy-cities and 15-minute-neighbourhood strand — a 15-minute walk that residents will not take at 8 p.m. is not 15 minutes — and gives MGWR a substantively meaningful non-stationary covariate rather than a purely statistical one.
