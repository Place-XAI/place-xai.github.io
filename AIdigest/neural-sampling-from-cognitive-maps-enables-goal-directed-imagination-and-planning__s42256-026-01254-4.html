Neural sampling from cognitive maps enables goal-directed imagination and planning

Hui Lin · Yukun Yang · Rong Zhao · Giovanni Pezzulo · Wolfgang Maass Published 2026-07-21 DOI 10.1038/s42256-026-01254-4 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Adding an inverse model (a matrix W mapping state differences back to the actions that caused them) plus noise to a learned cognitive map turns a deterministic predictor into a generative planner — the GCML (generative cognitive map learner) — that imagines a diverse menu of goal-reaching trajectories without executing any action. The same architecture reproduces rodent hippocampal replay (including rerouting around novel barriers), delivers heuristic k-shortest-path solutions on random graphs, and solves an NP-hard compositional tiling task on silhouettes it has never seen. Crucially, it needs no backpropagation — only local, self-supervised synaptic plasticity — and incurs zero replanning cost when the goal changes, making brain-like planning feasible on energy-efficient in-memory / memristive edge hardware.

★

Why It Matters

  • Planning without deep learning or LLMs. The paper's headline claim is deflationary in the best way: a core feature of intelligence — generating solutions to problems never encountered before — does not require deep networks, backpropagation, or large language models. It requires a well-shaped relational geometry plus noise.
  • Goal-directed sampling is the missing primitive. MCMC and Monte Carlo tree search sample, but are not goal-directed; RL is goal-directed, but must relearn when the goal moves. The GCML's inverse model gives a sense of direction in state space so that stochastic sampling is automatically pulled toward the goal, with a self-correcting homing-in property (a bad first move is compensated later).
  • Replanning is free. Fig. 6c shows the GCML has essentially no replanning latency when start or goal changes, whereas K*, mA* and BELA* latency grows with graph size. For any system where the target keeps moving — dispatch, service reallocation, evacuation routing — that asymmetry is the whole ballgame.
  • Strong compositional generalization from tiny training. Trained only on decompositions of five-building-block silhouettes, it decomposes eight-block silhouettes it never saw, and — without any retraining — solves the inverted task of adding blocks to build a target silhouette (the action vector is simply negated).
  • Hardware-ready by construction. Learning uses local plasticity rules only; action selection is a single matrix–vector product W(s* − ŝ), which is exactly what a memristor crossbar computes in one step, with delay independent of environment size or action-set size.
  • A bridge, not an analogy. The same model reproduces published rodent hippocampal data (Pfeiffer & Foster 2013; Widloski & Foster 2022) and a human MEG compositional-planning benchmark (Schwartenbeck et al. 2023) and beats RL/MPC baselines — neuroscience and algorithmics validated in one object.

Core Data — Sources & Volume

This is a modelling paper: all data are synthetically generated (stated verbatim in Data availability). There is no observational dataset, no geography, and no census/remote-sensing input. The "data" are three simulated task environments plus published neural recordings used only as qualitative comparison targets.

Environment 1 — 2D arena
Synthetic continuous 2D arena traversed by a random-walk agent at constant speed (0.5 unit s-1) with Gaussian turn noise; simulation time step 10 ms. A quarter of the arena was deliberately left unexplored during learning, to test generalization of imagined trajectories into unvisited space.
Neural representation
1,000 grid cells modelled analytically as sums of three planar cosine gratings (wavevectors 60° apart); grid scale λ ~ U(0.05, 8), phases ϕ ~ U(0, 2π). Place cells drawn from a pool of 1,000 neurons, each assigned in a single behavioural-timescale-plasticity-style update. Obstacle runs: γ = 0.2, noise σn = 0.3; action step size ηa = 0.05.
Environment 2 — random graphs
Synthetic random graphs, node degree uniform in [2, 5]. Main demo graph: 32 nodes, trained on 200 random trajectories of length 32. Scaling comparison in Fig. 6: N ∈ {32, 64, 96, 128, 160}, 10 graphs per size × 100 random start–goal pairs per graph; for large N, 1,000 trajectories and state dimensionality 5,000. Path-statistics panels use 40 trajectories per noise level, repeated 100× with different seeds.
Environment 3 — silhouette tiling
Compositional benchmark adapted from the human MEG study of Schwartenbeck et al. (Cell, 2023), with the 1×1 atomic tile removed (it would make every silhouette trivially decomposable). 8 building blocks: 4 corner types (4 orientations, 1 px wide) + 4 bars (1×3, 3×1, 1×2, 2×1). Silhouettes drawn on a 10 × 10 grid, giving 664 possible actions and a 100-dimensional state space. Dataset: 18,000 training + 2,000 test samples, training silhouettes built from 5 blocks, tested on 8-block silhouettes. Non-empty-goal and add-block variants each evaluated on 1,000 start–goal silhouette pairs.
Biological reference data
No new recordings. Qualitative targets are published figures: hippocampal place-cell sequences to remembered goals (Pfeiffer & Foster, Nature 497, 74–79, 2013), flexible replay rerouting around barriers (Widloski & Foster, Neuron 110, 1547–1558, 2022), theta sweeps (Vollan et al., Nature 639, 995–1005, 2025), and compositional generative replay (Schwartenbeck et al., Cell 186, 4885–4897, 2023).
Compute environment
Wall-clock timing experiments on a workstation with dual Intel Xeon E5-2690 v4 CPUs (2.60 GHz; 28 physical cores / 56 threads) and 6 NVIDIA Quadro P6000 GPUs (24 GB each). Note: the GCML itself is cheap; the heavy compute is the RL/MPC baselines.

Core Methods

  • Cognitive map as a pair of linear embeddings. Observations are embedded by s = Q o and actions by V into a shared high-dimensional state space (~1,000-D for graphs, 100-D for silhouettes), such that ŝt+1 = st + V at. The map's geometry — not a value function — carries the problem structure.
  • Learning by local plasticity only. Q, V, W and the affordance matrix G are updated by outer-product Hebbian-style rules (ΔV = ηv(st+1 − ŝt+1)aT, ΔQ = ηq(ŝt+1 − st+1)oT, ΔW = ηw at(st+1 − st)T) that approximate gradient descent on a prediction objective — no backprop, no BPTT, fully self-supervised and online.
  • The inverse model is the key novelty. W maps a state difference to the action that would produce it, so utilities are ut = W(s* − ŝt). Linearity of V plus the prediction identity guarantees such a linear inverse exists. Inverse models are standard in motor control/robotics but, as the authors note, had not been used for cognitive maps.
  • Deterministic → generative by injecting noise. Gaussian noise ε is added to the eligibility et = ĝt ⊙ (ut + ε) before a winner-take-all action choice. This single change converts the deterministic CML into the sampling GCML: low noise ≈ shortest path, high noise ≈ diverse near-optimal paths (useful when secondary rewards matter).
  • Imagination = closing the loop internally. During planning no action is executed; the predicted next state replaces environmental observation (bootstrapping), so an entire trajectory is rolled out inside the network.
  • Grid-cell front end for the spatial case. Analytic path-integrating grid cells (three cosine gratings per cell) project to place cells; PCA over the largest-scale cells recovers a near-planar 2D manifold (a locally flat patch of the grid torus). Object/barrier cells enter as repulsive forces on action selection, reproducing barrier rerouting without place-field remapping.
  • Compositional embedding + affordance gating. Silhouettes are binary pixel vectors, so Q is compositional by construction; two affordance factors g1 (block actually present) and g2 (convolution-based weighting of protruding pixels) gate the action set. W saturates at 1 and becomes binary, effectively scoring pixel overlap between the residual shape and each block.
  • Baselines and evaluation. k-shortest-path: K*, mA*, BELA*, scored by relative percentage worse Lk (k = 5), nodes visited, and replanning wall-clock. Tiling: random policy, duelling double-deep Q-network (D3QN; conv 1→32→64→64, FF 6,400→1,024→1,024→512, Adam lr 10-5, batch 512, replay 2×105), and CEM-based MPC (horizon H = 5, N = 96 rollouts, I = 4 CEM iterations), plus the deterministic CML.

① Put the cognitive map on a real street network — from 32-node random graphs to city-scale routing

Extending this paperThe GCML's abstract-graph demonstration tops out at 160 nodes of synthetic random graphs with degree 2–5, and Fig. 6a already shows a mild degradation of k-shortest-path quality as N grows. Real road networks are nothing like these: they are near-planar, heavy-tailed in edge length, hierarchical (arterial vs local), and directionally anisotropic. The natural extension is to learn the embeddings Q, V and inverse model W directly on an OSM street graph and ask the paper's own question there — does the learned map still supply a usable "sense of direction", and does the degradation with N reflect graph size or graph geometry? Because real travel routes are demonstrably not shortest paths but follow a characteristic morphology, the GCML's noise parameter σ becomes an interpretable knob: fit σ so the sampled trajectory ensemble reproduces observed route-choice dispersion, converting a modelling hyperparameter into a measured behavioural quantity.

Added value to my researchThis is the fastest route from this paper into my accessibility work. My R2SFCA and service-area delineation pipelines currently recompute travel-time catchments from scratch whenever supply, demand or a road link changes — exactly the replanning cost that Fig. 6c shows the GCML eliminates. A cognitive-map embedding of the street network gives me an amortized accessibility operator: one learned map, then near-free re-evaluation for arbitrary origin–destination pairs, which makes real-time scenario analysis (clinic closure, telehealth substitution, transit disruption) tractable at metropolitan scale.

② Read the silhouette-tiling task as a facility-siting task — compositional planning for service coverage

Extending this paperThe paper's most striking result is that a map trained on 5-block decompositions generalizes to 8-block silhouettes and, with the action vector merely negated, to building a target silhouette — all on a 10×10 grid with 664 actions. Structurally this is a covering problem: tiles are catchment footprints, the silhouette is a demand surface, "decomposable" means "coverable". The extension is to swap the synthetic silhouette generator for a real demand raster and let the building blocks be heterogeneous service catchments (clinic, pharmacy, charger, transit stop), each with its own footprint and cost. The paper's affordance gating g1/g2 already encodes feasibility constraints, so zoning, budget and land-availability constraints slot in without touching the learning rules. The empirical test the paper invites: does compositional generalization survive when blocks are irregular and demand is continuous rather than binary?

Added value to my researchThis gives me a genuinely new solver family for the siting problems I work on — EV charging-station placement under efficiency-versus-equity trade-offs, and 15-minute-neighbourhood provisioning. Unlike a greedy or MILP siting routine, the GCML returns a menu of near-optimal configurations (its noise-driven diversity), which is exactly what an equity analysis needs: I can sample 40 configurations of comparable coverage and then rank them by Gini/entropy of access rather than being handed one optimum with no alternatives.

③ Validate the "sense of direction" against human wayfinding, not just rodent replay

Extending this paperThe paper's biological grounding is qualitative and rodent-centric: sampled trajectories "look remarkably similar" to recorded replay, and the tiling task borrows its design from a human MEG study without fitting human behaviour. A serious extension is quantitative human validation: fit the GCML to individual GPS trajectories or navigation-experiment data and test whether the learned inverse model predicts the specific detours, wrong turns and self-corrections people actually make. This directly probes the paper's own claim about the self-correcting homing-in heuristic, and it would let the authors estimate the noise level of human planning rather than merely inferring that hippocampal noise "is fairly high". Recent evidence that learning produces an orthogonalized state machine in the hippocampus also offers a sharper structural prediction to test: does Q converge to an orthogonalized code, or does the linear-embedding assumption break down for richer task sets?

Added value to my researchMy agent-based models currently move agents by shortest path or a hand-tuned utility — a known source of behavioural implausibility. Dropping a GCML into the agent as its planner gives me agents that imagine before moving, generalize to unvisited parts of the city, and produce realistic route dispersion from a single interpretable noise parameter. For health-geography applications (care-seeking, mental-health service journeys) that means the model can express hesitation, detour and satisficing rather than optimality.

④ Cash the neuromorphic cheque — measure, don't just argue, the energy case

Extending this paperThe paper's most consequential claim is deployment-oriented: local plasticity suits on-chip learning, and action selection W(s* − ŝ) is a single matrix–vector product ideal for memristor crossbars with size-independent latency. Yet no hardware is used anywhere — the timing experiments run on dual Xeons and six Quadro P6000s, and no joules are reported. The obvious extension is a hardware-in-the-loop study: map Q, V, W onto an analogue crossbar and quantify what device non-idealities do to the map's geometry. This is not a formality — device noise is additive noise in exactly the place the GCML already injects noise deliberately, so the fascinating hypothesis is that analogue imprecision becomes a computational resource rather than an error budget, and the paper's σ hyperparameter is partly supplied free by the physics.

Added value to my researchThis connects to my interest in privacy-preserving, on-device geospatial analytics. If a planner of this class runs in milliwatts, then mobile-phone-trajectory and GPS analysis can happen on the handset — raw traces never leave the device, only aggregate map updates do. That reframes the digital-divide and telehealth-access work I do: edge-capable spatial intelligence is a plausible answer to the "we cannot collect this data centrally" constraint that blocks so much health-geography fieldwork.

⑤ From one map per environment to a transferable cognitive-map foundation model

Extending this paperEvery GCML in the paper is trained on its own environment — a specific arena, a specific 32-node graph, a specific tile vocabulary. Its generalization is impressive but strictly within a learned map (unvisited regions, larger silhouettes, inverted actions). The paper's own Discussion flags the next step: multiple cognitive maps at different levels of abstraction, and sampling from a learned distribution instead of fixed Gaussian noise, connecting to planning-as-inference and active inference. The concrete extension: pretrain a shared V/W across many environments so the inverse model encodes transferable relational structure, then adapt Q per environment with a handful of local-plasticity updates — a cognitive-map foundation model with few-shot transfer, whose zero-backprop adaptation would be its distinguishing feature against transformer world models.

Added value to my researchThis lands squarely on my geospatial-foundation-model and physics-informed graph-learning agenda. A pretrained spatial inverse model, fine-tuned per city with local rules only, is the cheap-transfer story my urban-agglomeration and regional-collaboration work needs — and the transparency of the three matrices makes it explainable by construction: W can be inspected directly to see which state differences drive which actions, which is far more auditable than post-hoc SHAP on a black-box spatial predictor.
