Reinforcement learning control of quantum error correction

Volodymyr Sivak · Alexis Morvan · Michael Broughton · Rodrigo G. Cortiñas · Johannes Bausch · Andrew W. Senior · Alec Eickbusch · Noah Shutty · … · Hartmut Neven · William D. Oliver · Vadim Smelyanskiy · Paul V. Klimov (Google Quantum AI & Google DeepMind collaboration, 300+ authors) Published 2026-07-08 DOI 10.1038/s41586-026-10759-2 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Quantum error correction can calibrate the machine that runs it. By repurposing QEC error-detection events as a reward signal, a policy-gradient RL agent continuously steers >1,000 analogue control parameters of a Willow superconducting processor during computation, instead of halting the algorithm for recalibration. Against injected drift this improves logical-error-rate stability 2.4× (3.5× with decoder steering) and cuts LER by 24–31%; fine-tuning an already expertly calibrated device gives a further ~20% LER suppression, reaching record 7.72(9) × 10⁻⁴ per cycle for the distance-7 surface code and 8.19(14) × 10⁻³ for the distance-5 colour code. Simulations to distance-15 (~40,000 parameters) show the convergence rate is independent of system size, because the surrogate objective is sparse on a detector–parameter factor graph.

★

Why It Matters

  • Removes a hard blocker for long algorithms. Useful quantum algorithms are projected to run for days or months; the standard fix for drift — stop everything and recalibrate — is incompatible with that. Unifying calibration with computation removes the stop.
  • Turns an error signal into a learning signal. Detection events already exist for decoding; using the same stream as reward means the control loop costs no extra quantum resources — unlike proposals based on logical swaps or code deformation, which pay in runtime, footprint and complexity.
  • Beats human experts and physics-based calibration. A ~20% additional LER suppression after exhaustive conventional calibration shows model-free in-context tuning reaches regions the physical model does not describe — the regime that matters deep below threshold.
  • Scalability is argued, not assumed. The scale-independent convergence rate follows from factor-graph sparsity (locality of detecting regions), giving a mechanism for why the approach should survive to fault-tolerant machine sizes.
  • Modality-agnostic recipe. The requirements are only "error-detection signals + tunable controls", so the framework transfers to neutral atoms, ions, and codes with nonlocal connectivity — a general template for closed-loop control of any large system that emits local failure signals.

Core Methods

  • Surrogate objective from detection statistics. Instead of optimizing the logical error rate directly (exponentially expensive to resolve, unusable in real time, hopeless as a single scalar for thousands of parameters), the agent minimizes C = E[D], the average error-detection-event rate over all detectors — a local, cheap proxy whose cost to resolve is independent of code distance.
  • Validated gradient equivalence. From the surface-code scaling model ε_L ∝ Λ^(−d/2), the paper derives ∇log ε_L = (d+1)/2 · ∇log C and confirms it experimentally with finite-difference derivatives along random directions in the high-dimensional control space (Fig. 2c).
  • Factor-graph sparsity. Detectors are local in space–time, so each term of C depends only on gates inside its detecting region. This bipartite detector↔parameter factor graph is the structural prior that makes the problem tractable and makes convergence size-independent.
  • Parameter-exploring policy gradients. A factorized multivariate Gaussian over all control parameters is maintained; each sample is a complete control policy handed to the classical controller. Mean μ(t) tracks the optimum; diagonal covariance σ(t)² sets exploration.
  • Stability and efficiency stack. Proximal policy optimization for stable updates, entropy regularization to prevent the policy from collapsing to deterministic (this is what enables steering under drift), a replay buffer for sample efficiency, and gradient masking on the factor graph for Monte-Carlo variance reduction.
  • Controlled drift injection. Artificial drift with step-like, sinusoidal and stroboscopic profiles is injected on selected CZ coupling strengths, XY pulse amplitudes and frequencies at chosen grid locations, so the steering response can be measured against ground truth (Fig. 4).
  • Joint decoder steering. The same RL loop reweights the decoder's matching graph; note the honest caveat that decoder steering currently relies on LER estimation and is therefore not yet real-time scalable.
  • Exploration–exploitation analysis. Simulated real-time steering identifies a critical drift frequency (~1/150 epochs) below which the aggregate performance including exploration noise still beats a fixed policy — the condition for steering during a single-shot long algorithm.

① Replace the factorized Gaussian with a factor-graph neural policy

Extending this paperThe authors explicitly concede the weak link: because the data rate is limited, they fall back on a diagonal multivariate Gaussian — a policy with no parameter coupling, no memory, and no transfer between devices or code distances. Yet they hand us the exact structure a network should consume: the bipartite detector↔parameter factor graph (302 parameter nodes per detector, 18 detectors per parameter at d = 5). The extension is to make the policy a message-passing GNN over that graph, conditioned on recent detection statistics, trained across distances 3→7 and transferred to 15 — turning per-device re-optimization into amortized inference and testing whether the size-independent convergence of Fig. 5c survives (or improves) with a coupled policy.

Added value to my researchThis is the same architecture I want for physics-informed graph learning on service networks: a policy that reads a bipartite demand-cell ↔ facility-parameter graph and outputs allocations. The "detecting region" is my catchment; gradient masking is exactly the credit-assignment rule an R2SFCA optimizer needs so that a clinic's capacity is updated only from signals inside its own service area — a principled alternative to the global objective I currently optimize for EV-charging siting.

② Push the drift-frequency response from a threshold to a full transfer function

Extending this paperThe paper reports two scalar summaries of its dynamics: a ~130-epoch response time and a critical steerable drift frequency of ~1/150 epochs, plus ~4 dB of low-frequency suppression under natural drift. That is a controller Bode plot with only three points on it. Extending the paper: measure the full frequency response and phase lag of the RL loop across drift amplitudes and profiles, then add a predictive (feedforward) drift model — learn the device's drift spectrum from historical epochs and pre-compensate, moving the critical frequency upward instead of accepting it. The paper concedes fast correlated drift (cosmic-ray-like impacts) must be handled in hardware; a forecast-then-steer agent is the obvious software attempt.

Added value to my researchSpatial accessibility surfaces drift too — clinics close, POIs churn, populations move, telehealth uptake shifts after a policy change. This gives me a quantitative recalibration-cadence criterion: characterize the drift spectrum of a service landscape and derive how often a 2SFCA/accessibility index must be re-estimated before it is "outdated" in exactly the paper's sense. It reframes my spatiotemporal KDE and MGWR updates as a tracking problem, not a sequence of independent cross-sectional fits.

③ Interrogate the surrogate objective: when does the local proxy lie?

Extending this paperEverything rests on C = E[D] being a faithful stand-in for the logical error rate, justified by one scaling model and validated only in the small-perturbation regime. The paper itself flags the danger — detection rates could fall because detection capability is impaired rather than because errors are suppressed (they check this once, via logical performance in Fig. 4c). A systematic extension: map the failure envelope of the surrogate — leakage, crosstalk, correlated and non-Markovian noise channels where minimizing detection events actively degrades the logical outcome — and build an explainable audit that attributes each unit of C-improvement to a physical error channel, so the agent cannot quietly optimize the wrong thing.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction agenda restated in a hard-science setting: proxy indicators (POI counts as "access", provider addresses as "capacity", search volume as "demand") are optimized all the time in urban and health analytics, almost never with a stated regime of validity. Borrowing the paper's discipline — derive the proxy↔outcome gradient relation, verify it empirically, and state where it breaks — is a directly transferable methodological standard for accessibility indices.

④ Close the loop on decoder steering without ever estimating the logical error rate

Extending this paperThe most valuable single number in the paper — 3.5× stability — depends on decoder steering, and the authors state plainly that this branch relies on LER estimation and "is not straightforwardly scalable to the real-time setting". The paper thus reports its best result using a component it admits cannot yet run in production. The concrete extension: make decoder reweighting driven purely by online detector correlation statistics (as sketched in the alternatives they cite), then re-run the drift experiment to see how much of the 3.5× survives without oracle LER access. That single experiment decides whether joint control+decoder steering is a real-time capability or a benchmark artefact.

Added value to my researchStructurally identical to a problem I face in spatial epidemiology and health-access modelling: the observation model drifts (reporting completeness, geocoding quality, testing intensity) at the same time as the process I am trying to estimate. Learning to reweight the inference graph from internal consistency signals alone — never from ground truth, which I also never have — is the transferable trick, and it pairs naturally with physics-informed constraints to keep the reweighting identifiable.

⑤ Export "never stop computing" to city-scale infrastructure control

Extending this paperThe authors claim modality-generality (only "error-detection signals and tunable controls" are required) but demonstrate it on one processor family. The strongest test of that claim is not another qubit platform — it is a completely different substrate that emits sparse local failure signals. Extending the paper means formalizing its three ingredients (local surrogate objective, factor-graph sparsity, entropy-regularized steering) as a domain-independent control template with stated preconditions: sparse locality between failure detectors and controls, cheap local signals, slow drift relative to the learning response time. Publishing that template with a non-quantum stress test would convert an impressive experiment into a reusable methodology.

Added value to my researchUrban service systems satisfy those preconditions almost exactly: EV-charging queues, telehealth session failures, transit dwell-time violations and clinic no-shows are sparse, local, continuously emitted failure detectors, and the controls (pricing, dispatch, appointment capacity, station power caps) are tunable in place. The paper hands me a defensible reason to expect size-independent convergence in a city-scale controller, and its exploration-noise analysis gives the safety criterion I would need before steering a live public service: only steer when the system's drift is slower than the agent's response time.
