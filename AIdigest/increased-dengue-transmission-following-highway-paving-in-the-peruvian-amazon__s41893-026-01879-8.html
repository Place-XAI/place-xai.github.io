Increased dengue transmission following highway paving in the Peruvian Amazon

Alyson L. Singleton · Andres G. Lescano · Kevin S. Martel · Andrew J. MacDonald · Lisa Mandle · Terrell J. Sipin · Ana S. M. Bautista · Esteban D. R. Carrera · Gustavo A. Choque · César V. Munayco · Stephen P. Luby · Erin A. Mordecai Published 2026-07-02 DOI 10.1038/s41893-026-01879-8 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

The 2009 paving of the Interoceanic Highway through the previously isolated Madre de Dios region of the Peruvian Amazon is treated as a natural experiment. Using a difference-in-differences design comparing healthcare facilities near versus far from the highway, before versus after paving, the authors attribute an additional 10,950 dengue cases (95% CI 3,186–18,715) to paving over 2009–2022 — a 403% (117–689%) increase in incidence rates at near-highway facilities relative to their pre-paving baseline. The effect attenuates beyond ~1 km from the road, is absent for leishmaniasis (a forest sand-fly disease used as a negative control), and tracks Aedes aegypti range expansion and rising traffic volume — pointing to human mobility and vector dispersal along the corridor as the mechanism. The policy implication is blunt: infectious-disease burden belongs inside road-project impact assessments.

Why It Matters

  • It converts a correlational folk belief into a causal estimate. "Roads bring disease" has long been asserted in tropical health geography; here the claim is quantified with a credible identification strategy and an explicit confidence interval on attributable cases — a number a ministry or lender can actually put in a cost–benefit table.
  • It fixes a real gap in environmental impact assessment (EIA). Standard EIAs for highway projects score deforestation, biodiversity and emissions but almost never score infectious disease transmission. This paper supplies the effect size, the spatial decay scale (~1 km), and the time horizon (14 years and counting) needed to make that line item operational.
  • The spatial gradient is the actionable part. Because the excess risk concentrates within roughly a kilometre of the pavement, mitigation is targetable: vector control, surveillance posts and clinic capacity can be prioritized along narrow corridors rather than spread thinly across a whole department.
  • The negative control is what makes it persuasive. Leishmaniasis — transmitted by forest-edge sand flies rather than a container-breeding urban mosquito — shows no comparable jump. That asymmetry rules out a large class of confounders (better reporting, more clinics, generic post-paving development) that would have lifted both diseases.
  • It is directly transferable to a live policy pipeline. Amazonian and pan-tropical road programmes (IIRSA/COSIPLAN and successors) are still expanding; the same design can be replayed wherever a paving date, a road centreline, and facility-level surveillance records coexist.
  • Fully reproducible. Case data (via the Peruvian Ministry of Health), population, environmental, traffic and spatial layers plus all analysis code are posted in a single public repository — unusual for surveillance-based health studies.

Core Methods

  • Natural experiment framing. The 2009 paving of a single, long-planned highway through a previously isolated region provides an exogenous, sharply dated treatment — the intervention was driven by international corridor politics (IIRSA/COSIPLAN), not by local dengue conditions, which is what makes the exogeneity claim defensible.
  • Difference-in-differences (DiD) identification. The estimand is the change in dengue incidence at near-highway facilities after paving, net of the contemporaneous change at far-from-highway facilities. Facility fixed effects absorb time-invariant unobservables (chronic under-reporting, catchment composition); period fixed effects absorb region-wide shocks (serotype introductions, climate years, national surveillance reforms).
  • Distance–response ("dose") specification. Rather than a single binary treatment, facilities are binned by distance to the highway, producing the attenuation curve in Fig. 3. A monotone decay with distance is far stronger evidence of a road-mediated mechanism than a binary near/far gap alone, since most confounders have no reason to decay over ~1 km.
  • Negative-control outcome. Cutaneous leishmaniasis is run through the identical pipeline. Its flat post-paving trajectory (Fig. 2) is a falsification test: reporting improvements, clinic construction, or generic development would have raised both series.
  • Event-study / dynamic treatment effects. Year-by-year coefficients relative to 2009 test the parallel-trends assumption before paving and trace how the effect accumulates over the 14 post-years — the basis for the cumulative 10,950-case counterfactual.
  • Count-model estimation with careful zero handling. The reference list points to fixest (fast high-dimensional fixed effects), glmmTMB (generalized linear mixed models), and Chen & Roth (2024) on "logs with zeros" — signalling that rare-count facilities and log-transform pathologies were handled explicitly rather than by a naive log(y+1).
  • Environmental and hydrological confounding controls. ERA5-Land climate covariates absorb temperature/rainfall-driven transmission suitability; HydroSHEDS river networks let the authors distinguish road-based connectivity from the pre-existing riverine connectivity that historically structured Amazonian movement and A. aegypti spread (including oviposition on boats).
  • Mechanism triangulation, not mediation modelling. Fig. 4 juxtaposes independent evidence — documented A. aegypti range expansion and rising MTC traffic counts — against the paving date. The mechanism (mobility + vector dispersal) is argued by concordance rather than formally decomposed, which is the paper's most obvious methodological opening.
  • Burden translation. Estimated rate differences are multiplied through WorldPop denominators and the 14-year window to yield an attributable case count with a propagated confidence interval — the form policy audiences can use.
  • Reproducible R stack. sf for spatial features, ggspatial for cartography, fixest/glmmTMB for estimation; whole pipeline public.

① Turn the binary "near/far" treatment into a continuous road-accessibility field

Extending this paperThe paper's exposure is Euclidean distance to a single highway centreline, thresholded into near/far bins. That is a strong first cut, but it treats a 1 km buffer as if all points within it were equally connected, and it ignores the feeder-road network that the paved trunk induced. A direct extension: replace distance with a network travel-time accessibility surface — minutes to Puerto Maldonado and to each border/market node, recomputed for the pre- and post-paving road graph — and re-run the DiD with a continuous, time-varying exposure. This converts the ~1 km attenuation in Fig. 3 from a geometric artifact into an interpretable travel-cost gradient, and it lets the design absorb the highway's second-order effect: the informal side roads that sprout from a paved trunk and carry the mosquito further than any buffer around the trunk itself. It also allows a proper placebo on the river network, since HydroSHEDS gives a competing travel-time field over water.

Added value to my researchThis is my accessibility machinery pointed at a hazard instead of a service. The same network catchment logic I use for 2SFCA/R2SFCA service areas — where a facility's reach is a travel-time isochrone, not a circle — becomes an exposure catchment for road-borne transmission. Concretely: build a dual-surface model in which each settlement carries a supply-side accessibility score (minutes to a health post, my usual R2SFCA target) and a demand-side hazard score (minutes to the paved corridor), then study where the two diverge. Places with high road exposure and low clinic accessibility are the compound-risk cells — precisely the kind of joint metric my healthcare-accessibility work has been missing, and one that reuses the routing/isochrone pipeline I already maintain.

② Open the mechanism black box: from concordant time series to an explicit mobility–vector transmission model

Extending this paperThe paper's mechanism claim — "likely via its effects on human mobility and vector dispersal" — rests on visual concordance in Fig. 4 between the paving date, A. aegypti range expansion, and MTC traffic counts. That is honest but weak: three series moving together does not apportion the effect. The extension is a coupled metapopulation model in which nodes are settlements along the corridor, edges are road-derived mobility flows, and each node carries a temperature-dependent transmission term (the corpus already supplies the thermal-suitability machinery through the authors' own prior work). Fitting that model to the same surveillance series would decompose the 403% increase into an importation component (infected humans arriving faster and more often) and an establishment component (vector colonizing newly reachable settlements) — a distinction with opposite policy responses: traveller screening and corridor surveillance versus larval-source management at the settlement scale.

Added value to my researchThis is a natural home for my agent-based and SIR modelling strand, but with a causal anchor most ABM work lacks: the DiD estimate becomes a calibration target, so the simulation must reproduce a measured 403% jump rather than a plausible-looking curve. That discipline — fitting mechanistic simulations to quasi-experimental effect sizes instead of to raw incidence — is a methodological pattern I can carry straight into my urban health simulations, where I routinely have interventions with known dates (transit openings, clinic closures) and no principled way to validate the ABM against them.

③ Scale the design from one highway to a pan-tropical staggered-adoption panel

Extending this paperThe study is a single, beautifully documented treatment event — which is also its ceiling: one road, one region, one paving date, and therefore no way to know whether 403% is characteristic or extreme. The extension is to build a global road-paving event catalogue from open sources (OSM history, remotely sensed road-surface change, national transport ministry records) and re-estimate as a staggered difference-in-differences across dozens of paving events in dengue-endemic tropics, using modern heterogeneity-robust estimators. The payoff is a distribution of effect sizes conditioned on moderators the single case cannot identify: baseline urbanization, pre-existing vector presence, climate suitability, forest cover, and whether the road connects to an already-endemic metropolis. That is what turns a compelling case study into the dose–response table an impact assessment actually needs when a new corridor is proposed.

Added value to my researchThis is the intersection of my multi-source data fusion and open science / FAIR commitments: a harmonized, versioned "infrastructure event × health outcome" panel is exactly the kind of reusable research asset I want to publish rather than a one-off analysis. It also gives my explainable ML work a genuinely causal substrate — instead of explaining a black-box predictor of dengue incidence, I would be modelling heterogeneity in a causal effect, where SHAP-style attributions answer "which places suffer most from a new road" rather than the far weaker "which features correlate with cases."

④ Fix the denominator problem: the road moved the people, not just the pathogen

Extending this paperA paved highway changes the population it is measured over. Migration along the Interoceanic corridor is well documented in the paper's own reference list, which means near-highway facilities after 2009 serve a larger, younger, more mobile, partly non-immune population than before — while WorldPop denominators are themselves modelled products whose accuracy near a new road is uncertain and probably lagged. The paper's fixed effects cannot absorb this because it is a time-varying, treatment-correlated shift in the denominator. Extending it: reconstruct settlement-level population with annual remotely sensed built-up growth plus night-lights, run the DiD on both official and RS-derived denominators as a bounding exercise, and add a susceptibility-depletion term so that in-migration of non-immune arrivals is modelled rather than silently folded into "transmission." Even a bounded result would strengthen the paper: showing the effect survives the worst plausible denominator error is more convincing than assuming the denominator is right.

Added value to my researchThis is the same denominator pathology that quietly corrupts 2SFCA accessibility ratios — supply-to-demand ratios are only as good as the demand surface, and I routinely inherit census-year populations for fast-changing peri-urban areas. Building a remote-sensing-informed dynamic denominator and propagating its uncertainty through the accessibility ratio is a transferable methods contribution I can apply to telehealth and primary-care access studies, where rapid residential change between census waves is the norm and error bars on accessibility are almost never reported.

⑤ Make it prospective: a road-project dengue-risk screening layer for impact assessment

Extending this paperThe paper's closing recommendation — account for dengue in road impact assessments — currently has no instrument attached. An assessor reading it learns that paving can raise incidence 403% somewhere in Peru, but not what their proposed corridor implies. The extension is a forward-looking screening tool: given a planned alignment, generate the induced accessibility change, overlay climate-driven vector suitability under scenario projections, apply the estimated distance-decay effect kernel from Fig. 3, and output an attributable-case envelope per kilometre of road with explicit uncertainty. Coupling it to spatiotemporal kernel density estimation of historical cases would let the tool flag the specific settlements where the corridor intersects existing transmission foci — the places where mitigation buys the most. Delivered as an open web layer alongside the existing GitHub repository, this is the difference between a finding and a usable regulation.

Added value to my researchThis is my efficiency-versus-equity optimization template transplanted to health mitigation. In the EV-charging work I allocate scarce stations across a Pareto frontier; here the scarce resource is vector control and surveillance capacity along a new corridor, and the objectives are averted cases (efficiency) versus protection of the most isolated, least clinic-accessible settlements (equity). Same optimizer, same explainability layer, a policy question with far higher stakes — and it links my accessibility work to the health-outcome side directly, rather than stopping at "access to care" as a proxy for health.
