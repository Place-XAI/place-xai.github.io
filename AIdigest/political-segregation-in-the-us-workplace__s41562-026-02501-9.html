Political segregation in the US workplace

Justin Frake · Reuben Hurst · Max Kagan Published 2026-06-22 DOI 10.1038/s41562-026-02501-9 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Americans are politically segregated at work, not only at home. Linking employment records to voter files for over 45 million workers, the average Democrat's coworkers are 11.7 pp [10.6, 12.8] more Democratic than the average Republican's; net of geography, industry and occupation the residual segregation is 2.9 pp [2.7, 3.1] — on par with analogously measured gender segregation (2.8 pp). Segregation is strongest among donors (14.8 pp) and senior executives (14.7 pp), is asymmetric (the average Republican's coworkers are 50% Democratic, whereas only 32% of the average Democrat's are Republican), and has been essentially flat from 2012 to 2024 (11.1 pp → 11.0 pp).

★

Why It Matters

  • Closes the last big gap in the sorting literature. Partisan sorting has been mapped for neighborhoods, families, congregations and dating markets; the workplace — where working adults spend most of their non-home waking hours — was the missing context. This is the first population-scale measurement of it.
  • Reframes the contact-hypothesis debate empirically. The workplace has been the great hope for cross-cutting political contact. The paper shows that hope is partly misplaced but not dead: raw segregation is large, yet most of it is explained by where and in what job people work, leaving a modest but real residual of firm-level sorting.
  • Separates composition from choice. By decomposing 11.7 pp into a geography/industry/occupation component and a 2.9 pp residual, it distinguishes structural sorting (labor markets are already spatially and sectorally partisan) from firm-level sorting (selection into and out of specific employers).
  • Identifies who drives it. Segregation concentrates in the politically active and the powerful (donors, senior executives) — precisely the people who set firm policy, hiring criteria and public political posture, giving the finding organizational teeth.
  • Asymmetry has policy consequences. Republicans encounter far more out-party coworkers than Democrats do. Any intervention built on "workplace contact" therefore acts on the two parties unequally.
  • Stability is the surprise. Affective polarization rose sharply over 2012–2024, yet workplace segregation did not — implying the drivers of polarization are not simply a shrinking of cross-partisan work contact.

Core Methods

  • Probabilistic record linkage at population scale: merge resume/employment records to voter registrations using Fellegi–Sunter-style probabilistic matching (the paper's reference list cites Fellegi & Sunter 1969, Enamorado, Fifield & Imai 2019, and embedding-based linkage) to build a 45M+ worker panel with both employer and partisanship.
  • Hierarchical partisanship imputation: a sequential decision rule (Extended Data Fig. 1) — L2 party registration first; else primary-voting history; else third-party registration mapped to the ideologically aligned major party; else Bayesian imputation taking the maximum-posterior label. Independents are dropped.
  • Exposure-based segregation measure: for each focal worker, compute the partisan composition of their coworkers; segregation is the gap in mean coworker partisanship between the average Democrat and the average Republican, expressed in percentage points. This is an exposure/isolation-style index rather than a dissimilarity index, which is what makes the asymmetric result (50% vs 32%) directly readable.
  • Fixed-effects decomposition: re-estimate the gap conditioning on geography, industry and occupation, isolating the residual within-market, within-sector firm-level sorting (11.7 pp → 2.9 pp).
  • Same-estimator benchmarking: apply the identical estimator to gender so the political effect size (2.9 pp) is interpretable against a familiar social cleavage (2.8 pp) instead of on an arbitrary scale.
  • Heterogeneity analysis: split by political activity (donors vs non-donors) and by hierarchical/market power (senior executives vs rank-and-file), plus occupation and industry ridge distributions (Figs. 3, 5).
  • Longitudinal replication: re-run the estimator annually across 2012–2024 to test whether workplace segregation tracked the documented rise in affective polarization (Fig. 7).
  • Robustness by specification curve: the reference list cites specification curve analysis (Simonsohn et al. 2020), indicating results are reported across many analytic choices rather than a single preferred specification; Supplementary Figs. 1–12 and Tables 1–7 carry the robustness set.
  • Coverage validation: benchmark sample demographics, industry mix and O*NET occupation zones against CES, ACS and BLS to characterize non-representativeness of resume-based data.

① Give the paper a commute: turn workplace segregation into a spatiotemporal exposure field

Extending this paperThe paper's unit of exposure is the employer roster — a set with no geometry and no clock. Two workers count as "coworkers" whether they sit at adjacent desks or in offices 2,000 km apart, and a night-shift nurse is credited with the same exposure as a day-shift colleague she never meets. Extending it: geocode the Revelio establishment records, attach commute sheds and shift/occupation time-use profiles, and recompute the 11.7 pp / 2.9 pp gaps as a spatiotemporal co-presence field rather than a roster overlap. My kernel-density-in-space-time machinery is exactly the right estimator here, and the prediction is sharp and testable: the residual 2.9 pp should grow once co-presence is measured properly, because roster-based counting dilutes real contact with nominal contact. It would also convert Fig. 2's metro map from a set of static bars into a continuous exposure surface that can be differenced against the residential partisan surface.

Added value to my researchThis is a direct import into my job–housing network line: the same linked employer–residence panel yields partisan-labeled commute flows, which lets me ask whether job–housing imbalance and political sorting are the same phenomenon viewed from two ends of the trip. It also gives my spatiotemporal KDE work a genuinely population-scale test bed — 45M workers with employer, home ZIP and occupation is a far richer substrate than the GPS-trajectory samples I normally work with, and the resulting "experienced political exposure" surface is a new covariate I can push into the health-geography models where neighborhood social composition already matters.

② Replace the fixed-effects decomposition with an explainable-ML attribution of where sorting happens

Extending this paperThe paper's central move — 11.7 pp collapsing to 2.9 pp once geography, industry and occupation are absorbed — is a global, additive, linear decomposition. It tells us how much the three controls explain on average, but not where in the country, in which sectors, or for which worker types the residual firm-level sorting concentrates; a 2.9 pp national mean is fully consistent with near-zero sorting in most labor markets and severe sorting in a few. Extending it: refit the same target with a gradient-boosted or GNN model over (metro, industry, occupation, firm size, seniority, donor status) and use SHAP-style attributions plus MGWR to produce a spatially varying decomposition — effectively an interaction-aware, non-stationary version of Fig. 4. Given the paper's own specification-curve robustness ethos, this is a natural companion rather than a challenge to it.

Added value to my researchThis is my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction agenda applied to a social rather than an environmental outcome, and it stress-tests a methodological claim I care about: that MGWR bandwidths and SHAP interaction values can be read as substantive statements about scale, not just diagnostics. A 45M-record national panel with clean administrative labels is an unusually good place to check whether the two attributions agree. Whatever calibration I learn transfers straight back to my healthcare-accessibility models, where I face the identical question of how much of a disparity is compositional versus locally generated.

③ Audit the measure itself: aggregation bias, imputation error and the "what counts as a coworker" problem

Extending this paperThree measurement choices carry the whole result and none is currently bounded. First, the coworker unit: firm, establishment, department or team — segregation indices are notoriously sensitive to the aggregation level, and a firm-level roster in a 50,000-person employer is close to meaningless as a contact measure. Second, Independents are dropped, which is a substantial and non-random deletion that mechanically sharpens the two-party contrast. Third, the Bayesian imputation step assigns partisanship to an unreported share of records, and imputation that borrows strength from geography and demography risks importing the very residential sorting the paper is trying to net out. Extending it: run a full sensitivity surface — vary the aggregation unit, re-include Independents under several allocation rules, and re-estimate with imputed records excluded — reporting how far the 2.9 pp residual can be pushed. The corpus already contains a template for exactly this kind of aggregation-bias audit for racial segregation.

Added value to my researchThis is the segregation-metric analogue of the MAUP and catchment-definition problem that dogs every 2SFCA study I publish: my accessibility scores are similarly hostage to the choice of unit and the treatment of unclassified records. Building a reusable sensitivity-surface protocol here — vary unit, vary inclusion rule, vary imputation, report the envelope rather than the point estimate — gives me a reporting standard I can carry into my own accessibility papers, and it fits the open-science/FAIR line directly since the protocol is publishable independently of the restricted data.

④ From a static gap to a dynamic model: who leaves, who is hired, and how the equilibrium stays flat

Extending this paperThe paper's most puzzling finding is the flat 2012–2024 trend (Fig. 7) during a period of steep affective polarization. A cross-sectional gap cannot explain this, because the same 11 pp can be produced by a frozen workforce or by furious churn in which partisan hiring and partisan quitting exactly offset. Since Revelio records are longitudinal position histories, the paper's own data can be reorganized as a transition model over the firm network: estimate partisan-conditional hiring and separation hazards, then run an agent-based or Markov simulation to ask which micro-rules reproduce a flat macro trend. Layering this on the corpus's worker-mobility network gives a mechanism where the paper currently reports only a stable moment — and it directly tests the alternative that segregation is stable because sorting-in and sorting-out have reached a steady state.

Added value to my researchAgent-based modeling and network community detection are core to my toolkit, and this is a rare setting where the micro-transitions are observed rather than assumed — which makes it an ideal calibration case for ABMs I otherwise have to parameterize from survey priors. The firm-to-firm mobility graph, partitioned by partisan composition, is also structurally the same object as my urban-agglomeration and regional-collaboration networks, so community-detection methods developed here transfer with almost no modification.

⑤ Push the finding downstream into health: does workplace partisan exposure move health behavior and service use?

Extending this paperThe paper stops at measurement — it establishes exposure but never tests a consequence. The obvious and highest-value downstream question is health, because the COVID era produced sharply partisan gradients in vaccination, masking, telehealth uptake and trust in providers, and the paper's own reference list already includes work showing that Democratic and Republican physicians deliver different care on politicized issues. Extending it: link the metro- and industry-level segregation estimates (Figs. 2–3) to county health-behavior outcomes and ask whether higher out-party workplace exposure predicts convergence in health behavior — a population-scale contact-hypothesis test with a health endpoint, using segregation as the treatment and geography-matched pairs for identification. Ecological inference is the obvious threat and must be handled explicitly, but the design is feasible entirely with public outcome data.

Added value to my researchThis lands squarely in my health-geography and telehealth/digital-divide work: I already model spatial access to primary and mental-health care, and workplace partisan composition is a plausible and completely unexplored determinant of whether accessible care is actually used. It supplies a new social-exposure covariate for my 2SFCA models — one measured at the workplace rather than the residence, which is exactly the blind spot in residence-based accessibility measures — and opens a genuinely novel framing: political accessibility as a companion to physical accessibility in explaining unmet health need.
