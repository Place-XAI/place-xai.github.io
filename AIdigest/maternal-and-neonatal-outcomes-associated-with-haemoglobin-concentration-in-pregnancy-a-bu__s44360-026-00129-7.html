Maternal and neonatal outcomes associated with haemoglobin concentration in pregnancy: a Burden of Proof study

Ni Gusti Ayu Nanditha · Dong Keun Rhee · Corey J. Teply · Heidi A. Tandiono · Ihunaya Okorie · Heather J. Taylor · … · Christopher J. L. Murray · Simon I. Hay · Theresa A. McHugh · Nandita Perumal · Nicholas J. Kassebaum (32 authors) Published 2026-06-24 DOI 10.1038/s44360-026-00129-7 Source nature.com ↗
Core Conclusion

Maternal haemoglobin is related to pregnancy outcomes through nonlinear, two-sided dose–response curves, not a step at a fixed cutoff: even small deviations from the level of lowest predicted risk — especially on the low (anaemic) side — track with higher risks of all-cause maternal and neonatal mortality, postpartum haemorrhage, maternal sepsis, preterm birth, low birth weight and large-for-gestational-age. Across outcomes reaching at least moderate evidence strength (>2 stars in the Burden of Proof rating), the haemoglobin level associated with lowest risk ranged from 109 to 135 g l⁻¹ — an outcome-specific spread that challenges the appropriateness of a single fixed anaemia threshold. Because the curves are steep near the optimum, even modest population-level shifts in the haemoglobin distribution would meaningfully change the burden of adverse outcomes, arguing for trimester- and outcome-specific thresholds.

★

Why It Matters

  • It attacks a threshold that governs global practice. The WHO haemoglobin cutoff for anaemia in pregnancy is one of the most widely applied diagnostic constants in the world. Showing that the risk-minimising level is outcome-specific (109–135 g l⁻¹) reframes anaemia from a binary label into a continuous, multi-outcome risk surface.
  • Two-sided risk, not just deficiency. Both low and high haemoglobin carry risk, so screening logic built only around a lower bound is structurally incomplete — a point with direct implications for supplementation and transfusion policy.
  • Uncertainty is a first-class output. The Burden of Proof framework converts a heterogeneous, noisy literature into conservative risk functions plus a quantitative star rating, so weak evidence is discounted rather than silently averaged in — a template for evidence-to-policy translation.
  • Population-shift framing makes it actionable. Because the curves are nonlinear, the paper reasons about what happens when a whole distribution moves, which is the quantity public-health programmes (fortification, supplementation, antenatal care coverage) actually control.
  • Very high leverage per unit effort. Maternal anaemia is among the most prevalent conditions in pregnancy worldwide; small threshold refinements propagate to a very large number of pregnancies.

Core Methods

  • Comprehensive systematic reviews per outcome: literature searched and screened separately for each maternal and neonatal outcome, with alternate and excluded outcome definitions documented explicitly (Extended Data Table 1) — the definitional bookkeeping that makes cross-study pooling defensible.
  • Burden of Proof meta-analytic framework (Zheng et al., Nat. Med. 28, 2038–2044, 2022): a mixed-effects meta-regression that estimates a continuous, monotonicity-agnostic dose–response curve rather than a single pooled relative risk.
  • Explicit separation of two uncertainty layers: the forest plots distinguish 95% uncertainty intervals excluding versus including between-study heterogeneity — the second is what the conservative risk function is built on.
  • Quantitative star rating of evidence strength: each risk–outcome pair receives a star score derived from the most conservative interpretation of the curve; only pairs with >2 stars are used for the headline threshold claim.
  • Identification of the level of lowest predicted risk per outcome (the risk-minimising haemoglobin), then comparison of that set of optima against fixed WHO cutoffs.
  • Complementary binary analyses: pooled estimates for low Hb, anaemia and high Hb versus each outcome (Extended Data Figs. 2, 4–7), providing a bridge to the conventional threshold-based literature.
  • Evidence-coverage mapping: country-level counts of unique extracted studies per outcome, exposing geographic gaps in the underlying evidence base.
  • Population-shift reasoning: because the fitted curves are nonlinear, the paper evaluates how shifting the population haemoglobin distribution — not just moving individuals across a cutoff — changes expected burden.

① Give the risk curves a geography: spatially downscaled attributable burden

Extending this paperThe paper produces one global dose–response curve per outcome and then maps only where the studies came from (Extended Data Figs. 1, 3) — never where the burden falls. The obvious extension is to convolve each fitted curve with a subnational haemoglobin exposure surface for pregnant women (survey clusters plus gridded population and covariates) to produce high-resolution maps of attributable maternal and neonatal burden, with the star rating propagated as a spatially explicit confidence layer. That turns an aspatial evidence synthesis into a targeting instrument, and it makes the paper's own "modest population-level shifts matter" claim testable place by place: the same 5 g l⁻¹ shift buys far more in a district sitting on the steep left arm of the curve than in one near the optimum.

Added value to my researchThis is a clean template for my GeoAI burden-mapping work: an exposure–response function as the physical prior, a machine-learned exposure surface as the spatial layer, and explainable ML to attribute residual spatial variation to socio-environmental covariates. It also gives me a principled way to convert any dose–response literature into a place-based priority index — exactly the object I need when linking space–human systems to health policy.

② From one global optimum to spatially varying, context-conditioned thresholds

Extending this paperThe headline finding is that the risk-minimising haemoglobin spans 109–135 g l⁻¹ across outcomes — but the paper still reports a single optimum per outcome, pooled over all contexts. Haemoglobin, however, is strongly modified by altitude, smoking, malaria and haemoglobinopathy prevalence, plasma-volume expansion by trimester, and assay/point-of-care measurement mode (the reference list cites exactly these adjustment debates). A natural extension is a meta-regression with spatially varying and context-varying coefficients — letting the optimum itself be a function of altitude, trimester and endemicity — so that the output is a threshold surface rather than a threshold number, with uncertainty widening where studies are absent.

Added value to my researchThis is MGWR/GWR logic imported into evidence synthesis: non-stationarity of an effect optimum rather than of a regression coefficient. I can reuse my spatially varying coefficient tooling plus SHAP-style attribution to ask which contextual variables actually move a clinical threshold, and to quantify how much harm a one-size-fits-all cutoff does in specific geographies — a directly publishable health-geography contribution.

③ Close the loop from risk curve to service delivery: accessibility-aware supplementation targeting

Extending this paperThe paper stops at risk. It never asks the operational question its own conclusion raises: if a district's haemoglobin distribution should shift, through which facilities and supply chains does that happen? Extending it means coupling the fitted curves to an antenatal-care accessibility model — 2SFCA/R2SFCA catchments over facility locations, travel-time networks and pregnancy demand surfaces — and then solving an allocation problem for iron/IV-iron and screening capacity that maximises expected averted burden subject to travel-time and stock constraints. The output is not a threshold but a ranked deployment plan, and it makes the two-sided nature of the curve operational (over-supplementation in high-Hb subpopulations is a real cost, not a rounding error).

Added value to my researchThis fuses my two strongest lines: R2SFCA accessibility and efficiency-versus-equity facility optimization (the same Pareto machinery I built for EV charging stations transfers directly to antenatal screening points and haematinic distribution). ④ Replace the static exposure with a trimester-resolved haemoglobin trajectory

Extending this paperThe paper itself calls for trimester-specific thresholds, yet its inputs are largely single-timepoint haemoglobin measurements, because that is what the published literature reports. The extension is a functional / longitudinal dose–response model where the exposure is the shape of the haemoglobin trajectory across gestation — baseline level, magnitude of the second-trimester haemodilution nadir, and rate of recovery — rather than a scalar. Digital antenatal platforms and routine point-of-care testing now generate exactly these repeated measures at scale, so the framework could be re-estimated on trajectory features and the star ratings recomputed. This would test whether the apparent "high-Hb risk" arm is partly a failure of physiological plasma-volume expansion rather than a haemoglobin effect at all.

Added value to my researchTrajectory-as-exposure is spatiotemporal modelling in a clinical register, and it plugs into my telehealth/digital-divide agenda: repeated antenatal measures exist only where digital and physical access exist, so trajectory-based thresholds will be systematically better calibrated in well-served areas. Quantifying that gradient is itself a health-geography result, and it feeds my spatiotemporal kernel/trajectory methods a genuinely high-stakes application.

⑤ Audit the evidence base itself: access-driven selection bias in the inputs, and an open evidence-gap engine

Extending this paperExtended Data Figs. 1 and 3 quietly show that the underlying studies are geographically clustered — yet the pooled curves are then applied globally. Two extensions follow. First, a bias audit: pregnancies observed in the literature are those reaching facilities with haematology and follow-up, so both exposure measurement and outcome ascertainment are conditioned on healthcare access; the paper's star rating penalises heterogeneity but not this structural selection. Modelling access as an explicit missing-not-at-random mechanism would show how the optimum shifts. Second, because the Burden of Proof code, curves and per-analysis data points are all public, this becomes a reproducible, continuously updatable evidence-gap engine — country-by-outcome maps of where one additional well-designed study would most tighten the curve.

Added value to my researchThis is my open-science/FAIR line with real teeth: a living, versioned pipeline that turns a published meta-analysis into a queryable spatial evidence product, plus an explainable-ML diagnostic for access-driven data bias that I can carry straight into my telehealth and primary-care accessibility studies, where "who appears in the record" is exactly the confound I keep fighting.
