Model-guided data production unlocks reliable reaction predictions

Research Briefing — no byline listed on the article page; it is a summary of Neves, P., Hao, B., Aikonen, S., Diccianni, J. B., Wegner, J. K., Schwaller, P. & Strambeanu, I. I. Published 2026-08-10 DOI 10.1038/s43588-026-01018-5 Source nature.com ↗ Summarizes 10.1038/s43588-026-01017-6
Core Conclusion

For Buchwald–Hartwig C–N cross-coupling, the bottleneck on machine-learning reliability is not the algorithm but the data. A newly produced high-throughput experimentation (HTE) dataset was merged with harmonized open-source HTE data to build the largest curated BH corpus to date, and active learning was used to decide which experiments to run next rather than merely to fit an existing table. The resulting model holds up out of distribution — on substrates and conditions it never saw — and its recommendations were confirmed at the bench, rescuing historically failed couplings and enriching hit rates in a previously unexplored chemical space. The transferable message: model-guided data production, plus diversity-aware curation, beats simply scraping more data.

Why It Matters

  • Closes the loop between model and laboratory. Most reaction-prediction work treats the dataset as fixed. Here the model designs the next plate: two of the three HTE iterations were model-guided, targeting reactions where the model was most uncertain. Data production becomes part of the learning algorithm.
  • Attacks out-of-distribution failure head-on. Reported yield models routinely look excellent on random splits and collapse on new substrates. The underlying study benchmarks on held-out chemical clusters — training on nine clusters and testing on three that contain unseen chemistry — which is the regime medicinal chemists actually live in.
  • Shows diversity beats volume. The headline data-centric result: OOD performance correlated more strongly with a chemical-reaction diversity score (Pearson r ≈ 0.79 against ROC AUC in the hardest OOD test) than with raw training-set size (r ≈ 0.60). "Add more rows" is the wrong instinct; "add more different rows" is the right one.
  • Prospectively validated, not just retrospectively scored. Model-ranked conditions rescued 10 of 11 historically unsuccessful aryl-halide–amine pairs, and high-confidence predictions in a ~2.46 × 1011-reaction virtual space raised the experimental success rate from ~19% (baseline selection) to ~33% — a 1.64-fold enrichment.
  • Industrial data, released. A pharmaceutical HTE campaign (labelled JnJ25 in the study) was standardized and pushed into the open alongside code — a rare case of proprietary reaction data becoming a public benchmark.

New HTE data
11,300 (11.3k) high-quality Buchwald–Hartwig reactions generated in-house across three iterations — iteration 1 for condition-space coverage, iterations 2–3 model-guided toward high-uncertainty substrate space. Described as the largest and most comprehensive BH HTE dataset in the literature to date (labelled JnJ25).
Aggregated corpus
The new data were merged with all published open-source BH HTE datasets (eight open-source sets are compared in the study) after standardization into a unique-structure-per-entity format, giving a combined dataset of ~27,500 (~27.5k) reactions ("All w JnJ25").
Label harmonization
Heterogeneous quantification methods (CAD, isolated yield, MisER, LCAP) were harmonized into a binary go/no-go label at a 10% yield threshold. Only 11.9% of reactions fall in the 5–15% boundary band where measurement noise could flip the label.
Reagent space
Models operate within 32 palladium precatalysts, 12 bases and 12 solvents seen in training — comprehensive but an explicit subset of commercial options (stated limitation).
Virtual spaces
Active-learning acquisition explored a virtual space of 64,000,000 (64M) BH reactions. Prospective OOD validation sampled a space of 13 catalysts × 12 bases × 7 solvents × 12,252 aryl halides × 18,364 amines ≈ 2.46 × 1011 reactions.
Experimental validation
Rescue set: 11 historically unsuccessful aryl-halide–amine pairs, screened over 1,092 candidate reagent-condition combinations (reagents with ≥400 training samples); the 4 top-ranked conditions per pair → 44 reactions, 27 succeeded (>10% yield), 10/11 pairs (91%) rescued. Prospective set: baseline 4,800 reactions at ~19% success; moderate-confidence (<0.85) 21% (1.11×); high-confidence (>0.85) 33% (1.64×).
Platforms & assay
96-well and 384-well plate HTE with automated solid/liquid dispensing, designed in Katalyst; quantification by UPLC–MS–CAD. Cross-format reproducibility check on 384 reactions (6 substrate pairs × 64 conditions): 59% agreed within <5% yield, 27% within 5–10%, 13% within 10–13.2%, none beyond 13.2%.

Core Methods

  • Two-phase data production. Phase 1 builds a baseline model from an informer set chosen by structure diversity, chemical-space density and undesired-substructure filtering. Phase 2 runs active learning on top of that baseline to select the next experiments.
  • Ranked batch-mode active learning. Acquisition combines model uncertainty with Tanimoto distance from already-selected points plus diversity and density terms, so a batch is informative and internally non-redundant — the right formulation when experiments arrive a plate at a time, not one at a time.
  • Representation ensemble to control model bias. Six ML models trained on two feature families — the differential reaction fingerprint (DRFP) and a physicochemical descriptor vector — plus one SMILES-based language model (BERT Enriched Embedding, used to drive acquisition). Top families after hyperparameter search: random forest, histogram-based gradient boosting, and MLP.
  • Cluster-based OOD benchmarking. All reactions are embedded as DRFPs and partitioned by k-means into 12 clusters; models train on nine clusters and test on three held-out ones, cycling until every cluster has been tested, averaged over 10 (calibration: 20) random initializations. Substrates present in training are excluded from test clusters — a deliberately strict split.
  • Metric-bias control. Eight performance metrics were computed and four retained (AUPRC, balanced accuracy, F1, ROC AUC) to cover uncertainty quality, precision and recall under the strong class imbalance typical of OOD screening.
  • A data-centric diversity score (CRDS). A composite of unique reactant pairs, unique reagent combinations and dataset size, with empirically fitted exponents. It correlates with OOD ROC AUC at r ≈ 0.79 versus r ≈ 0.60 for size alone — but the authors flag that the exponents are fitted to BH chemistry and would need recalibration elsewhere.
  • Uncertainty calibration then decision use. Confidence (normalized to 0.5–1) is binned and checked against realized accuracy, then used operationally as a screening threshold (0.85) for prospective reaction selection.
  • Reagent-recommender deployment. The trained classifier is inverted into a condition recommender over frequently-observed reagents, ranking catalyst/base/solvent triples for a given substrate pair.

Expanded Ideas — this paper's extensibility & added value to my research

Each idea stays anchored to this paper: first how the work itself could be extended (drawing on our corpus), then the added value for my research lines (migis.net — GeoAI, 2SFCA/R2SFCA accessibility, explainable & physics-informed spatial learning, multi-source data fusion, reproducibility).

① Turn CRDS from a post-hoc correlate into a pre-training data-value planner

Extending this paperThe chemical-reaction diversity score is the paper's most portable idea, but it is currently used descriptively — computed on finished datasets and correlated with OOD ROC AUC (r ≈ 0.79), with exponents empirically fitted to Buchwald–Hartwig chemistry. The natural extension is to make it prescriptive and reaction-class-agnostic: learn the exponents jointly across several reaction families so CRDS becomes a calibrated predictor of achievable OOD performance, then invert it into a budget allocator that answers "which 500 wells, of the 64M candidates, buy the most generalization per dollar?" Pairing this with the paper's own held-out-cluster protocol would let CRDS be validated as a forecast rather than a hindsight statistic — and would test whether the "diversity beats size" claim survives outside C–N coupling.

Added value to my researchThis is directly transplantable to spatial machine learning, where I face the identical pathology: models trained on data-rich metropolitan areas that collapse on rural or under-surveyed regions. A geographic-representation diversity score — built from unique built-environment configurations, socio-demographic strata and network morphologies rather than reactant pairs — would let me forecast, before training, how far a telehealth-access or primary-care 2SFCA model will transfer to an unseen county. It also reframes my open-science work: a dataset's FAIR value becomes quantifiable as marginal diversity contributed to a pooled corpus, not merely row count.

② Make the acquisition function cost-aware and multi-fidelity

Extending this paperRanked batch-mode active learning here weighs uncertainty, Tanimoto distance, diversity and density — but treats every candidate reaction as equally expensive. In reality a 384-well plate costs far less per point than a 96-well plate, some reagents are scarce, and some substrates require synthesis before they can even be screened. Recasting acquisition as a cost- and fidelity-aware optimization — cheap low-fidelity 384-well screens to map the space coarsely, expensive high-fidelity 96-well confirmations only where the decision boundary is genuinely contested — would extend the paper's own cross-format reproducibility check (59% of reactions agreed within <5% yield between formats) into a principled two-tier design. The paper supplies exactly the calibration data such a multi-fidelity model would need.

Added value to my researchMy data-collection problems are structurally the same and just as budget-bound: survey waves, clinic-level audits, GPS-trace licensing and high-resolution imagery all differ by an order of magnitude in cost per observation. A cost-aware acquisition layer would let me plan where to sample next for accessibility modelling — which tracts to survey, which facilities to audit — instead of accepting whatever administrative data happens to exist. This turns the "digital divide" from a fixed obstacle into a sampling-design variable I actively optimize against.

③ Generalize cluster-held-out OOD benchmarking into a discipline-wide evaluation protocol

Extending this paperThe DRFP + k-means, nine-train / three-test cluster protocol is a genuinely strict OOD design, but it is one axis of shift only: substrate-structure novelty. Reaction models fail along other axes too — new reagent classes, new laboratories, new measurement instruments, and simple temporal drift as chemistry programmes move on. Extending the paper means building a shift taxonomy: condition-space holdouts (unseen catalyst families), source holdouts (train on open data, test on industrial ELN), and prospective time splits, each reported separately so that "robust OOD" stops being a single scalar. The eight-metric / four-retained discipline the paper already establishes gives the reporting scaffolding.

Added value to my researchThis is the chemistry counterpart of the spatial-block cross-validation argument I keep making, and it gives me an out-of-field precedent to cite. The direct import: define cluster-OOD splits over urban morphology and socio-demographic space, not just geographic blocks, and report accessibility-model performance per shift type — new city, new health system, new data vintage. Anything less continues to flatter spatial models that have memorized regional idiosyncrasy.

④ Replace confidence thresholds with conformal guarantees and explicit decision economics

Extending this paperThe model's calibrated confidence is used as a blunt screen — a 0.85 cut that lifts hit rate from ~19% to ~33%. That is impressive but leaves value on the table, because the threshold is not tied to the asymmetric cost structure of the decision: a wasted well is cheap, a missed viable route to a drug candidate is not. Extending the paper: wrap the classifier in conformal prediction for distribution-free coverage guarantees on OOD clusters, then choose the operating point by expected utility rather than a round number, reporting the full enrichment-versus-coverage curve. The paper's confidence-binned calibration data (20 random cluster initializations) is precisely the substrate for this.

Added value to my researchHealth-policy predictions are the extreme case of asymmetric cost: under-predicting mental-health service shortage in an under-served county is not symmetric with over-predicting it in a wealthy one. Conformal prediction sets with equity-weighted decision thresholds — where the utility function encodes distributional justice rather than mean accuracy — would let my EV-charging and clinic-siting optimizations state honestly where the model abstains. That abstention region is itself a policy finding: it maps where we lack the data to govern well.

⑤ Recover the signal that binary harmonization throws away — a measurement model for fused multi-source data

Extending this paperCollapsing CAD, isolated yield, MisER and LCAP onto a single 10% go/no-go threshold is what makes the ~27.5k-reaction merge possible, and the authors are candid that it discards quantitative yield and that 11.9% of reactions sit in the ambiguous 5–15% band. The principled extension is a hierarchical measurement model: treat true yield as a latent continuous variable and each quantification method as an observation channel with its own bias and noise, fitted from overlapping reactions (the 384-reaction cross-format comparison provides exactly such overlap). This would keep the ordinal signal, propagate label uncertainty into training, and let the ambiguous band contribute information instead of noise — plausibly recovering accuracy the binarization currently spends.

Added value to my researchThis is my multi-source fusion problem stated in another vocabulary. Mobile-phone records, social-media geotags, travel surveys and remote sensing each measure "mobility" or "exposure" through a different instrument with a different bias, and the field's default — harmonize by coarsening to a common denominator — destroys exactly the resolution that motivated multi-source fusion. A latent-construct plus per-source measurement model is the upgrade path for my spatiotemporal KDE and accessibility work, and it makes source bias an estimated quantity I can report rather than a caveat I write in the discussion.
