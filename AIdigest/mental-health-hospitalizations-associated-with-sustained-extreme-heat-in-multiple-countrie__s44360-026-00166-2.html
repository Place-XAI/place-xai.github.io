Mental health hospitalizations associated with sustained extreme heat in multiple countries

Yanming Liu · Zhihu Xu · Wenzhong Huang · Zhengyu Yang · Rongbin Xu · Wenhua Yu · Yiwen Zhang · Yao Wu · Pei Yu · Tingting Ye · Bo Wen · Gongbo Chen · Shuang Zhou · Yuxi Zhang · Ke Ju · Simon Hales · Eric Lavigne · Paulo H. Core Conclusion

Sustained extreme heat — not merely a hot day — acutely raises hospital demand for mental and behavioural disorders. Across 2,618,307 warm-season hospitalization records from 852 locations in Brazil, Canada, Chile and New Zealand (2000–2019), heatwave days (daily mean temperature above the location-specific 97.5th percentile for ≥4 consecutive days) carried a relative risk of 1.033 (95% CI 1.007–1.059) on the same day and 1.056 (1.011–1.103) cumulated over lag 0–8 days. The excess risk was concentrated among older adults and residents of low-population-density areas, implying that psychiatric surge capacity, not just cooling, belongs in heat preparedness plans.

Why It Matters

  • Closes a persistent evidence gap with cross-national scale. Heat–mental-health links had mostly been shown in single cities or single countries; this pools four countries on three continents and both hemispheres under one harmonized case-crossover protocol, so the signal is no longer an artifact of one health system or one climate.
  • Shifts the exposure metric from "hot day" to "sustained heat". Defining exposure as ≥4 consecutive days above the local 97.5th percentile — and testing alternative duration/percentile definitions — makes persistence, not peak temperature, the operative hazard, which is exactly what heat-health warning systems trigger on.
  • Names who is at risk and where. Stronger associations among older adults and in low-population-density locations point to a rural/peri-urban vulnerability that urban-centric heat plans routinely miss — a spatial-equity finding with immediate resource-allocation implications.
  • Quantifies the demand side of the health system. The attributable-fraction analysis (Fig. 4) converts relative risks into counts of hospitalizations attributable to heatwaves by cause and country — the currency that psychiatric bed planning, ED staffing and crisis-line surge models actually use.
  • Reproducible pipeline. Analysis code is public at github.com/LiamwLiu/multicountry_heatwave_mentalhealth_hospitalization, and statistical source data for all main and extended-data figures ship with the paper.

Core Methods

  • Time-stratified case-crossover design. Each admission serves as its own control, with referent days drawn from the same day-of-week within the same month and year. This self-matching removes all time-invariant individual confounding (sex, comorbidity, socioeconomic status) and controls season and long-term trend by construction — the workhorse for acute environmental triggers.
  • Conditional logistic estimation per location, then meta-analytic pooling. Models are fitted location by location (n = 852) and combined with random-effects meta-analysis, so between-location heterogeneity is estimated rather than assumed away, and country-specific pools (510/261/15/66) remain interpretable.
  • Relative heatwave definition with a definition grid. Primary exposure: daily mean temperature > location-specific 97.5th percentile for ≥4 consecutive days. Extended Data Figs. 3–7 sweep alternative percentile × duration combinations, so the headline estimate is reported against a full definition sensitivity surface instead of a single arbitrary cut.
  • Lag-response modelling over 0–8 days. Both same-day (lag 0) and cumulative (lag 0–8) risks are reported, capturing delayed presentations and the depletion/harvesting question for psychiatric admissions.
  • "Added risk" decomposition. Models additionally adjusted for daily mean temperature isolate the excess risk attributable to heatwave persistence beyond the effect of temperature level alone — separating the wave from the warmth.
  • Covariate control and sensitivity. Relative humidity enters via moving averages (temperature windows 8–12 days, humidity windows 5–9 days, all varied in Extended Data Fig. 9); precipitation and greenness are added in sensitivity models (Extended Data Fig. 8) and do not overturn the main estimate.
  • Effect-modification analysis. Stratification by sex, age group, country, cause category, and location characteristics (including population density, 426 low vs 426 high) identifies who and where the risk concentrates.
  • Attributable fraction translation. Pooled relative risks are converted into cause- and country-specific attributable fractions (Fig. 4), moving from association to burden.

① Break the location box: intra-urban heat exposure surfaces instead of one percentile per administrative unit

Extending this paperThe design assigns one ERA5-Land-derived temperature series and one 97.5th-percentile threshold to an entire location, and 852 locations span everything from a Brazilian municipality to a Canadian census division. That is a textbook MAUP + exposure-misclassification problem: within a single "location" the urban core and its periphery can differ by several degrees, so genuinely heat-exposed admissions are diluted with unexposed ones and the RR of 1.033 is plausibly a lower bound. A direct extension: replace the flat location exposure with a morphology-aware downscaled heat surface (building density, sky-view factor, imperviousness, canopy) and re-estimate the same case-crossover models on residence-level exposure, then check whether the low-population-density modifier survives or was itself an aggregation artifact — low-density units are exactly the ones where a single grid mean is least representative.

Added value to my researchThis is my spatial-scale agenda made concrete: a scale-sensitivity experiment in which the same health estimand is recomputed across nested spatial supports (grid → neighbourhood → municipality), with MGWR used to show how the heat–admission coefficient itself drifts with bandwidth. It also gives me a defensible template for pairing coarse administrative health records with fine remote-sensing exposure — the recurring bottleneck in every environment–health project I run, and a natural fine-tuning target for geospatial foundation models producing sub-kilometre thermal fields.

② Ask why low-density areas are worse: is it heat, or is it the distance to care?

Extending this paperThe paper's most policy-loaded result — stronger associations in low-population-density locations — is reported as an effect modifier but left mechanistically open. Two rival explanations are entirely confounded in the current design: (a) genuinely higher physiological/behavioural vulnerability in rural populations, versus (b) a health-service artifact, where sparse psychiatric supply means only the most acute cases are ever admitted, inflating the heat-triggered marginal admission. Extending the paper: join each of the 852 locations to a supply-side accessibility index of psychiatric beds, crisis services and community mental-health teams, then re-fit the meta-regression with accessibility as a location-level moderator alongside density. If accessibility absorbs the density effect, the intervention changes from "cool the countryside" to "extend crisis capacity into low-supply catchments during forecast heatwaves".

Added value to my researchThis is my 2SFCA/R2SFCA line entering climate epidemiology as an explanatory variable rather than an outcome. I get a construct I have wanted for years: an accessibility-adjusted attributable fraction, where the heat burden is decomposed into a hazard component and an access-deficit component. It also directly extends my telehealth/digital-divide work — crisis lines and remote psychiatric consultation are the cheapest surge lever precisely in the low-density catchments this paper flags, and their value can now be quantified against a measured burden.

③ From one hazard to a compound-exposure interaction surface, read with explainable ML

Extending this paperHeatwaves rarely arrive alone. The paper adjusts for humidity and tests precipitation and greenness, but it never models compound exposures — heat co-occurring with wildfire smoke, ozone episodes, night-time heat that blocks recovery sleep, or drought — nor does it allow non-additive interaction. A concrete extension keeping the same 852-location panel: define joint exposure states (heatwave × high PM2.5, heatwave × high Tmin, heatwave × low humidity) and estimate interaction on the additive scale (RERI), then fit a location-level gradient-boosted meta-regression with SHAP attribution to learn which combinations of climate, morphology, greenness and demography generate the high-RR tail — turning the paper's flat forest plot of modifiers into a learned vulnerability response surface. The sleep pathway is especially tractable: exposure defined on consecutive hot nights rather than hot days is a falsifiable mechanistic test the current data can already support.

Added value to my researchExactly my explainable-ML-for-spatial-prediction workflow, applied where it is most defensible: ML is used to explain heterogeneity of a causally-identified estimate rather than to make raw predictions from observational data — the methodological posture I want to standardize. It also supplies a clean benchmark case for comparing SHAP-based spatial attribution against MGWR coefficient surfaces on the same dataset, a comparison my methods papers keep needing a real health outcome for.

④ Turn a retrospective RR into a forecast-driven, spatially targeted psychiatric surge warning

Extending this paperEverything here is retrospective and population-averaged: the paper establishes that risk rises over lag 0–8 days but never issues a prediction anyone could act on. The eight-day cumulative window is, however, almost exactly the horizon of operational medium-range weather forecasts — so the natural extension is an operational pipeline: ECMWF/ERA5-consistent forecast temperature → location-specific percentile threshold → predicted excess mental-health admissions with uncertainty → alert to the specific hospital catchments and age groups the paper identifies. Two upgrades make it research rather than engineering: (i) treat the 852 locations as a graph and learn a spatio-temporal GNN that borrows strength across climatically similar locations for the small-n countries (Chile has only 15 locations, so it cannot support a standalone model); (ii) evaluate the alert not by AUC but by decision-curve/net-benefit under realistic bed-capacity constraints.

Added value to my researchThis is my physics-informed graph learning agenda with a real deployment target: climatological structure and lag physics enter as inductive bias, and the graph lets sparse jurisdictions inherit parameters from dense ones — the transfer problem I face in every multi-region health model. Pairing it with agent-based simulation of patient arrivals and bed occupancy converts my methods stack into an end-to-end early-warning demonstrator for a health department, which is precisely the translational artifact my urban-health policy work needs.

⑤ Promote greenness from nuisance covariate to the adaptation lever being evaluated

Extending this paperGreenness appears in this paper only as a sensitivity covariate (Extended Data Fig. 8), dismissed on the grounds that it changes slowly and cannot confound within-month comparisons. That reasoning is correct for confounding and beside the point for effect modification: slowly-varying greenness is precisely the kind of variable that can attenuate an acute heat effect. Extending the paper: stratify the 852 locations by canopy cover, park proximity and park accessibility (not just quantity), and estimate whether the lag-0 RR is measurably lower where residents are within a short walk of a small park — the same design that has already shown park-mediated attenuation for heat-related mortality. Layering the known greenness–mental-health dose–response on top yields a two-channel model: greenspace both cools the exposure and buffers the psychological response, and the 20-year, four-country panel is well powered to separate them.

Added value to my researchThis closes the loop between my healthy-cities/15-minute-neighbourhood work and a hard clinical endpoint: park accessibility measured with my own service-area methods becomes an evaluated intervention with an effect size in avoided admissions, not an amenity score. It gives greening-investment prioritization a health-outcome objective function — directly reusable in my efficiency-versus-equity facility-siting optimization, where "where to plant" can now be posed with the same machinery as "where to charge" or "where to clinic".
