Labour productivity loss and economic burden from PM2.5-related mortality in multiple countries

Bo Wen · Zanfina Ademi · Yao Wu · Rongbin Xu · Pei Yu · Yanming Liu · Wenhua Yu · Tingting Ye · Wenzhong Huang · Zhengyu Yang · Yiwen Zhang · Yuxi Zhang · Ke Ju · Simon Hales · Eric Lavigne · Paulo H. Core Conclusion

Short-term PM2.5 exposure destroys working life, not just life. Across 1,066 locations in seven countries (2000–2019), every 10 µg m−3 rise in PM2.5 was associated with a 2.70% (95% CI 2.15–3.26%) increase in productivity-adjusted life-years (PALYs) lost to premature death. Overall 5.0% (95% CI 4.0–5.9%) of PALY losses were attributable to PM2.5, an annual cost of US$21.5 billion; meeting the WHO air quality guideline would avoid 3.5% of PALY losses and US$16.7 billion per year. Burdens fall hardest on women, adults aged 15–44, and lower-middle-income areas — making clean air an economic-productivity argument, not only a health one.

★

Why It Matters

  • Changes the currency of the air-pollution debate. Most PM2.5 burden studies stop at deaths or DALYs. Converting mortality into PALYs — life-years weighted by workforce participation and productivity — puts the damage into the unit finance ministries and employers actually optimize.
  • Attaches a defensible price tag to a compliance target. The counterfactual "below the WHO AQG" scenario yields US$16.7 billion in avoidable annual cost, converting an air-quality guideline into a budgetary benefit line rather than an aspiration.
  • Relocates the burden onto the young and the working poor. Elevated risks in 15–44-year-olds and in lower-middle-income areas invert the usual "air pollution mostly kills the elderly" framing — each such death removes decades of productive life, so equity and economics point the same way.
  • A gender finding with policy teeth. Higher PALY risk among women interacts with sex-specific labour-force participation, meaning the productivity loss is measured against an already-unequal baseline — an explicitly gendered environmental-health inequality.
  • Seven-country evidence, one estimator. A harmonized multi-country design (2000–2019) makes the exposure–response comparable across very different income and air-quality regimes, which is what transferable policy advice requires.

Core Methods

  • Two-stage multi-location time-series design. Stage 1: location-specific regression of daily PALY loss on daily PM2.5, adjusting for time trend, day of week, temperature and humidity. Stage 2: multivariate meta-analysis pooling location-specific coefficients into country and overall estimates — the standard MCC-network architecture, consistent with the paper's reference list.
  • Distributed lag modelling. Fig. 2 reports both an exposure–response and a lag–response surface; the headline effect uses the 2-day moving average (lag 0–1) of PM2.5. The dlnm package (Gasparrini 2011) is cited, indicating distributed lag linear/non-linear models.
  • PALY construction. Each premature death is converted to remaining life-years, then weighted by age–sex-specific workforce participation (ILO) and a productivity index — the metric proposed by Ademi et al. (2021) and previously applied by this group to non-optimum temperatures in Brazil.
  • Attributable-burden accounting. The pooled exposure–response is converted into an attributable fraction of PALYs lost (5.0%), then into an annual monetary burden (US$21.5 bn) via GDP-based valuation.
  • Counterfactual policy scenario. Recomputation of PALY loss under a hypothetical PM2.5 distribution capped at the WHO air quality guideline, yielding 3.5% avoidable PALY loss and US$16.7 bn avoidable annual cost.
  • Effect modification / stratified analysis. Pre-specified subgroups — sex, age band (15–44 vs older working ages), area income level — with pooled subgroup risk ratios (Fig. 3).
  • Exposure validation. Modelled daily PM2.5 benchmarked against ground monitoring on 513,415 paired daily observations with R² and RMSE reported (Extended Data Fig. 1) — an explicit measurement-error check, backed by references on exposure measurement error in time-series studies.
  • Caveat: the full Methods section is behind the paywall; items above are reconstructed from the abstract, figure captions, extended data and the cited methodological references, and are flagged as such.

① Downscale the burden: from 1,066 administrative locations to a continuous PALY surface

Extending this paperThe paper's spatial unit is the administrative location — a city or district gets one daily PM2.5 value and one PALY loss. That aggregation hides exactly the within-city gradients where the burden concentrates, and it makes the "lower-middle-income areas" finding a between-area statement rather than a neighbourhood one. A direct extension: couple the paper's pooled exposure–response with a gapless 1-km daily PM2.5 field and gridded population/labour-participation rasters to render a continuous, sub-city PALY surface, then re-derive the US$21.5 bn burden as a spatially explicit map. Because the exposure–response is already estimated and pooled, the downscaling step is an ecological-inference problem, not a new epidemiological one — and the paper's own source data for Fig. 3 gives the subgroup coefficients needed to weight each grid cell.

Added value to my researchThis is my spatiotemporal kernel density + explainable spatial prediction stack applied to a monetized outcome: instead of predicting concentration, predict dollars of lost productive life per hectare per day. That single reframing gives me a health-geography dependent variable that is comparable across countries, directly stackable with my accessibility layers, and immediately legible to planners — an ideal target for MGWR/GWR to test whether the PM2.5→PALY coefficient itself is spatially non-stationary within a metropolitan area.

② Fix the exposure misalignment: workers do not breathe where they are registered

Extending this paperThere is a structural irony in this paper: it measures the loss of working life-years using an exposure metric assigned at the place of death registration — typically residence. The 15–44 age group carrying the highest risk is precisely the group that commutes, works outdoors, and spends the majority of its high-ventilation hours away from home. Extending the paper: rebuild the exposure term as a mobility-weighted, activity-space dose using mobile-phone or GPS trajectory data, weighting hourly PM2.5 by time-activity and by occupational ventilation rate. The testable prediction is that the 2.70% per 10 µg m−3 coefficient is attenuated by exposure measurement error — a bias the paper explicitly acknowledges by citing the measurement-error literature but cannot correct with residential assignment.

Added value to my researchThis is the cleanest possible use case for my multi-source data fusion line (mobile-phone records + POIs + remote sensing). It also converts activity-space exposure from a methodological curiosity into an economically valued quantity: the difference between residential and mobility-weighted dose becomes a dollar-denominated measurement-error correction, which is a far stronger argument for trajectory data than "our estimate is more accurate."

Cross-ref (corpus): Big mobility data reveals hyperlocal air pollution exposure disparities in the Bronx, New York — 10.1038/s44284-024-00093-x

③ Compound exposures: PALY loss under joint heat × PM2.5 stress

Extending this paperTemperature enters this analysis as a confounder to be adjusted away. But heat and PM2.5 co-occur (stagnation, wildfire smoke) and both attack the working-age population through the same cardiorespiratory pathway — so the marginal PALY loss on a hot, smoky day is plausibly super-additive, and the paper's additive adjustment would systematically under-price the worst days. Extending it: re-estimate with an explicit bivariate exposure–response surface (PM2.5 × temperature, distributed over lags) and report the interaction as an "extreme-compound-day" PALY premium. Under climate projections, that premium — not the mean effect — is what determines whether the US$21.5 bn figure is a floor or a ceiling for 2050.

Added value to my researchCompound-hazard surfaces are where my explainable ML agenda earns its keep: an ensemble with SHAP-style interaction attribution can recover the non-linear joint surface without pre-specifying its functional form, then be constrained back to the epidemiological model for interpretability. It also gives my healthy-cities work a concrete climate-adaptation deliverable — a map of neighbourhoods where compound exposure and low labour-market resilience coincide.

Cross-ref (corpus): Compound effects of extreme heat and wildfire-PM2.5 air pollution on child hospitalizations in multiple countries — 10.1038/s44360-026-00133-x

④ Turn pre-specified subgroups into a learned map of vulnerability

Extending this paperFig. 3 stratifies by sex, age band and area income level — three variables chosen in advance. That design can confirm hypothesized inequities but cannot discover unanticipated ones, and it treats "lower-middle-income area" as a discrete label rather than a position on a continuum of deprivation, occupational structure and healthcare access. Extending the paper: replace the fixed stratification with a causal-forest / effect-modification-learning stage over location-level covariates (industrial mix, informal-employment share, greenness, healthcare accessibility, housing quality), yielding a continuous vulnerability score for each of the 1,066 locations rather than three binary cuts. This directly tests whether the sex effect survives adjustment for occupational segregation — a mechanism the paper reports but cannot adjudicate.

Added value to my researchHealthcare accessibility is the covariate I can supply that the epidemiology literature usually cannot: a 2SFCA/R2SFCA primary-care and emergency-care accessibility layer per location, tested as an effect modifier of the PM2.5→PALY coefficient. If access moderates the mortality response, then facility siting becomes an air-pollution intervention — a result that unifies my health-geography and environmental-health lines into one policy argument.

Cross-ref (corpus): Racial/ethnic disparities in PM2.5-attributable cardiovascular mortality burden in the United States — 10.1038/s41562-023-01694-7

⑤ Close the valuation gap: from mortality-only PALYs to a full spatial cost-benefit engine

Extending this paperThe paper's US$21.5 bn is explicitly a mortality-only figure — PALYs lost to premature death. It omits morbidity, absenteeism and presenteeism among survivors, which in labour economics typically dominate the productivity channel; it also monetizes with country-level GDP, ignoring the substantial within-country spatial variation in output per worker. Extending it: (i) add a morbidity arm using the hospital-admission data the authors already hold, (ii) replace national GDP with subnational gross value added, and (iii) put the resulting spatial burden surface on the cost side of an intervention ledger — traffic restrictions, industrial siting, EV transition, indoor filtration — so the WHO-AQG counterfactual becomes a ranked, spatially explicit portfolio of abatement measures rather than a single aggregate number.

Added value to my researchThis is the natural bridge from my EV charging-station efficiency-vs-equity optimization to health outcomes: an EV siting plan can be scored not only on utilization and equity but on avoided PALY loss per dollar invested, spatially resolved. More broadly it gives my urban-analytics work a common objective function — monetized health burden — that lets accessibility, mobility and energy interventions be compared on one axis, which is exactly what regional-collaboration and healthy-cities planning has lacked.
